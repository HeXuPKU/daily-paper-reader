# Abstract 20 Genomic prediction of complex traits is limited when phenotype records are restricted and 21 when using linear models. Increasing the amount of phenotypic data with high-throughput, 22 image-based phenotyping could result in better genomic prediction and stronger signals in 23 variant detection. Here, we analysed phenotypic and genomic data from a selectively bred 24 cohort of the Australasian snapper (Chrysophrys auratus ) to identify genetic variants 25 associated with growth traits. We used a high-throughput phenotyping pipeline to extract 13 26 measurements of size from images. Phenotypic correlations among image-derived and 27 manually measured traits (weight, fork length), together with heritabilities, were analysed. All 28 measurements were significantly positively correlated with each other, and heritability ranged 29 from 0.20-0.38. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) identified 28 growth-associated 30 SNPs, while GBLUP was used to predict phenotypes, and XGBoost machine-learning models 31 were used to jointly predict phenotypes and report important variants. Both GBLUP (mean R 232 = 0.50) and XGBoost (mean R 2 = 0.77) performed well on the training data, but performance 33 dropped on testing sets (both = 0.11), which decreased further when accounting for genetic 34 relatedness (both = 0.06). Despite this, approximately 20% of the genetic variance for growth 35 traits was captured by the models, and feature importance from XGBoost reflected signals seen 36 in GWAS. Our findings highlight the utility of integrating computer vision-based phenotyping 37 with GWAS, GBLUP, and ML for trait prediction. Despite detecting shared biological signals 38 as GWAS, genomic prediction faces challenges with population structure and relatedness that 39 are inherent in breeding programmes of mass spawning species, including many aquatic 40 species. 41

# Article summary 42 Incorporating genetic information into selective breeding programmes can accelerate gains but 43 may also miss gene interactions in complex traits. Machine learning approaches, such as 44 decision trees, can capture those relationships and potentially improve genomic predictions. 45 We used high-throughput computer vision phenotyping to uncover biological signal for genetic 46 growth variants involved in the Australasian snapper. Both types of models captured 18-40% 47 of the genetic variation, but the prediction accuracies were hampered by the population 48 structure in this cohort of mass-spawning fish. 49 

# Introduction 50 Selective breeding programmes are fundamental to both plant and animal production systems, 51 where they are used to improve economically important traits such as growth, yield, survival, 52 product quality, reproductive performance, and feed efficiency (Godfray et al. 2010; Boyd et 53 al. 2022). Over recent decades, the increasing availability of genomic resources, including 54 whole-genome assemblies and dense panels of DNA markers, has transformed selective 55 breeding approaches, even in non-model species (Ekblom and Galindo 2011; Ellegren 2014). 56 Genetic improvement has progressively shifted from conventional pedigree-based selection 57 towards marker-assisted and genomic selection, or combinations of both approaches 58 (Sandoval-Castillo et al. 2022). These advances have substantially improved the estimation of 59 breeding values and the identification of superior breeders without the need for additional 60 phenotyping, accelerating genetic gain across a wide range of production systems. This is 61 especially important where mass phenotyping is not practical. 62 Despite these advances in genomics, phenotyping remains a major bottleneck limiting further 63 progress in breeding programmes (Houle 2010). This 66 challenge is particularly pronounced for traits that are labour-intensive, invasive, expensive, or 67 difficult to measure repeatedly across large numbers of individuals. In aquaculture systems, 68 phenotyping challenges are especially severe because of the aquatic environment itself (Fu and 69 Yuna 2022). Fish must often be removed from water, anaesthetised and handled before 70 measurements can be obtained, creating substantial logistical constraints and introducing 71 handling stress that can affect animal welfare and, in some cases, phenotype expression. 72 Importantly, many measurements must be completed within very short time windows while 73 fish remain under anaesthesia, limiting the number and complexity of traits that can realistically 74 be collected manually. This makes the measurement of traits such as morphology, behaviour, 75 stress responses, welfare indicators, and condition particularly difficult at large scales. 76 Consequently, phenotyping has emerged as one of the key constraints limiting the efficiency 77 and scalability of aquaculture breeding programmes. 78 Recent technological advances in machine learning (ML), computer vision, automated 79 imaging, and deep learning networks provide new opportunities to overcome these limitations 80 (Yang et al. 2021). Image-based computer vision approaches have transformed phenotyping in 81 aquaculture by enabling rapid, non-invasive, and high-throughput trait extraction from images 82 and videos (Tuckey et al. 2022; Babu et al. 2023). These approaches substantially reduce 83 handling time, minimise stress on animals, and allow the capture of detailed morphological and 84 behavioural information that would otherwise be difficult or impossible to measure manually. 85 In aquaculture, computer vision systems have increasingly been used to estimate growth, body 86 shape, swimming behaviour, health status, feeding activity, deformities, and biomass in species 87 such as salmon, seabream, tilapia, shrimp, and other cultured fish species. # Materials and Methods 139 

Fish rearing 140 Snapper used in this study came from the long-term selective breeding programme at The New 141 Zealand Bioeconomy Science Institute Ltd. (BSI), Nelson, New Zealand. Genetic and growth 142 data were obtained from the F 4 generation, produced in November 2021 from the third 143 selectively bred generation. Fertilized eggs were collected and incubated for 5 days. Larval 144 rearing protocols followed standard methods developed for this species (Samuels et al. 2024). 145 Fish were then reared at BSI’s land-based fish facility. The facility uses a flow-through tank 146 system, where water from the Nelson Haven is withdrawn from the intertidal zone via an 147 engineered aquifer filled with various hard substrates that provide filtration. All work was done 148 under the conditions of animal ethics application AEC-2021-PFR-05, approved by the Animal 149 Ethics Committee at the Nelson Marlborough Institute of Technology (Te Pūkenga), along with 150 fish-farm licence FW208.03. 151 

Manual and image-based phenotyping 152 Prior to any measurements, snapper were anaesthetised with AQUI-S® (15–20 ppm), sufficient 153 to induce loss of equilibrium and absence of response to handling. At three months of age, fish 154 were weighed (FX-300i WP, A&D Company Ltd.), photographed and measured manually. 155 Additional phenotypic measurements (Fig. 1A) were obtained from the photographs using an 156 computer vision pipeline that extracts traits such as length, and specific body area 157 measurements, directly from the image. Measurements from this pipeline are available in 158 Supplementary Table 1. In total, 1011 individual fish were phenotyped by imaging. The first 159 principal component (PC) derived from measured traits (using prcomp from the stats library in 160 R) were included in downstream analyses. Trait correlations were assessed using the R package 161 corrplot v0.92. 162

Genetic data collection 163 SNP data for these fish were collected using a multi-species SNP chip (Montanari et al. 2023). 164 This SNP chip included 18,489 snapper SNPs, but after filtering for quality and levels of 165 polymorphism 11,164 SNPs were retained for the 1,011 fish included in this work. SNP 166 positions were remapped from the original snapper reference genome to the updated snapper 167 genome assembly (Blommaert et al. 2024) using snplift v1.0.4 (Normandeau et al. 2023). SNPs 168 that could not be lifted over were further processed by extracting a genomic region of 70 bp 169 surrounding each SNP from the original assembly and performing a BLAST search against the 170 new genome assembly. SNPs with multiple mapping locations in the updated genome were 171 excluded, as were SNPs that did not align to the expected chromosome. 172 

Genetic relatedness and heritability 173 Genetic relatedness among individuals was estimated from SNP genotype data to visualise 174 population structure within the F 4 fish and to stratify the data set for genomic prediction 175 analyses. Pairwise relatedness coefficients were calculated in R by centering the genotype 176 matrix (subtracting the column-wise mean genotype value for each SNP) and computing the 177 genomic relationship matrix (GRM) as the dot product of the centred matrix and its transpose, 178 divided by the total number of SNPs. We grouped genetically similar individuals using k-means 179 clustering (k = 19) applied to the GRM. The resulting cluster assignments were visualised by 180 principal component analysis (PCA) to confirm consistency between the k-means groupings 181 and the genetic structure apparent in the PCA. 182 We estimated narrow-sense heritability for each trait using ASREML-R v4.2 (Bulter et al. 183 2023), fitting mixed-effects models with fish id as a random effect. Variance components were 184 estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) and heritability was calculated as the 185

Variants were excluded 193 if the MAF was below 0.05. Additionally, a more conservative MAF filter of 0.1 was also 194 applied. Identifying growth-related variants using GWAS 202 We conducted genome-wide association studied (GWAS) using rMVP v1.1.1 (Yin et al. 2021), 203 applying three standard models: general linear model (GLM), mixed linear model (MLM, and 204 Fixed and Random model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU). For each trait, we 205 modelled the phenotype as a function of the SNP being tested, with the first two genomic 206 principal components included as covariates to account for population structure. Cross validation of genomic prediction using GBLUP 222 We used GBLUP to predict weight, length, PC1 of the phenotypes, eye width, and height at 223 75% of the fish’s length from genome-wide SNP data using ASREML v4.2. We chose these 224 traits to represent the range of heritabilities in our dataset and to investigate if there was any 225 increased signal in composite traits (e.g. PC1) compared to individual measurements. The 226 previously described GRM was used, with a small value added to the diagonal prior to 227 computing its inverse to ensure numerical stability. We evaluated two prediction strategies to 228 assess the impact of relatedness between training and testing individuals. 229 In both approaches, we portioned individuals into training and testing sets and individuals in 230 the testing set were masked by setting their measured phenotype values to missing. In the first 231

Nested cross-validation for genomic prediction using machine learning 243 Genomic prediction analyses for weight, length, PC1 of the phenotypes, eye width, and height 244 at 75% of the fish’s length were performed using the XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin 2016) 245 algorithm in the R package xgboost v1.7.8.1. Models were tuned using tidymodels v1.2.0 246 (Kuhn and Wickham 2020) with 5-fold internal cross-validation. We evaluated performance 247 using root mean square error (rmse) between predicted values and real measurements as a 248 metric. To minimise potential data leakage due to genetic relatedness, we partitioned the dataset 249 into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets 1) randomly, and 2) based on the relatedness 250 clusters identified above, as was done for GBLUP. As above, these CV splits were performed 251 50 times, using the same seeds as for the GBLUP cross validation. 252 Model preprocessing was defined using a recipes workflow. We included all SNP genotypes 253 as predictors, while individual identifiers were retained only as sample identifiers and excluded 254 from model fitting. Predictive performance was evaluated by generating predictions for both the training and 276 testing sets. Model accuracy was assessed using RMSE and the coefficient of determination 277 (R²). We retained importance matrices from the final XGBoost models for downstream 278 analyses. 279 We also extracted the feature importance matrix from each of the 50 cross validation replicates 280 and produced a summary table including mean gain, mean cover, and a count table for how 281 often each SNP occurred in an importance matrix across the 50 cross-validation runs for either 282 the cluster based split or the individual based splits. To evaluate the feature selection reliability, 283 SNPs were grouped by the number of CVs they were selected in (out of 50). SNPs that were 284 selected in 35 CVs or more were classed as persistent markers, and those that were selected in 285 fewer than 35 CVs were designated as background markers. We used mean gain across the 50 286 CVs as the primary measure of feature importance. A non-parametric bootstrapping approach 287 in R was taken to compare the mean gain between the persistent and background markers. For 288 each stratification approach and SNP stability category, the observed pool mean of SNP mean 289 gain values was resampled over 1000 independent bootstrap iterations. The bootstrap sample 290 mean was calculated for each iteration and unified 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were 291 extracted. To test the directional hypothesis that persistent markers exhibit significantly higher 292 feature importance than transient background noise, a one-tailed Mann-Whitney U (Wilcoxon 293 rank-sum) test was applied to the empirical distributions. 294

# Discussion 382 This study demonstrates how high-throughput phenotyping, genomic prediction, genome-wide 383 association analyses, and machine-learning approaches can be integrated to support selective 384 breeding programmes. Using snapper as a case study, an emerging species for aquaculture, we 385 show how image-based phenotyping can generate scalable measurements of growth and 386 morphology, which can then be linked with genomic information to estimate breeding values, 387 identify trait-associated variants, and explore alternative prediction frameworks. One of our 388 findings is that image-based phenotyping not only increased the number of measurements 389 collected from individuals but also resulted in the discovery of more significant SNPs via 390 GWAS. This was not only because more measurements and traits could be analysed via GWAS, 391 but derivatives of those allometric and complex traits could also be more fully explored and 392 subsequently included if needed. We were also able to predict weight using both GBLUP and 393 XGBoost, capturing between 18% and 39% of total heritable variance (h 2 = 0.38). While both 394 models showed high training accuracy, the observed overfitting, especially in the relatedness 395 aware based data stratification approach, likely reflects the reproductive skew observed in this 396 species (Ashton et al. 2019), and the estimated low effective population size of this cohort. 397 Despite these challenges within this dataset, XGBoost identified similar biological signals to 398 traditional linear methods, highlighting shared genomic hotspots for growth. 399 Computer vision methods are becoming increasingly common in biology for tasks including 400 species identification, population census counts, and phenotyping (Yang et al. 2021; Fu and 401 Yuna 2022; Tuckey et al. 2022; Babu et al. 2023). Here, we were able to increase the number 402 of measurements taken from each individual while decreasing the amount of labour involved 403 and stress on the fish (due to a reduced time out of the water). Image-based, computer vision 404 phenotyping could also be extended to underwater photographs or videos in the future, further 405

Data leakage was indeed more apparent in 437 the random split approach to data stratification because more of the siblings and half-siblings 438 of the training set fish were in the testing set. The disruption of this leakage in the 440 relatedness aware data stratification did lead to a reduction in over-fitting, but also in the 441 prediction accuracy. This is lower than that 443 captured in another study comparing genomic prediction in other aquaculture species, but our 444 results from the random split are more similar, and data leakage control due to relatedness does 445 not seem to have been considered (Kriaridou et al. 2020). Conclusions 490 This study has demonstrated how high-throughput, image-based phenotyping can be paired 491 with genomic prediction methods to understand genomic architecture of complex traits and 492 guide breeding decisions in an emerging aquaculture species, the Australasian snapper. The 493 implementation of computer vision phenotyping expanded the data volume and increased the 494 number of SNPs discovered by GWAS, but genomic prediction was hampered by data leakage 495 and population structure stemming from reproductive bias and the design of the breeding 496 program. While the non-linear prediction method (XGBoost) did not outperform the linear 497 approach of GBLUP in this instance, XGBoost did identify genomic signals overlapping to 498 those identified using the linear GWAS approach, and resulted in a larger list of potentially 499 important variants. This type of finding should be validated in species with broader functional 500 genomic resources. Ultimately, our findings highlight the utility of machine learning in 501 phenotyping and genomic prediction, even if prediction accuracy faces the same hurdles as 502 those faced by GBLUP. 503
