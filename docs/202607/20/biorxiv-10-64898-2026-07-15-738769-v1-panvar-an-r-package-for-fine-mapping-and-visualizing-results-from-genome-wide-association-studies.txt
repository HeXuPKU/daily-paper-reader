Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) use statistical models to correlate single nucleotide 18 

polymorphisms (SNPs) to a phenotype of interest. This scan of the entire genome identifies 19 

regions of association with a phenotype, but due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), GWAS on their 20 

own cannot identify single genes responsible for phenotypic variation. Rather, fine-mapping of 21 

GWAS regions is required, necessitating the use of additional tools and software. With the 22 

introduction of more pangenomic resources in a number of crops (Guo et al. 2025; Hufford et al. 23 

2021), the fidelity of these fine-mapping efforts is growing, presenting the opportunity to 24 

leverage new information about allelic variation towards gene discovery (Shi et al. 2023; Della 25 

Coletta et al. 2021). Panvar is a tool developed to integrate existing software and resources to 26 

perform GWAS and fine-mapping in one seamless step. For each identified GWAS peak, panvaR 27 

outputs information about LD and SNP effect prediction for each SNP and by layering locations 28 

of nearby genes, creates a refined list of possible candidate genes. We have implemented Panvar 29 

as an R package, “panvaR”, which runs the analysis functions, creates interactive and static 30 

visualizations, and outputs results tables. This tool seeks to bridge the gap between GWAS and 31 

gene speeding up an important step of quantitative genetic studies. 32 

33 

Introduction 34 

35 

Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) have proved powerful for identifying regions 36 

associated with a wide range of quantitative traits in plants (Tibbs Cortes, Zhang, and Yu 2021) 37

assisted selection in plant breeding (Collard and Mackill 2008). However, as the adoption of 39 

gene editing technology grows, the identification of specific genes is becoming increasingly 40 

important for crop improvement (Voytas and Gao 2014). Additionally, with genomic sequencing 41 

becoming ubiquitous, researchers are increasingly able to leverage insights from synteny and 42 

evolutionary conservation to translate functional gene assignments across species (Whitt et al. 43 

2023). 44 

45 

GWAS rapidly screen an entire genome for regions that associate with a phenotype of interest, 46 

but because of linkage disequilibrium (LD), neighboring single nucleotide polymorphisms 47 

(SNPs) are correlated, leading to Manhattan plots’ characteristic “peaks”. Depending on the 48 

species and the size of LD blocks, a peak may contain up to hundreds of genes. Consequently, 49 

fine-mapping to prioritize the most likely causal gene for the putative association is essential to 50 

identify a causal gene underlying a GWAS peak. 51 

52 

Recent advances in sequencing have led to the creation of many pan-genome datasets, where all 53 

lines are fully sequenced and the near-complete inventory of polymorphisms can be known and 54 

evaluated for phenotypic consequences (Morris et al. 2026) (Hufford et al. 2021) (Guo et al. 55 

2025) (Kang et al. 2023). However, pan-genome datasets usually include thousands of 56 

polymorphisms within each GWAS peak and are difficult to sort through (Tao et al. 2021). 57 

Integrating the different types of data that are needed for this fine mapping step using dense 58

61 

The tool described here, “Panvar”, seeks to overcome this data integration hurdle, speeding up 62 

fine-mapping and paring down the list of possible candidate genes underlying a given GWAS 63 

peak.Within the Panvar workflow, multiple data types are layered, and quantitative and 64 

qualitative filtering steps are applied to extend GWAS results. The flexibility of the package is 65 

intended to allow quick integration and visualization of arbitrary, user-defined metrics of SNP 66 

and gene importance. Exploratory analysis and subsequent verification have demonstrated 67 

Panvar’s capacity to capture expression-level signals, which can help refine GWAS results to 68 

identify potential candidate genes. Our test cases show that Panvar can produce successful 69 

outcomes across different species and phenotypes. 70

Materials & Methods 72 

Workflow 73 

PanvaR works on a single locus at a time and integrates data around this position to display 74 

information at the SNP and gene levels. Required inputs for the program are: 1) a SNP file in vcf 75 

or plink format, 2) a gene annotation file that gives locations and annotations of genes, and 3) a 76 

specific locus of interest, usually a SNP. The workflow then proceeds by first formatting inputs 77 

and optionally re-running GWAS, then generating tables at the SNP and gene level around the 78 

QTL and finally, plotting these results. The program relies on two main dependencies: plink2 79

Formatting inputs 82 

The first step of the program is to generate standardized inputs for downstream functions 83 

(make_panvar_inputs() ). This uses plink2 to apply optional quality control filters to the genotype 84 

file. Users can select a minor allele frequency and missing rate threshold. This also standardizes 85 

column names and input files for GWAS using an rMVP input standardization function. 86 

Running GWAS 87 

While panvaR is meant to be run after GWAS, there are still many common scenarios where it 88 

may be desirable to re-run GWAS. This is explored further in the discussion. rMVP is used as the 89 

GWAS implementation and generalized linear model (GLM) and mixed-linear model (MLM) 90 

outputs are supported ( panvar_mvp_gwas() ). 91 

The tag SNP 92 

An important step of fine-mapping is determining regions of interest or QTL. Typically this is 93 

done by grouping peaks by a combination of p-value, LD and distance to create groups of SNPs 94 

that represent discrete loci displaying association with the phenotype ( snp_make_clumps() ). A 95 

function is presented to help the user group their output into loci which can be analyzed by 96 

panvaR one by one. A single representative SNP, referred to in the package as the “tag snp”, 97 

centers the locus and is usually the top p-value SNP in an identified locus. 98 

Generating tables 99

PanvaR allows the integration of different types of data at this point, both qualitative and 100 

quantitative and at the SNP and gene level. Measures of impactfulness at these two levels can be 101 

added to the GWAS results and annotation table to be carried through the analysis. A major 102 

functionality of the package at this step is to aggregate snp-level measures of impact or 103 

importance to the gene level. The program will collapse SNPs within a gene start and end, and 104 

plus or minus an optional buffer of physical distance, into a single value per gene ( supplemental 105 

figure 1 ). This gives a more compact summary of the maximum impactful measure for a given 106 

gene ( make_panvar_tables() ). 107 

SNP scores 108 

At the table generation step, panvaR can also generate scores at the SNP level which can then be 109 

aggregated to a gene level to summarize an arbitrary number of quantitative impactfulness 110 

measurements. To generate the score, metrics are first oriented in the proper direction. The final 111 

score will be interpreted so that a larger value indicates a more impactful SNP. Metrics where 112 

smaller is better (for example p-value) need to be reversed. Next, a min-max normalization is 113 

applied to scale metrics to (0,1). Then the mean is taken to generate the final result. An option is 114 

supplied to weight this mean by each metric if a user wants to prioritize certain measurements. 115 

By default, if a user requests scores to be computed, the program uses p-value, distance to the tag 116 

SNP, and LD. 117

the tables generated in the previous step ( plot_panvar() ). This flips the traditional manhattan plot 120 

90 degrees and arrays genes located in this physical range alongside. Colors of points in the 121

Shiny 127 

A shiny implementation of the pipeline is presented for users that prefer a more interactive 128 

experience. Users must go through pre-processing steps up to the generation of the output tables 129 

before using the app including formatting GWAS results or optionally re-running GWAS ( Figure 130 

2). The shiny app will create tables and plot the results ( panvar_shiny() ) ( Figure 3 ). 131

Results 133 

To validate the ability of panvaR to identify previously published genes we present two case 134 

studies. 135 

136 

The first case study involved the SvLes1 gene (Sevir.5G085400) identified by Mamidi et al. 137 

(2020) (Mamidi et al. 2020). We ran a GWAS and identified the most significant p-value SNP at 138 

6,857,045 base pairs on chromosome 5, which corresponded with the large GWAS peak shown 139 

in the paper ( Figure 4A ). We ran panvaR with a 75 kilobase window up and downstream of this 140 

SNP ( Figure 4B ). After generating SNP scores using the default distance, p-value and LD to 141 

rank SNPs and aggregating SNPs to the gene level, the SvLes1 gene was ranked second of 19 142 

genes in the window. Mamidi et al. took a similar approach in that they identified protein-143

Discussion 169 

170 

As our two case studies indicate, panvaR speeds up fine-mapping a GWAS peak, producing an 171 

easily reviewable list for the user and high-quality visualizations. By synthesizing popular tools, 172 

panvaR is able to streamline the display of common fine-mapping data streams like gene 173 

locations and linkage disequilibrium information. With its flexible nature in accepting inputs 174 

from any species and its extendability in accepting arbitrary measures of SNP importance, 175 

panvaR serves as a platform for easily navigating the process of linking GWAS to causal genes. 176 

177 

Ease of use is a prime motivation for panvaR’s design but its framework also has other benefits. 178 

Multi-locus models have become increasingly popular in plant GWAS as they effectively control 179 

false positives while simultaneously limiting false negatives (Liu et al. 2016). This balance has 180 

proven an effective tool for finding strong trait associations (Ward et al. 2019), but information 181 

about the significance of phenotypic association is collapsed into a single SNP in subsequent 182 

fine-mapping. By running a more permissive, single-locus model, panvaR fills in the information 183 

that was collapsed during the model-fitting process, giving more insight into the structure of a 184 

GWAS peak than using only p-values from the multi-locus model. Another context in which this 185 

filling in of p-values might be useful is if the GWAS was run on a reduced SNP set to conserve 186

computational resources. PanvaR makes it easier to move from a reduced SNP set to focus on all 187 

of the pan genomic data for a single locus. 188 

189 

PanvaR is intended as a data integration tool and its gene prioritization system relies on a 190 

weighted average of user-defined factors. As such, the list output by the tool should still be 191 

inspected manually for best results. Even so, this coarse approximation performs well in our two 192 

case studies, where we successfully identified two known genes within a small number of 193 

previously top-ranked genes. PanvaR is intentionally designed to be flexible to whichever tools 194 

the user wants to incorporate into the scoring system meaning it can easily accommodate novel 195 

measures of both SNP and gene importance. With the speed that DNA language models are 196 

evolving, it is likely that more performant importance metrics will be released over the coming 197 

years (Zhai et al. 2025). 198 

199 

PanvaR is designed to visualize linkage disequilibrium of one or a small number of tag SNPs to 200 

all other SNPs in a region. However, more complicated LD structures can exist within a GWAS-201 

identified region which are not displayed using this approach. Visualizing a full pair-wise LD 202 

matrix is often useful as it can give insight into more complex inheritance patterns present in the 203 

region of interest (Larsson, Lipka, and Buckler 2013). Furthermore, this focus on a single lead 204 

SNP can lead to spurious identification of causal genes caused by the phenomenon of “synthetic 205 

association” (Platt, Vilhjálmsson, and Nordborg 2010). This can occur either when a rare causal 206 

variant is linked to a more common variant or there are multiple genes in the region controlling 207 

the trait of interest (Dickson et al. 2010) (Sasaki et al. 2021). These more complex forms of 208

linkage are not detectable within the panvaR framework. That being said, a future direction of 209 

the tool will look to include visualizations of the full LD structure in the region and more 210 

comprehensive haplotype analysis which should give more insight to the user in these instances. 211 

212 

As a data integration tool, Panvar does not systematically combine data in a statistical way as 213 

Bayesian (Chen et al. 2016) and frequentist (Cho et al. 2009) statistical fine-mapping tools do. 214 

These tools have been used extensively in human genetics reports of their efficacy in plants has 215 

not been comprehensively validated (Wu et al. 2022). Some hurdles to the use of these statistical 216 

fine mapping approaches include computation time (Chen et al. 2016), inconsistency of results 217 

between different models and a tendency towards false negatives depending on the model 218 

(Schaid, Chen, and Larson 2018). Despite these hurdles, these statistical fine-mapping could be 219 

integrated into the framework of Panvar as additional decision making criteria in causal gene 220 

identification as they become more developed for use in plants. 221 

222 

Conclusions 223 

PanvaR synthesizes data types commonly used in fine-mapping GWAS peaks to speed up this 224 

step in the gene discovery pipeline. Information afforded by pangenomic resources can easily be 225 

mined around previously identified peaks, further adding to panvaR’s utility. Going forward, 226 

panvaR should be a worthy addition to the quantitative genetic researchers’ toolbox, enabling 227 

novel insights into genes and their functions. 228
