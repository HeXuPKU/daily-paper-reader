ABSTRACT 16 

While whole -genome sequencing captures millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) 17 

and hyperspectral imaging (HSI) enables non -destructive plant phenotyp ing, integrating these 18 

modalities to link genotype to phenotype remains challenging due to their high dimensionality 19 

and non -linearity. This study presents DeepPheno a deep learning framework that predicts SNP 20 

genotypes from HSI data, using model predictab ility as a proxy for genotype -phenotype 21 

association. HSI data were acquired from 194 lettuce genotypes under field conditions. HSI 22 

data patches (20×20 pixels × 224 spectral bands) were used to train a hybrid CNN to predict 23 

the variant of a specific SNP. Th e framework was validated on SNPs with known phenotypic 24 

effects (anthocyanin, leaf serration, pale pigmentation), achieving high predictive performance 25 

(AUC ranging from 0.806 to 0.935 ), whereas models trained on randomly shuffled labels 26 

performed at  chance (mean AUC ≈ 0.51). Extending the workflow to 50 randomly selected 27 

putatively neutral SNPs, most yielded low predictability, but two showed high performance 28 

(AUC > 0.76), suggesting uncharacterized genotype -phenotype links. Explainable AI, 29 

including  SHAP and Grad -CAM, identified relevant spectral and spatial features driving these 30 

predictions, particularly the green and red -edge wavelengths associated with pigment dynamics 31 

and leaf structure. These results establish a framework for understanding compl ex 32 

genotype -phenotype interactions in plants and extracting these links from HSI data without 33 

predefining the exact trait values. It provides an avenue for high -throughput trait discovery and 34 

description and extends the integration of image -based phenomics  with plant genetics. 35

1. INTRODUCTION 40 

Advances in crop productivity, resilience, and nutritional quality are essential to meet the 41 

projected doubling of global food demand by 2050 [1] . Achieving this goal requires 42 

accelerating plant breeding, which fundamentally depends on understanding the relationship 43 

between genetic variation and phenotypic traits that can be targeted for crop improvement. 44 

Recent advances in next -generation sequencing technologies have enabled the identification of 45 

millions of single -nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), providing a scalable resource for 46 

studying genetic variation in plants [2] While many SNPs are phenotypically neutral, functional 47 

variants can influence important traits such as anthocyanin biosynthesis [2] and leaf serra tion [3] 48 

in lettuce. However, translating genomic information into robust phenotypic understanding 49 

remains a major challenge in plant phenomics, largely due to limitations in phenotyping 50 

approaches [4] . Traditional phenotyping methods are often destructive, labor -intensive, and 51 

time -consuming, there by restricting their application across large breeding populations [5] .52 

To address these limitations, advanced high -throughput phenotyping methods are required. 53 

Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) has emerged as a powerful alternative, enabling non -destructive 54 

an d high -throughput measurement of plant biochemical and physiological properties. By 55 

integrating imaging and spectroscopy, HSI captures contiguous narrow -band spectral 56 

information across plant growth stages, allowing detailed characterization of plant 57 

physi ology [6] . In addition, HSI -derived spectral indices have been shown to associate with 58 

trait -related genetic variation [7] , and the technology enables early detection of stress responses 59 

prior to the appearance of visible symptoms [8] . Despite HSI data providing rich phenotypic 60 

information , mapping these traits, beyond the individual wavelengths, to underlying genetic 61 

variation is a key challenge. Genome -wide association studies (GWAS) have been widely used 62 

to identify statistical associations between SNPs and phenotypic traits [9] . The integration of 63 

HSI with genetic mapping approaches including GWAS, has successfully uncovered the 64 

genetic architecture of complex traits across various crops. These include mapping temporal 65 

spectral QTLs i n field -grown lettuce [3] , evaluating nitrogen response mechanisms in wheat [10] 66 

and identifying seed vigor loci in peanut [11] . However, these approaches typically rely on 67 

predefined, manually measured traits or use classical RGB image based phenotyping, these may 68 

not fully capture the complex, high -dimensional phenotypic variation embedded in 69 

hy perspectral data. While HSI does not directly measure genetic variation, it does capture the 70 

effect of SNPs with an influence on plant biochemical and physiological processes, and 71 

therefore in turn generate detectable spatial -spectral patterns . Consequentl y, there is a need for 72 

computational approaches capable of modeling nonlinear relationships between high -73 

dimensional hyperspectral signals and underlying genetic variation .74 

Recent studies integrating HSI with machine learning (ML) have demonstrated promis ing 75 

progress in addressing these challenges [12] . Machine learning approaches have been widely 76

Despite these advances, there remains a lack of systematic frameworks that directly link genetic 87 

variants to high -dimensional hyperspectral phenotypes. Most HSI -based approaches focus on 88 

predicting phenotypic traits rather than inferring genotypes at the level of individual SNPs, 89 

thereby limiting the resolution required to dissect the functional role of specific genetic 90 

variants [18] . Similarly, GWAS approaches rely on handcrafted or aggregated traits as proxy 91 

phenotypes, which may fail to capture the full richness of hyperspectral data. Another major 92 

limitation is the “black -box” nature of many machine learning models, whic h often lack 93 

interpretability and thus provide limited biological insight into how spectral signals relate to 94 

underlying physiological or biochemical processes. 95 

These limitations highlight the need for integrative frameworks that can quantify how genetic 96 

variation is expressed through measurable spatial -spectral phenotypes. Such frameworks would 97 

enable systematic evaluation of whether functional genetic variants produce detectable 98 

signatures in hyperspectral data through their influence on plant biochemistr y and physiology. 99 

Moreover, they would facilitate the identification of specific spectral features associated with 100 

genetic variation, thereby enabling the discovery of novel traits and supporting marker -assisted 101 

breeding strategies. 102 

Building on this need , this study proposes a complementary perspective: instead of predicting 103 

phenotype from genotype, we predict SNP genotypes from HSI phenotypes as a proxy for 104 

genotype –phenotype association . We hypothesize that functional SNPs, those linked to 105 

variation in a pre -defined trait, alter plant biochemical properties, thereby generating consistent 106 

and learnable signatures in hyperspectral data, whereas neutral variants do not produce 107 

detectable patterns ( Figure 1 ). Under this framework, the ability of a model to pre dict the SNP 108 

variant from hyperspectral features provides a direct measure of the biological impact of this 109 

SNP. High predictive performance indicates a meaningful genotype -phenotype association, 110 

while near -random performance suggests limited or no detecta ble HSI phenotypic effect. 111 

To test this hypothesis, we present DeepPheno, an integrative deep learning framework that 112 

combines HSI, genomic data, and explainable AI (XAI). The framework comprises four key 113

genotype labels were randomly shuffled while preserving class balance and sample size. This 238 

procedure disrupts genuine genotype -phenotype associations while mainta ining the HSI data 239 

structure. Label permutation was repeated 300 times to obtain a stable estimate of the null 240

2.3.3 Selection of randomly sampled putatively neutral control SNPs 251 

To establish a ne gative control framework, a set of putatively neutral SNPs with no known or 252 

expected association with the target phenotypes was curated. These SNPs —hereafter referred 253 

to as randomly selected SNPs —serve to determine whether observed genotype –phenotype 254 

assoc iations arise from true biological signals in the HSI data rather than from artifacts, 255 

population structure, or linkage to known functional variants. Additionally, this approach 256 

enables the identification of unexpected predictive signals that could reflect  previously 257 

uncharacterized genotype –phenotype relationships. 258 

SNP selection followed a controlled pipeline ( Supplementary Figure S2 ) designed to match 259 

the quality and minor allele frequency (MAF) characteristics of the known phenotypic SNPs 260 

while ensuring  genetic independence and likely phenotypic neutrality. From the full SNP 261 

matrix, quality filtering was applied to retain variants with call rate ≥95% and MAF ≥3%, 262 

removing rare variants with limited statistical power and maintaining allelic balance. To avo id 263 

linkage with known functional loci, linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was performed by 264 

excluding: (i) SNPs with pairwise correlation |r| > 0.3 with any of the four known phenotypic 265 

SNPs, and (ii) all SNPs within a 1 Mb window on the same chromosome as  any of these four 266 

SNPs. Furthermore, to mitigate confounding effects due to population structure, SNPs strongly 267 

correlated with the first two principal components of genetic variation (variance explained 268 

<5%) were excluded. The remaining SNPs were conside red putatively neutral relative to the 269 

known loci and unlikely to exhibit phenotypic effects linked to causal variants. 270 

From this filtered pool of putatively neutral variants (about 10458 SNPs),  50 SNPs were 271 

randomly sampled  for downstream analysis. The MA F distribution of the selected SNPs was 272 

verified to be comparable to that of the known phenotypic SNPs. Selected SNPs were labeled 273 

using a standardized format (e.g., MAF_0.123_chr1_123456), indicating MAF, chromosome, 274 

and genomic position. 275

2.4 Deep Learni ng for SNP Identification 277 

To predict SNP genotypes from hyperspectral imaging (HSI) data, we developed a hybrid 278 

convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture that extends the 3D -2D CNN framework of 279 

Okyere et al [2] by incorporating an additional spectral feature extraction branch. 280 

2.4.1 Proposed Model Architecture 281 

The baseline 3D -2D CNN model extracts joint spatial -spectral features using 3D convolutions, 282 

followed by reshaping and 2D convolutions to extract higher -le vel spatial representations. 283 

While effective, this approach limits detailed spectral feature extraction due to the 284 

computational expense of the 3D convolutional block, which limits kernel usage. To address 285 

this limitation, the architecture was modified by channeling the feature maps of the 3D block 286 

into two parallel branches: 2D and 1D CNN blocks ( Figure 4 ). The spatial branch receives the 287 

reshaped feature maps from the 3D block and applies three homogenous depthwise separable 288 

convolutional layers, each fol lowed max -pooling and batch normalization layers. This branch 289 

captures structural and spatial patterns such as leaf shape, venation, and surface texture, 290 

enabling efficient and dense spatial feature learning while maintaining computational 291 

efficiency. 292 

The spectral branch (1D CNN) receives the 3D output and reshapes it into one -dimensional 293 

feature vectors, which are passed through three 1D convolutional layers (kernel sizes 3 and 5; 294 

filters = 8,16 and 32). This extracts detailed spectral features capturing biochemical and 295 

physiological properties, including pigment composition, anthocyanin accumulation, and 296 

stress -induced reflectance changes [27] .297 

Batch normalization and dropout (0.3 –0.5) were interspersed between the convolutions to 298 

stabilize training and reduce overfitting. A global average pooling layer was applied to each 299 

branch to c ompress features into compact representations. The outputs from the two parallel 300 

branches were concatenated and passed through a fully connected layer with a binary 301 

classification head. The model predicts homozygous reference (state 0) and homozygous 302 

alter nate (state 2) genotypes . The full layer -by -layer architecture of the proposed hybrid model 303 

is provided in Supplementary Table S1 .304

2.4.2 Developing the training dataset 310 

To ensure unbiased model evaluation and prevent data leakage, the dataset was partitioned at 311 

the plot level. Each of the 194 genotypes was grown in two replicate plots, each containing 36 312 

plants. For each plot, after excludi ng the border plants, 16 plants per plot were retained for 313 

further analysis. Plots were randomly split into 80% training (n = 310 plots) and 20% test (n = 314 

78 plots). For each plant, five non -overlapping patches were randomly extracted, requiring at 315 

least 5 0% of plant pixels correspond to plant tissue based on an NDVI mask ( Supplementary 316 

Figure S3 ). Each patch was assigned the SNP genotype label corresponding to its source plot, 317 

ensuring label consistency. This resulted in 124,000 training patches (310 plots × 16 plants × 5 318 

patches × 5 time points) and 31,200 test patches (78 × 16 × 5 × 5). The same plot -level grouping 319 

was strictly preserved across all cross -validation folds, ensuring that patches from the same plot 320 

never co -occurr ed in training and validation sets. Within the training set, five -fold cross -321 

validation was performed, corresponding to an 80:20 split between training and validation data 322 

in each fold ( 99,200 training patches and 24,800 validation patches per fold). To en hance 323 

spatial -spectral variability and improve generalization, 20% of training patches were 324 

augmented using geometric transformations (horizontal/vertical flipping and rotation) and 325 

additive noise. 326

2.4.4 Model interpretation using Explainable AI 342 

To interpret model’s predictions, two explainable AI (XAI) techniques were employed: 343 

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Gradient -weighted Class Activation Mapping 344 

(GRAD -CAM). SHAP analyzes the contribution of spectral features to model predictions based 345 

on cooperative game theory [28] . The analysis was performed on the fused latent feature 346 

representation from the concatenation of the spatial branch (2D CNN) and spectral branch (1D 347 

CNN) outputs prior to the final classification layer. The 3D CNN branch is progressively 348 

collapsed through the 2D and 1D branches before fusion; SHAP values corresponding to the 349 

1D spectral branch were therefore isolated and aggregated to derive a global spectral 350 

importance profile, enab ling direct attribution of prediction contributions to individual spectral 351 

bands. For a trained model f(x), where x ∈ ℝd represents the fused feature vector, the prediction 352 

is expressed as: 353 

f(x) = ɸ 0 + i (1) 354 

where ɸ 0 is the expected model output over th e background dataset and  ϕi represents the 355 

contribution of the i -th feature to the prediction. SHAP values were computed using the 356 

DeepExplainer framework with 200 randomly selected training samples used as background 357 

data. To derive global spectral import ance profile, SHAP values corresponding to the spectral 358 

branch were isolated and aggregated. Positive and negative SHAP values indicate features that 359 

increase or decrease the predicted probability of a given genotype class respectively. 360 

Grad -CAM was applie d to the final convolutional layer of the spatial branch to identify regions 361 

contributing to model predictions. For a target class c, with S c been the score and A k, the K th

2.4.5 Evaluation metrics for model performance 369 

Model performance was evaluated using average accuracy (AA), F1 score, and receiver 370 

operating characteristic (ROC) cur ve analysis. AA provides a class -wise performance summary 371 

by computing the mean accuracy across classes. The F1 -score accounts for class imbalance by 372 

combining precision and recall into a single metric . ROC analysis was critical because the 373 

workflow requir es tuning the trade -off between sensitivity and specificity using different 374 

thresholds (e.g., high sensitivity for rare allele detection, high specificity for reliable 375 

genotyping). While AA provides an overview of per -class performance, the F1 -score is mor e376 

robust under class imbalance. ROC analysis complements both metrics by quantifying the 377 

model’s ability to distinguish between genotype classes across all thresholds. Together, these 378 

metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of model robustness, sensitiv ity, and 379 

discriminative performance. 380

4. DISCUSSION 636 

This study presents a deep learning framework linking genetic variation to high -dimensional 637 

phenotypic signatures by inferring SNP genotypes directly from hyperspectral imaging (HSI) 638 

data. Prior to model development, spectral profiles were analyzed to assess genotype -dependent 639 

variation. Although average spectral profiles across genotypes appeared broadly similar, su btle 640 

differences in spectral shape and intensity across wavelengths enabled discrimination among 641 

genotypes. These variations are consistent with established relationships between reflectance 642 

characteristics and plant physiology. For instance, variation in the red -edge and near -infrared 643 

regions is associated with internal leaf structure and light scattering within the mesophyll, while 644 

visible wavelengths are primarily linked to pigment absorption [29] . Furthermore, reflectance 645 

variation is influenced by canopy architecture, which affects the near -infrared plateau through 646 

changes i n scattering dynamics [31] [32] .647 

These subtle yet informative variations hi ghlight the need for modeling approaches capable of 648 

capturing complex spatial -spectral relationships. From a methodological perspective, the 649 

hybrid model demonstrated that jointly modeling spatial and spectral information improves 650 

predictive performance. C ompared to state -of -the -art models, the hybrid approach reduced 651 

variance and improved generalization, suggesting that genotype –phenotype relationships are 652 

inherently complex and nonlinear. Furthermore, the reduced computational cost of the hybrid 653 

model rel ative to 3D convolutional approaches makes it more suitable for scalable phenotyping 654 

pipelines [33] .655 

By leveraging both spectral and spatial information, the proposed framework enables the 656 

prediction of SNP genotypes from HSI -derive d phenotypes, providing a complementary 657 

perspective to conventional genotype –phenotype mapping. The high predictive performance 658 

observed for SNPs with known phenotypic effects, particularly those associated with 659 

anthocyanin -related loci (e.g., ant.loc5 and ant.loc9 ), supports the hypothesis that functional 660 

variants affect biochemical processes, which in turn generate consistent and learnable spectral 661 

patterns in HSI data. In contrast, neutral variants do not produce distinguishable signatures. 662 

The ability o f the model to accurately predict specific SNP genotypes from HSI data highlights 663 

the sensitivity of hyperspectral measurements to underlying biochemical changes. Anthocyanin 664 

accumulation, for example, is known to influence reflectance properties in the vi sible and near -665 

infrared regions, particularly within wavelengths associated with pigment absorption [34] . The 666 

high pred ictive performance for SNPs with known phenotypic effects therefore provides 667 

biological validation of the framework, suggesting that the model captures physiologically 668 

meaningful variation rather than spurious correlations. Similarly, the detection of SNPs 669

two SNPs, we computed LD (r²) between them and the four known phenotypic SNPs. All 683 

pairwise r² values were well below our exc lusion threshold of 0.3 (maximum r² = 0.131), 684 

confirming that the observed signals are unlikely to be driven by linkage to the known 685 

functional loci. However, these observations should be interpreted with caution. Despite efforts 686 

to minimize LD with known  loci, residual LD with unknown functional variants cannot be ruled 687 

out. Alternatively, these SNPs may capture subtle phenotypic effects not detected by 688 

conventional GWAS approaches, highlighting the sensitivity of HSI to underlying 689 

physiological variation.  In this case, model predictive performance may serve as a proxy for 690 

biological relevance, although further validation is required .691

anthocy anin -related SNPs aligns with established spectral signatures of anthocyanin and 696

rather than background artifacts. In contrast, low -performing SNPs exhibited diffuse and 701 

unstructured SHAP and Grad -CAM patterns, consistent with the absence of meaningful 702 

genotype –phenotype signals. Notably, high -perfor ming randomly selected SNPs displayed 703 

more structured spectral and spatial patterns, suggesting that the model captured consistent, 704 

non -random variation. However, whether these patterns reflect true biological effects or 705

5. CONCLUSION 747 

This study demonstrates that hyperspectral imaging (HSI), combined with deep learning, can 748 

be used to  detect  genotype --phenotype relationships  by  predicting genotypes at SNP loci from 749 

hyperspectral phenotypes  - a reverse paradigm compared to conventional approaches . The 750 

hybrid CNN framework achieved high predictive accuracy for SNPs with known p henotypic 751 

effects, while a statistical null distribution confirmed that this performance reflected genuine 752 

biological signals rather than noise. Explainable AI further linked model predictions to 753 

interpretable spectral and spatial features, bridging the ga p between data -driven modeling and 754 

plant physiology. 755 

Beyond demonstrating feasibility, this framework has practical implications for crop breeding 756 

and genetic discovery. By enabling non -destructive prediction of SNP genotypes from HSI 757 

data, DeepPheno offer s a pathway for rapid, high -throughput screening of breeding populations 758 

without the need for extensive genotyping. The ability to identify genotype –phenotype 759 

associations from imaging data also positions this approach as a hypothesis -generating tool for 760 

discovering novel traits and candidate genes. In the future, integrating this framework with 761 

temporal HSI data, multi -environment trials, and genomic prediction models could further 762 

accelerate marker -assisted selection and precision breeding. With validatio n and scaling, 763 

DeepPheno has the potential to become an AI tool in plant breeding pipelines, complementing 764 

genomic selection by providing cost -effective, field -deployable phenotypic profiling. 765
