# GCM: metric-guided clustering by genetic algorithm for correlation-defined modules 

Luis Javier Madrigal-Roca 1* , John K. Kelly 1

1 Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology, University of Kansas * madrigalrocalj@yahoo.com 

KEYWORDS 

clustering, genetic algorithm, gene co-expression, gene modules 

# Abstract 

Gene co-expression analyses identify “good” modules by a correlation criterion. However, standard pipelines detect modules with greedy algorithms that optimize other quantities and only measure correlation afterwards. We present a method called Genetic Clustering by Metric(GCM hereafter), an open-source Python tool that closes this gap by treating module detection as maximum-likelihood inference and solving it globally. In GCM, the correlation objective is, up to a constant and the sample-size factor, the profile log-likelihood of an explicit generative model: a block-diagonal one-factor Gaussian in which each module is a single regulator with equal-magnitude ± loadings. This bases model selection on a principled footing through a genuine BIC/AIC in correlation space. GCM maximizes this likelihood with a memetic genetic algorithm: a population-based search hybridized with a greedy local refinement that reassigns genes after the fact, a move the agglomerative clustering at the core of co-expression pipelines cannot make. Across a replicated noise sweep, GCM reproducibly surpasses hierarchical correlation clustering and k-means with the lowest variance, and an ablation shows the local-search step is responsible; the advantage persists when the number of modules is unknown and when unstructured genes must be ignored. GCM faithfully optimizes geometric indices on the Iris benchmark dataset. For a breast-cancer RNA-seq it recovers coherent modules that predict tumor-versus-normal status. GCM depends only on NumPy and SciPy and exposes one swappable-metric interface with single- and multi-objective modes. 

# Author summary 

When biologists group genes by how similarly they are expressed, they usually run a standard clustering method and then score the result with a separate quality measure. The method, however, was never trying to do well on that measure because it optimizes its own internal objective. We built a tool, GCM, that removes this gap: the user picks the quality measure they actually care about, and the tool searches directly for the grouping that scores best on it. The search is performed by a genetic algorithm, a population-based optimizer that mixes and mutates candidate groupings over many generations. GCM includes a purpose-built score for “modules” of co-expressed genes, as well as several widely used geometric scores, and it can balance two competing scores at once to choose how many groups the data support. We show on synthetic data with a known answer, on a textbook dataset, and on real expression data that the tool recovers the intended structure and lets researchers make explicit, and optimize for, their own definition of a good cluster. # Introduction 1

High-throughput technologies for profiling gene expression have revolutionized the field of molecular biology, 2

enabling researchers to capture the intricate patterns of gene regulation and unravel the underlying genetic 3

networks. However, the sheer volume and complexity of gene expression data pose significant challenges in 4

extracting meaningful insights [1]. One critical step in this process is the clustering of genes based on their 5

expression profiles, which aims to group together genes exhibiting similar patterns of expression across 6

various experimental conditions or time points [2]. Clustering is a foundational step in computational biology, 7

from grouping co-expressed genes into putative regulatory modules [3, 4] to shedding light on regulatory 8

pathways mechanisms [5], disease-associated genes [6], and potential therapeutic targets [7]. By partitioning 9

a set of objects into groups based on similarity, cluster analysis identifies intrinsic structures in data to gain 10 

insight [8, 9]. 11 

The dominant practice has two stages. First, a fixed algorithm such as k-means [10] or hierarchical 12 

clustering [3] produces a partition. Second,a cluster-validity index like the silhouette [11], the Davies–Bouldin 13 

index [12], the Calinski–Harabasz criterion [13], or an information criterion such as BIC [14] is used to 14 

evaluate that partition, often to choose the number of clusters. The objective the algorithm optimizes (for 15 

k-means, within-cluster sum of squares) and the objective by which the result is ultimately judged are 16 

therefore distinct. When the two disagree, the reported partition is optimal for neither the user’s stated 17 

criterion nor, necessarily, the underlying biology. 18 

This gap is particularly consequential in gene co-expression analysis. There, the notion of a good module 19 

is intrinsically correlational: genes in a module should be strongly co-expressed across conditions, regardless 20 

of their absolute expression levels or Euclidean geometry. Tools such as WGCNA [15], Clust [16], and 21 

UNCLES [17] encode this intuition through correlation networks or consensus procedures, and comparative 22 

studies show that module-detection performance depends strongly on how the objective is framed [18]. Yet 23 

the correlation-based quality of the final modules is still, in most pipelines, assessed only after the clustering 24 

algorithm has finished optimizing a different, geometric objective. 25 

We argue for inverting the workflow by making the validity metric itself the optimization target. 26 

Concretely, the user specifies the scalar criterion that defines a good partition for their problem, and a search 27 

procedure evolves a partition to optimize that criterion directly. This is attractive whenever the criterion of 28 

interest is (i) not what standard algorithms optimize, (ii) non-differentiable or combinatorial, or (iii) most 29 

naturally expressed as a trade-off between competing concerns—fit versus parsimony, compactness versus 30 

separation. Direct optimization of clustering criteria has a long history in evolutionary computation [19, 20], 31 

but it is rarely packaged as a practical, dependency-light tool aimed at the co-expression use case and built 32 

around a correlation objective. 33 

While traditional numerical optimizers—such as Newton-Raphson, Powell’s method, or the Simplex 34 

algorithm—are highly effective for continuous parameter estimation, they are fundamentally ill-suited for this 35 

clustering framework. These methods require a continuous search space and differentiable objective functions 36 

to calculate valid step directions. In contrast, identifying optimal partitions is an inherently discrete, 37 

combinatorial problem. Our objective functions are highly discontinuous with respect to categorical cluster 38 

labels, resulting in a rugged fitness landscape riddled with local optima where gradient-based methods 39 

become trapped. Furthermore, while stochastic Bayesian approaches (such as Reversible Jump MCMC) 40 

could theoretically navigate this discrete landscape by jumping between partition states, the computational 41 

time cost of sufficiently sampling such a vast combinatorial space is prohibitive for large datasets. The 42 

Genetic Algorithm provides the necessary robustness to directly evolve discrete partition vectors, efficiently 43 

navigating the non-differentiable landscape via crossover and mutation without the extreme computational 44 

overhead of exhaustive Bayesian sampling or the restrictive assumptions of gradient-based methods. 45 

Here we present GCM (Genetic Clustering by Metric), an open-source Python package that clusters data 46 

by directly optimizing a chosen cluster-validity metric with a memetic genetic algorithm (GA), which is an 47 

evolutionary algorithm hybridized with a problem-specific local search that refines each candidate solution 48 

within its own “lifetime,”. This strategy was introduced by Moscato [21] and surveyed by Neri and 49 

Cotta [22]. Genetic algorithms (GAs) could be a promising solution for clustering tasks [23], particularly in 50 

the context of gene expression data [9]. Other attempts have been made to use evolutionary algorithms in 51 

this or similar contexts, with overall promising results [24 – 27]. GAs are optimization algorithms that mimic 52

# Materials and methods 67 

## Overview and problem statement 68 

GCM clusters n elements (the rows of a data matrix X ∈ Rn×d; in co-expression analysis, n genes measured 69 

across d samples) by searching the space of partitions for one that optimizes a user-chosen validity metric. A 70 

partition is represented as an integer label vector ℓ ∈ { 0, 1, . . . , K }n, where ℓi = c assigns element i to cluster 71 

c and the optional label 0 marks an element as unassigned. The user fixes an upper bound gmax on the 72 

number of clusters. Each candidate partition ℓ is scored by one or more metric functions m(ℓ) ∈ R; the GA 73 

evolves a population of partitions to optimize these scores in their natural direction (maximize or minimize). 74 

## Performance-target metrics 75 

GCM treats every metric as an interchangeable “performance target” or fitness measure [28]. Table 1 lists 76 

the built-in targets, the input format each one is design to work with, and its optimization direction. They 77 

fall into two families: correlation-based objectives designed for modular data, and standard 78 

geometric/probabilistic indices. 79

Model selection: information criteria. The raw likelihood favours more modules, since splitting a 100 

coherent module into coherent sub-modules does not necessarily lower L. Because each module carries a 101

Geometric and probabilistic indices. For data whose clusters are defined geometrically, GCM provides 109 

four standard indices computed on X: the mean silhouette coefficient [11] (maximize; range [ −1, 1]), the 110 

Davies–Bouldin index [12] (minimize), the Calinski–Harabasz variance-ratio criterion [13] (maximize), and 111

correlation objective, BIC and AIC reward parsimony explicitly and so can be used for model selection on 116

## Data preparation 118 

Input is a numeric matrix (CSV/TSV or array) with rows as elements and columns as features; header rows 119 

and index columns are auto-detected. By default the data are standardized to zero mean and unit variance. 120 

For the correlation objective, standardization is applied per row (per element/gene), which is the regime 121 

under which the Pearson correlation between rows is meaningful; for the geometric indices, per-feature 122 

standardization is the appropriate choice. The element-by-element correlation matrix R is computed once 123 

and reused across all evaluations of L. 124 

## Dimensionality-reduction visualization 125 

Visual inspection of clustering results is essential for communicating and sanity-checking a partition. GCM 126 

provides built-in tools for projecting the clustered data into two (or three) dimensions, with the cluster labels 127 

shown as colours. Two embedding methods are always available (no extra dependencies): principal 128 

component analysis (PCA), a linear projection preserving maximal variance, and correlation 129 

multidimensional scaling (correlation MDS), a classical MDS embedding [36] on the correlation distance 130 

matrix 1 − | R|. The second method is particularly informative for the correlation objective because it 131 

projects the elements into the same distance space the log-likelihood operates in: if modules are 132 

well-separated in correlation, they appear well-separated in the MDS embedding. For users with additional 133 

packages installed, t-SNE [37] and UMAP [38] are also supported and may better resolve local 134 

neighbourhood structure in large or high-dimensional datasets. These tools are exposed both through the 135 

Python API ( reduce dimensions and plot clusters ) and the command line ( gcmrk visualize ). 136 

## Genetic algorithm 137 

GCM evolves a population of partitions with a self-contained GA (Fig 3). The components are: 138 

1. Initialization. The initial population is seeded with three kinds of partition across candidate cluster 139 

counts k = 2 , . . . , g max : k-means++ partitions (a small internal NumPy k-means with multiple 140 

restarts); correlation-aware partitions obtained by agglomerative (average- and complete-linkage) 141 

hierarchical clustering of the correlation distance 1 − | R|, the classical co-expression clustering strategy; 142 

and random partitions for diversity. User-supplied seeds (e.g. the output of WGCNA or Clust) can also 143 

be injected. The correlation-aware seeds are important for the loglik objective: Euclidean k-means 144 

splits strongly anti-correlated elements that are coherent under the absolute-correlation criterion, 145 

whereas the correlation-distance seeds already respect the structure the GA is asked to optimize. 146 

2. Evaluation. Each partition is scored on the chosen target(s); a cache keyed on the label vector avoids 147 

recomputing identical partitions. 148 

3. Selection and variation. In weighted mode the targets are combined into a single fitness through 149 

direction-aware weights after a bounded squashing transform that places metrics of different scales on a 150 

comparable footing, and elitist tournament selection is applied. In nsga2 mode the targets are treated 151 

as competing objectives and NSGA-II [32] fast non-dominated sorting with crowding distance is used. 152 

Variation is two-point crossover on label vectors plus a reassignment mutation that moves a small 153 

fraction of elements to random clusters. 154 

4. Repair. After every operator a partition is relabelled to consecutive integers and constrained to have 155 

between 2 and gmax clusters, so that all validity metrics remain well defined (degenerate single-cluster 156 

or empty-cluster partitions are repaired). 157 

5. Memetic local search. For the correlation objective, the seed partitions and the elite of each 158 

generation are refined by a greedy hill-climb that repeatedly applies the single element reassignment 159 

most improving the objective until none remains (a memetic, Lamarckian GA). The gain of a move 160 

touches only the source and target modules, so it is computed incrementally in O(module size) from 161 

Cs (Eq (1)); a full sweep is O(n2). This step is decisive: it performs exactly the move that 162

## Choosing the number of clusters 172 

Because L (Eq (2) ) has no parsimony term (penalization against more clusters), GCM offers three routes to 173 

the right number of clusters. (i) Constrain gmax to the expected number of modules. (ii) Optimize a 174 

penalized correlation criterion (loglik aic or loglik bic , Eq (3) ) as a single objective under a loose gmax ; 175 

this selects K within correlation space and is our recommended default when K is unknown. Equivalently, 176 

one can scan gmax with loglik and pick the K that minimizes Eq (3) . (iii) Explore an explicit trade-off by 177 

optimizing loglik jointly with a parsimony target under NSGA-II and reading the knee of the Pareto front; 178 

pairing with the geometric bic is informative only when the modules are also geometrically compact, 179 

whereas the penalized correlation criteria avoid that assumption. 180 

## Benchmark datasets and evaluation 181 

We evaluate on three datasets with known ground truth and one empirical expression dataset. 182 

Synthetic-Easy and Synthetic-Hard are generated by GCM’s modular simulator: each module is driven by 183 

a shared latent factor (with random ±1 loadings, so within-module genes are strongly correlated in absolute 184 

value while genes in different modules are not), with per-entry Gaussian noise added and rows finally 185 

standardized. Synthetic-Easy has three equal modules (75 genes across 80 samples) at low noise, giving 186 

strong separation (mean within-module |r| = 0 .66 versus between-module 0 .07). Synthetic-Hard has five 187 

unequal modules (15, 20, 25, 30, 10 genes; 100 genes across 50 samples) at higher noise, giving weak 188 

separation (within 0 .33 versus between 0 .12). 189 

Iris [33] is the classic 150-sample, 4-feature, 3-class benchmark; it is low-dimensional and geometric rather 190 

than correlational, and we use it to show that the same engine, driven by geometric targets, faithfully 191 

optimizes those indices on data outside the co-expression regime. 192 

Empirical data. We apply GCM to GSE183947 [39], a breast cancer RNA-seq dataset of 60 samples (30 193 

tumours and 30 patient-matched adjacent-normal tissues) with 20246 genes quantified as FPKM. After log 2 194 

transformation we cluster the 200 most variable genes into co-expression modules, and assess each module 195 

both by its internal correlation coherence and by whether its eigengene (the leading principal component of 196 

the module’s expression across samples) distinguishes tumour from normal tissue (Welch t-test), linking the 197 

recovered modules to the clinical phenotype. 198 

Decisive benchmark. To test whether globally optimizing the block likelihood actually improves on greedy 199 

alternatives, we run a replicated benchmark on the five-module design over a noise sweep 200 

σ ∈ { 0.7, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0}, with 30 random datasets per noise level. We compare: GCM (memetic); GCM with 201 

the local-search step disabled (an ablation isolating its contribution); agglomerative clustering on 1 − | R| 202 

with average and with complete linkage (the algorithmic core of correlation-network methods such as 203 

WGCNA), cut at the true k; and k-means. We also evaluate model selection with k unknown 204 

(GCM/ loglik aic versus hierarchical clustering whose cut minimizes the same penalized criterion) and 205 

robustness to unstructured “noise” genes that belong to no module. 206 

We use the adjusted Rand index (ARI) [40] wirsanskyHGA2020; for the single-dataset summaries 207 

(Table 3) we also report Hungarian-matched accuracy. Results are reported as mean ± SD over the random 208 

datasets. All code to reproduce the experiments, figures, and tables is provided with the package. 209

# Results 210 

## Recovering correlated modules on synthetic data 211 

On Synthetic-Easy, GCM driven by the correlation log-likelihood (with gmax set to the true count) recovers 212 

the planted three-module structure perfectly (ARI = 1 .00), whereas the k-means baseline—given the same 213 

true k—fails badly (ARI = 0 .22). The gap is diagnostic: because module loadings carry random signs, each 214 

module contains strongly anti-correlated genes that are coherent under the absolute-correlation objective but 215 

lie far apart in Euclidean space, so k-means splits them. The GA converges within the first few dozen 216 

generations (Fig 3). On Synthetic-Hard, where weak separation and higher noise make the problem 217 

substantially harder, GCM still recovers the five modules well (ARI = 0 .99) while k-means remains poor (ARI 218 

= 0 .27). The correlation-aware hierarchical seeding (Methods) is important here: it supplies the GA with 219 

starting partitions that already respect the absolute-correlation structure, which a Euclidean seeder cannot. 220 

## Global optimization beats greedy clustering, and local search is why 221 

The central methodological question is whether globally optimizing the block likelihood improves on the 222 

greedy agglomerative clustering it is seeded from. The replicated noise sweep (Fig 1, Table 2) shows that the 223 

answer is yes, decisively and reproducibly. At the hard noise level ( σ = 1 .5, mean over 30 datasets), GCM 224 

attains ARI 0 .94 versus 0 .80 for average-linkage hierarchical clustering at the true k and 0 .31 for k-means, 225 

and its variance across datasets is the smallest of all methods, so the advantage is consistent rather than 226 

occasional. The gap widens as noise grows. An ablation pins the cause on the memetic local search: 227 

disabling it drops GCM to ARI 0 .88 (Fig 2). The genetic operators and correlation-aware seeds alone already 228 

match or modestly exceed hierarchical clustering, but it is the hill-climb with the designed reassignment of 229 

elements a posteriori (a move greedy agglomeration cannot make) the feature that produces the decisive 230 

margin and the low variance. 231

## Selecting the number of clusters without knowing it 232 

When only loglik is optimized under a loose gmax , the correlation objective can over-segment, since Eq (2) 233 

carries no parsimony term. The effect, however, is mild once the likelihood is optimized well: on 234 

Synthetic-Easy the optimum already coincides with the true partition ( k = 3), and on Synthetic-Hard a loose 235 

bound yields only k = 6 (true 5; Table 3), one module above the truth. The penalized correlation criteria 236 

(Eq (3) ) keep selection near the truth within a single objective: on Synthetic-Easy loglik aic returns k = 3 237 

at ARI = 1 .00, and on this Synthetic-Hard dataset k = 6 at ARI = 0 .90. The decisive test is replication: 238 

over 30 datasets loglik aic selects 6 .7 modules on average at ARI 0 .87, closer to the truth and more 239 

accurate than hierarchical clustering whose cut minimizes the same criterion (7 .5 modules, ARI 0 .83; 240 

Table 4). So, the advantage of global optimization persists when the module count must be inferred. The 241 

fit–parsimony trade-off is also visible by scanning gmax (Fig 4): loglik rises and plateaus with k while the 242 

penalized criteria turn over near the true number of modules. 243

the multi-objective mode optimizes loglik against a parsimony target under NSGA-II and returns the full 245 

Pareto front (Fig 5). We note that pairing loglik with the geometric BIC is less effective on these data, 246

## Robustness to unstructured genes 250 

Real gene sets contain many genes that belong to no module. When the five-module design is embedded 251 

among unstructured “noise” genes and the methods must both find the modules and not be derailed by the 252 

noise, GCM retains ARI 0 .96 against 0 .53 for hierarchical clustering (30 datasets). The likelihood isolates the 253 

incoherent genes rather than letting them pollute real modules, whereas greedy linkage forces them into the 254 

nearest module and degrades the recovered structure. 255 

## Optimizer validation on geometric data: Iris 256 

We include Iris not as a recovery benchmark—it is geometric rather than correlational, outside the correlation 257 

objective’s domain—but to verify that the GA is a faithful, competent optimizer of any geometric index it is 258 

given. It is: driven by silhouette it returns a partition with silhouette 0 .58, matching exactly the optimum 259 

found by k-means at k = 2 (0 .58), so the optimizer reaches the index’s best value rather than a local one. 260 

That this partition has k = 2 rather than three clusters is a property of the index , not the optimizer: on 261 

Iris the three-species labelling has silhouette only 0 .38, below both the two-cluster split (0 .58) and the best 262 

three-cluster k-means partition (0 .46), because versicolor and virginica overlap so heavily that merging them 263 

is geometrically cleaner (Fig 6). No silhouette-maximizing method can return the three species here; GCM 264 

correctly returns what the criterion prefers (ARI = 0 .57 against the species, matching k-means at the same 265 

k). The lesson is that the choice of criterion, not the optimizer, determines the answer, which is precisely the 266 

control GCM gives the user, and the reason the correlation objective matters for data where a geometric 267 

index would be the wrong question. 268 

## Empirical co-expression modules: breast cancer 269 

Applied to the 200 most variable genes of GSE183947 with the penalized correlation criterion loglik aic , 270 

GCM resolves 12 co-expression modules. These are strongly coherent: the mean within-module absolute 271 

correlation is 0.59, against a background of 0.31 over all gene pairs. Crucially, the modules are biologically 272 

meaningful with respect to the tumour/normal axis that the clustering never saw: 7 of the 12 module 273 

eigengenes differ significantly between tumour and patient-matched normal tissue (Welch t-test, 274 

Benjamini–Hochberg q < 0.05; Fig 7, Table 5). Because the genes were selected only for variability and 275 

clustered only by mutual correlation, the recovery of phenotype-associated modules indicates that the 276 

correlation objective captures co-regulation structure that tracks the underlying biology. 277 

## Visualizing cluster structure in reduced dimensions 278 

To provide visual confirmation that the recovered modules occupy distinct regions of the space in which the 279 

objective operates, we embed each clustering result into two dimensions using PCA and correlation MDS 280 

(1 − | R|; see Methods). On Synthetic-Easy the three recovered modules form well-separated clouds in both 281 

embeddings (Fig 8), consistent with the perfect ARI. On Synthetic-Hard, where noise and unequal module 282 

sizes degrade separation, PCA shows considerable overlap because co-expressed but anti-correlated genes are 283 

geometrically distant; the correlation MDS, which measures distance in the same absolute-correlation space 284 

as the objective, reveals clearer module boundaries. For the empirical breast-cancer data, the 285 

correlation-MDS embedding of the 12 modules shows that even on real gene expression data the recovered 286 

modules occupy coherent, distinguishable regions of correlation space (Fig 9). These visualizations are 287 

produced automatically by the software and are available to users for any dataset. 288

# Discussion 289 

GCM reframes co-expression module detection as maximum-likelihood inference under an explicit generative 290 

model. Solving that inference problem is globally effective. The correlation objective is not a heuristic: it is, 291 

up to a constant and the sample-size factor, the profile log-likelihood of a block-diagonal one-factor Gaussian 292 

model in which each module is driven by a single regulator with equal-magnitude ± loadings [41] (S1 293 

Appendix). This footing makes its model selection principled, in the sense that the number of modules is 294 

chosen by BIC/AIC over the d samples (Eq (3)), and explains why pairing it with a geometric information 295 

criterion fails: those assume Euclidean-Gaussian clusters, which the absolute-correlation model contradicts 296 

whenever modules contain anti-correlated genes. 297

Given the model, the contribution that matters in practice is optimization quality. Greedy agglomerative 298 

clustering on the correlation distance—the core of widely used co-expression pipelines (is a strong seed but a 299 

poor optimizer): once it merges two genes it can never separate them. Our replicated benchmark shows that 300 

hybridizing the GA with a memetic local search, which reassigns genes after the fact, surpasses hierarchical 301 

clustering across the noise range and does so consistently (lowest variance), and an ablation attributes most 302 

of the gain to the local-search step specifically. The same advantage holds when the number of modules is 303 

unknown and when unstructured genes must be ignored. In other words, the true (simulated) module 304 

structure is the likelihood optimum, and the value of the method is that it actually reaches it. 305 

The approach has clear limits. Local search is what makes the optimizer competitive, so on problems 306 

where the objective does not decompose into incremental moves that are numerically inexpensive, it will be 307 

less efficient. Scaling to genome-wide matrices is bounded by the O(n2) correlation matrix and 308

# Conclusion 320 

We introduced GCM, a dependency-light Python tool that detects co-expression modules by maximizing the 321 

likelihood of an explicit block-diagonal one-factor Gaussian model with a memetic genetic algorithm. Placing 322 

the correlation objective on a likelihood footing makes its model selection principled, and hybridising the 323 

search with a greedy local refinement lets it reproducibly surpass the greedy agglomerative clustering on 324 

which standard co-expression pipelines rely. This advantage we establish with a replicated benchmark and an 325 

ablation, and that persists under unknown module count and unstructured-gene contamination. The same 326 

engine generalizes to geometric indices and recovers phenotype-associated modules in breast-cancer RNA-seq. 327 

The software, documentation, and full reproduction scripts are openly available. 328
