# Evolutionary regimes determine the accuracy of epistasis inference from temporal genetic data 

Kai S. Shimagaki 1,2,3 and John P. Barton 1,2, †     

> 1Department of Computational and Systems Biology, University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine, USA. 2Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of Pittsburgh, USA. 3Michigan Center for Applied and Interdisciplinary Mathematics, College of Literature, Science, and the Arts, University of Michigan, Michigan, USA. †Address correspondence to: jpbarton@pitt.edu.

Epistasis, the non-additive effects of mutations, shapes fitness 1

landscapes and evolutionary trajectories. Temporal genetic 2

data reveal evolutionary dynamics and could be used to infer 3

epistatic interactions, especially through linkage disequilibrium 4

(LD) between interacting mutations. However, other evolution-5

ary forces can also generate LD, challenging inference. Here, 6

we systematically evaluated the accuracy of a variety of epista-7

sis inference approaches across a range of selective pressures, 8

recombination rates, and population sizes. In general, we found 9

that inference accuracy depends on the evolutionary regime: 10 

methods based on marginal path likelihood (MPL) performed 11 

best under strong selection and low recombination, whereas 12 

quasi-linkage equilibrium (QLE) approaches were more accu-13 

rate when recombination is frequent. We further showed that 14 

the strength of genetic drift can influence inference accuracy for 15 

approaches that learn from changes in allele frequencies over 16 

time. Collectively, our results show that the detectability of 17 

epistasis from temporal genetic data depends on the interplay 18 

between selection, recombination, and genetic drift, providing 19 

guidance for method selection across evolutionary contexts. 20 

Introduction 21 

The concept of a fitness landscape, which maps genotype to 22 

reproductive capacity or fitness, is one of the most fundamen-23 

tal frameworks for understanding population evolution. This 24 

idea was introduced as an intuitive description of evolution as 25 

a hill-climbing process on a landscape, where regions of high 26 

and low survival correspond to peaks and valleys 1,2 . Because 27 

fitness landscapes are inherently high-dimensional, where the 28 

dimension grows exponentially with sequence length, and be-29 

cause empirical information is limited, their practical use was 30 

historically restricted. However, recent experimental and the-31 

oretical advances have made quantitative estimation of fitness 32 

landscapes increasingly feasible, and their role in understand-33 

ing evolutionary dynamics has become ever more central. 34 

Epistasis, broadly defined as the non-additive effect of 35 

mutations on phenotype, including fitness, is central to 36 

understanding genotype-phenotype maps and fitness land-37 

scapes 1,3,4 . When the fitness effect of a mutation depends 38 

on the surrounding genetic background, evolutionary trajec-39 

tories become historically contingent. That is, because mu-40 

tations that are beneficial in one genetic background may be 41 

neutral or deleterious in another, early mutations can con-42 

strain the set of accessible evolutionary paths. Such effects 43 

have been observed experimentally in diverse systems, in-44 

cluding protein evolution and antimicrobial resistance 5–14 .45 

Epistasis can be specifically defined in several ways, and 46 

different communities emphasize different aspects of the phe-47 

nomenon 15–17 . Throughout this work, we focus on specific 48 

(or idiosyncratic) epistasis, in which the fitness effect of a 49 

mutation depends directly on the allelic state of one or more 50 

other loci and can therefore be represented by explicit inter-51 

action terms in the fitness function. Other important forms 52 

of epistasis, including global epistasis arising from nonlin-53 

ear genotype–phenotype relationships or higher-order inter-54 

actions among multiple loci 4,18–22 , are not considered here. 55 

Our goal is therefore not to distinguish among these differ-56 

ent forms of epistasis, but rather to determine how accurately 57 

pairwise epistatic interactions can be inferred from evolution-58 

ary data under different conditions. 59 

Practically, accurately estimating epistasis, a key deter-60 

minant of fitness, can advance a wide range of fields 3.61 

A deeper understanding of epistasis provides insight into 62 

historical contingency 6,9–14 , and may help to predict how 63 

pathogens such as viruses evolve and escape from im-64 

mune responses 23–27 , as well as how drug resistant vari-65 

ants emerge 28–30 . An accurate characterization of fitness 66 

and epistasis is also essential for guiding artificial evolution, 67 

including protein engineering aimed at developing proteins 68 

with desired functions 31–33 .69 

Recent experimental and theoretical advances have made 70 

it increasingly possible to characterize epistasis and the 71 

structure of fitness landscapes. One important direction 72 

for estimating epistasis, which is the primary focus of the 73 

present work, is the analysis of time-series genetic data, 74 

whether from laboratory evolution experiments 12,34–36 , an-75 

cient DNA 37–39 , or the evolution of pathogens within or be-76 

tween hosts 40–44 . These data reflect selective pressure and 77 

have become increasingly accessible. If epistatic interactions 78 

shape evolutionary dynamics, their effects may be reflected, 79 

at least indirectly, in the temporal patterns of allele frequen-80 

cies and genetic correlations observed in these data 41,42 .81 

Here, we study the performance of computational ap-82 

proaches for inferring epistatic interactions from temporal 83 

genetic data. We focus in particular on evolution from ini-84 

tially clonal populations, a condition that is common in ex-85 

perimental evolution and pathogen transmission with narrow 86 

bottlenecks. Intuitively, epistasis affects the frequencies of 87 

interacting mutation pairs, and measuring their covariance 88

inferring epistasis from temporal data is challenging because 90 

correlations between mutations may also arise from shared 91 

evolutionary history. For example, a selected genotype can 92 

sweep through the population while carrying neutral or dele-93

even in the absence of positive epistatic interactions. Recom-95

evolutionary regime, including the strength of selection, the 101 

rate of recombination, and the magnitude of genetic drift 45 .102 

Recently, numerous computational methods have been 103 

proposed to estimate epistatic interactions or signatures of 104 

epistatic fitness 21,40,45–57 . However, only a limited number 105 

of these approaches have been applied to temporal genetic 106 

data 40,54–57 . It remains unclear which methods most accu-107 

rately detect epistatic interactions and under which evolution-108 

ary regimes they perform best. Furthermore, prior studies 109 

have not systematically examined inference accuracy across 110 

broad evolutionary regimes considering a range of selection 111 

strengths, recombination rates, mutation rates, and popula-112 

tion sizes 40,54–57 . This gap limits our understanding of the 113 

advantages and limitations of these inference methods. Here, 114 

we address this gap by evaluating the accuracy of epista-115 

sis detection across diverse evolutionary regimes using pub-116 

lished methods that infer epistatic interactions from temporal 117 

multilocus genetic data without requiring intensive computa-118 

tion 54–57 .119 

We found that the accuracy of epistasis detection depends 120 

on the underlying evolutionary regimes, specifically the rel-121 

ative contributions of selection, recombination, and genetic 122 

drift. However, the timing of peak detectability is relatively 123 

insensitive when using frameworks incorporating statistics 124 

integrated over generations. In addition, we characterized 125 

the evolutionary regimes and linked them to conditions un-126 

der which inference accuracy is high, using several statistical 127 

metrics. 128

Evolutionary regimes shape population structure 178 

Before studying epistasis inference, we first quantita-179 

tively characterized how the underlying population structure 180 

changes under different evolutionary conditions. Adaptation 181 

from an initially clonal population has been studied exten-182 

sively 12,62–69 , revealing a transition from sweep-like evolu-183 

tion at low mutation supply to clonal interference when mul-184 

tiple beneficial mutations segregate simultaneously. Here, we 185 

quantify these evolutionary regimes using a set of comple-186 

mentary summary statistics describing allele-frequency dy-187 

namics, genetic diversity, fitness, and multilocus population 188 

structure. We then examine how these quantities depend on 189 

selection strength and recombination rate, providing a frame-190 

work for interpreting the inference results presented below. 191 

Site-frequency classes provide a simple summary of allele-192 

frequency dynamics. We classify mutant alleles as high-193 

frequency ( nhigh /L ) when their frequency exceeds 90%, 194 

low-frequency ( nlow /L ) when it is below 10%, and polymor-195 

phic ( npoly /L ) otherwise. These classes show clear differ-196 

ences in simulations with weak and strong selection ( Fig. 1A-197 

B). Under weak selection, recurrent mutation primarily in-198 

creases the number of polymorphic sites, while few muta-199 

tions reach high frequency. In contrast, under strong selec-200 

tion, beneficial mutations and ones on high-fitness genetic 201 

backgrounds rise toward fixation, increasing nhigh at the ex-202 

pense of polymorphic and low-frequency sites. Thus, weak 203 

selection maintains genetic diversity, whereas strong selec-204 

tion drives reduced diversity. 205

i, measures the average single-locus genetic diversity across 207 

the population. Under weak selection, mutation introduces 208 

new variation while alleles remain at intermediate frequen-209 

cies, causing H to increase over time ( Fig. 1C-D ). Strong 210 

selection drives alleles toward fixation or loss, reducing the 211 

average heterozygosity despite the transient increase in di-212

Inference Frameworks for Epistasis Detection 285 

To detect epistatic interactions from temporal genetic se-286 

quences, we considered four approaches: (1) a linkage dis-287 

equilibrium (LD) statistic based on pairwise correlations; (2) 288 

a log-ratio of allele frequency method called universal foot-289 

print of epistasis (UFE) 45,54 ; (3) a quasi-linkage equilibrium 290 

(QLE) method derived from a steady state approximation; 291 

and (4) marginal path likelihood (MPL) method that explic-292 

itly models genotype frequency dynamics and linkage ef-293 

fects. All of these approaches make explicit use of the single-294 

site and pairwise allele frequencies, xi(t) and xij (t), where t295 

indicates the generation at which the sequences are measured 296 

(see Methods for additional details). 297

approaches to quantifying non-random genetic association, 299

metric (LD), which can be used to quantify epistatic effects 301 

with specific caution depending on the problems 72,73 . The 302 

LD metric Dij (t) for interaction between loci i and j at gen-303

Genetic drift can limit inference from allele frequency 538 

trajectories 539 

One of the key differences between the MPL and QLE frame-540 

works is the way that they use temporal information. The 541 

MPL approach infers selection coefficients and epistatic in-542 

teractions from changes in allele frequencies over time. In 543 

contrast, QLE estimates epistatic interactions from covari-544

a result, QLE implicitly integrates information across time, 546 

whereas MPL depends more directly on short-term allele fre-547 

quency changes. 548 

Therefore, we hypothesized that noise due to genetic drift 549 

might impair the accuracy of MPL when the population size 550 

is small. Specifically, fluctuations in allele frequencies due 551

Statistical power for epistasis detection 601 

To determine whether our conclusions depend on the choice 602 

of performance metric, we complemented the AUROC anal-603 

ysis with the true-positive rate at a fixed false-positive rate 604 

of 5% (TPR@5% FPR), which assesses statistical power. 605 

While AUROC summarizes performance across all decision 606 

thresholds, TPR@5% FPR quantifies the fraction of true 607 

epistatic interactions recovered while controlling the false-608 

positive rate. This metric therefore provides a more stringent 609 

measure of detection power. 610 

The TPR@5% FPR analysis confirmed the main results of 611 

our previous AUROC analyses ( Supplementary Figs. S21-612 

S22 ). For both positive and negative epistasis, inference ac-613 

curacy generally improved with increasing population size, 614 

with MPL showing the largest gains as genetic drift was re-615 

duced. QLE performed well in smaller populations, although 616 

its performance improved more gradually with increasing N .617 

Recombination reduced detection power across methods, and 618 

stronger selection generally increased it. Consistent with 619 

the AUROC analyses, positive epistatic interactions were de-620 

tected more accurately than negative interactions, but with a 621 

similar relative dependence on selection strength, recombina-622 

tion rate, population size, and mutation rate. 623

Discussion 654 

Temporal genetic data encode selective pressures, including 655 

features of the fitness landscape and epistatic interactions, yet 656 

inferring these effects remains challenging. Linkage disequi-657 

librium (LD) provides a useful signal of both selection and 658

on the evolutionary dynamics of the population. 663 

Here, we tested four approaches for inferring epistatic in-664 

teractions from temporal genetic data: linkage disequilibrium 665 

(LD), the universal footprint of epistasis (UFE) 45,54 , quasi-666 

linkage equilibrium (QLE) 79,80 , and marginal path likelihood 667

mal. Instead, inference accuracy depended on both the evo-671

method. 673 

These differences can be understood by considering the 674 

assumptions underlying each approach. LD and UFE rely 675 

primarily on pairwise genetic associations and therefore per-676 

form best when epistatic interactions generate detectable cor-677 

relations that are not overwhelmed by background linkage or 678 

sampling noise. QLE infers epistatic interactions from co-679 

variance structure averaged across multiple time points, mak-680 

ing it comparatively robust to stochastic fluctuations in allele-681 

frequency trajectories. In contrast, MPL explicitly models 682 

temporal changes in allele frequencies and therefore benefits 683 

from large populations, where genetic drift is weak and evo-684 

lutionary trajectories more faithfully reflect selection. Con-685 

sequently, MPL performs best under conditions of strong se-686 

lection, limited recombination, and sufficiently large popula-687 

tions, whereas QLE remains competitive in smaller popula-688 

tions and recombining populations that more closely satisfy 689 

its underlying assumptions. 690 

Genetic drift emerged as one of the key factors determining 691 

inference accuracy. Trajectory-based methods such as MPL 692 

infer selection and epistatic effects from temporal changes 693 

in allele frequencies, and can therefore be sensitive to their 694 

fluctuations. When the population size is small, fluctuations 695 

due to drift can obscure contributions of selection and epis-696 

tasis, reducing the accuracy of MPL even when linkage is 697 

strong. In contrast, methods based on covariance averaged 698 

across time, such as QLE, are less sensitive to these fluctu-699 

ations. Increasing population size therefore suppresses drift 700 

and substantially improves MPL, while having a compara-701 

tively smaller effect on QLE. 702 

Under our simulation conditions, we found that epistasis 703 

detection based on LD alone is limited to a transient tempo-704 

ral window early in evolution, in line with prior work 45 . Af-705 

ter a transient peak where LD distributions between epistati-706 

cally interacting sites separate from one another, correlations 707 

between loci increasingly reflect random linkage rather than 708 

epistatic interactions, reducing detection accuracy. This be-709 

havior is well captured by a simple scaling heuristic, where 710 

the time of peak detectability coincides with the typical fixa-711 

tion time for selected mutations. 712 

While we considered a range of inference methods and 713 

simulation conditions, our study also has several limitations. 714 

Our fitness model was rather simple, including only addi-715 

tive mutation effects and pairwise epistasis, while omitting 716 

higher-order interactions or global epistasis 10,18,20,21,94–97 .717 

This model also featured a fixed sparsity of epistatic interac-718 

tions and uniform magnitudes of fitness effects. An increase 719 

in the density of true epistatic interactions increases the num-720 

ber of unknown parameters and strengthens the cumulative 721 

effects of interactions, likely making the inference of spe-722 

cific interacting sites more challenging. At the same time, 723 

sparse interaction networks result in a highly imbalanced in-724 

ference problem, with few true interacting pairs among many 725 

possible ones. Systematically varying interaction density 726 

would therefore be a valuable extension of the present work. 727 

Our simulations used a clonal initial condition, one that may 728 

be well-suited to some biological scenarios. For example, 729 

tight transmission bottlenecks are common in the spread of 730 

pathogens, where one or a few individuals seed a new infec-731 

tion 60 , and some experimental evolution studies use clonal 732 

starting populations. However, we also acknowledge that this 733 

condition is not universally applicable. An additional factor 734 

not considered here is the frequency with which genetic data 735 

are sampled over time. For methods such as MPL that rely on 736 

temporal changes in allele frequencies, the spacing between 737 

sampling time points can strongly influence inference accu-738 

racy 41,55 . Finally, our analysis is based on simulated data, 739 

and further work will be needed to assess performance in em-740 

pirical settings where these assumptions may not hold. 741 

Our results also suggest several practical guidelines for ex-742 

perimental design. First, successful epistasis inference re-743 

quires sufficient genetic variation. Larger effective popula-744 

tion sizes and adequate mutation supply increase the num-745 

ber of informative polymorphisms and reduce the effects 746 

of genetic drift, improving the statistical power to resolve 747 

epistatic interactions. Second, epistatic signals must persist 748 

long enough to be observed. Extensive recombination can 749 

weaken informative associations between loci, implying that 750 

inference is more feasible in genomic regions or experimental 751 

systems where recombination is limited, or for interactions 752 

between more closely linked loci. Lastly, the choice of infer-753 

ence framework should reflect the underlying evolutionary 754 

regime. Trajectory-based approaches such as MPL are pow-755 

erful but perform best when allele frequency changes can be 756 

measured accurately over time, while covariance-based ap-757 

proaches such as QLE remain effective in small populations 758 

and regimes with more limited temporal information. 759

AUROC calculations 1286 

Here, we used AUROC as one quantitative measure of the 1287 

accuracy of epistasis inference. Because the true epistatic co-1288 

efficients take three possible values, sij ∈ {± σ, 0}, we evalu-1289 

ated positive and negative epistasis detection as separate one-1290 

vs-rest binary classification problems. For positive epistasis, 1291 

pairs of mutations are sorted in descending order based on 1292 

their corresponding inferred epistatic interactions. These mu-1293 

tations are then transformed into boolean values: true if the 1294 

true epistatic interaction is positive ( sij > 0), and false oth-1295 

erwise (i.e., one-vs-rest). Similarly, to obtain AUROC values 1296 

of negative epistatic interactions, mutations are treated as true 1297 

if corresponding true epistatic interactions are negative, and 1298 

false otherwise. The AUROC values for selection coefficients 1299 

are obtained in the same manner. If the inference is perfect, 1300 

that means all true positives appear first, followed by all false 1301 

positives, resulting in an AUROC of 1.0. In contrast, if true 1302 

positives appear uniformly at random, the AUROC value ap-1303 

proaches 0.5. The AUROC values are obtained from inde-1304 

pendent simulations, and the mean and standard deviation of 1305 

the AUROC values are calculated across the 30 replicates. If 1306 

pairwise frequency remains low over time or if pairs of mu-1307 

tations are never observed, inferring epistasis and analyzing 1308 

the detectability of epistasis is impractical 122 . Therefore, we 1309 

excluded the pairs of mutations with an average pairwise fre-1310 

quency xij below 0.05 over time. 1311

Supplementary Fig. S7. The cases shown correspond to r = 0 case. Y = ∑

α x2

α, where xα is the frequency of genotype α,serves as a genotype-concentration parameter. Its inverse, 1/Y , represents the effective number of genotypes. We also define 

Q = ∑

i<j ⟨χij ⟩/ ∑

i<j ⟨χii χjj ⟩, where χij is the second-order cumulant and ⟨·⟩ denotes the average over independent simulations with the same underlying fitness parameters. Thus, Q measures LD relative to single-locus diversity. The difference is that Y measures concentration at the genotype level, whereas Q measures correlated allele structure across loci. These quantities are expected to behave similarly when genotype-level concentration creates linkage disequilibrium among many loci. Under neutral evolution, Y ∼

1/(1 + 2 μN L ), and this approximation matches the steady-state values of Y for the weak-selection cases with σ = 10 −3 (panels A,

C, E, and G). Selection can increase Y by amplifying high-fitness genotypes; however, in our simulations, the effect of selection on 

Y was more subtle (panels B, D, F, and H). As expected, Q behaves similarly to Y . However, Q is generally higher in the larger-σ

cases, even when Y is close to 1/N (for example, compare panels G and H). This suggests elevated nonrandom association between loci under stronger selection. Overall, Y and Q show qualitatively similar behavior, suggesting elevated LD is often associated with genotype concentration. However, elevated Q when Y is small suggests also that nonrandom associations can also persist even when the population is diversifed ( Y ≃ 1/N ), likely due to selection and epistasis.
