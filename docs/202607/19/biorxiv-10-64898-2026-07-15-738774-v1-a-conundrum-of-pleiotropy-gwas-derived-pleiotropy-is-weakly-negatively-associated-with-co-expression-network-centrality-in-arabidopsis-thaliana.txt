Abstract 19 Pleiotropy, the influence of a single gene on multiple phenotypic traits, is a fundamental feature 20 of genetic systems but remains difficult to quantify at genome-wide scales. Genome-wide 21 association studies (GWAS) provide one avenue to characterize pleiotropy through multi-trait 22 genetic associations, while functional genomic approaches such as gene co-expression networks 23 offer indirect predictions of gene importance and pleiotropic potential based on network 24 centrality statistics. However, the relationship between GWAS-derived pleiotropy and network-25 based measures of gene importance remains unclear. Here, we quantified gene-level pleiotropy 26 in Arabidopsis thaliana using gene-trait associations from the AraGWAS Catalog and AraPheno 27 databases and compared them to previous estimates of pleiotropy based on gene co-expression 28 networks. GWAS pleiotropy was measured using the Hill number of order 𝑞 = 1, an entropy-29 based metric adapted from ecological diversity theory. Across 2,179 genes, pleiotropy exhibited 30 a right-skewed distribution, with most genes influencing relatively few traits and a smaller subset 31 exhibiting broad multi-trait effects. Contrary to expectations, pleiotropy was weakly negatively 32 correlated with multiple measures of co-expression network centrality, including degree, 33 strength, closeness, and betweenness. Additional analyses showed that GWAS-associated genes 34 were enriched within the co-expression network, exhibited broader expression across tissues, and 35 were modestly enriched among conserved BUSCO genes. Together, these findings reveal a 36 conundrum: although genes identified by GWAS are broadly expressed and integrated into 37 functional networks, the most pleiotropic genes occupy relatively peripheral positions within 38 those networks. This unexpected pattern suggests that GWAS-derived pleiotropy captures a 39 distinct aspect of gene function than co-expression network centrality. 40 41

Introduction 45 

Pleiotropy, the phenomenon in which a single gene influences multiple phenotypic traits, is a 46 fundamental property of genetic systems and plays an important role in shaping evolutionary 47 dynamics (Paaby & Rockman, 2013; Pavlicev & Wagner, 2012). As genes participate in 48 interconnected biological processes, mutations often have effects that extend across multiple 49 traits, generating genetic correlations that can constrain or facilitate evolutionary responses to 50 selection (Wessinger & Hileman, 2016; Zhang, 2023). Consequently, the distribution of 51 pleiotropic effects across genes has important implications for evolutionary theory, influencing 52 the rate of adaptation and shaping the genetic architecture of adaptation (Hansen, 2003) as well 53 the “cost of complexity,” which predicts that mutations affecting many traits experience stronger 54 selective constraints than mutations with more limited effects (Orr, 2000; Orr, 2005). Evaluating 55 these theoretical predictions requires reliable estimates of pleiotropy across large numbers of 56 genes. 57 58 Despite its importance, estimating pleiotropy at genome-wide scales remains challenging. One 59 widely used approach is to infer pleiotropy from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), in 60 which genes associated with multiple phenotypic traits are interpreted as having broader 61 pleiotropic effects. Large-scale GWAS have revealed that pleiotropy is pervasive across complex 62 traits, identifying hundreds of loci associated with multiple phenotypes and enabling the 63 characterization of shared genetic architecture, causal relationships, and genetic overlap among 64 traits (Gratten & Visscher, 2016; Schaid et al., 2016; Visscher & Yang, 2016; Zhu et al., 2021). 65 An alternative approach estimates pleiotropy indirectly from functional genomic data. Gene co-66 expression networks use patterns of coordinated gene expression to infer functional connectivity, 67 with highly connected “hub” genes predicted to participate in numerous biological processes and 68 therefore exhibit greater pleiotropy (Mähler et al., 2017; Muzio et al., 2023). These 69 complementary approaches have increasingly been applied to address evolutionary questions, 70 including how pleiotropy shapes adaptation and parallel evolution (Lai et al., 2025). However, it 71 remains unclear whether GWAS-derived and network-based estimates quantify the same 72 underlying biological phenomenon or instead capture different dimensions of gene function. 73 74 Here, we compare gene-level estimates of pleiotropy obtained from these two complementary 75 approaches. Using Arabidopsis thaliana as a model system, we quantified pleiotropy from gene-76 trait associations in the AraGWAS Catalog (Togninalli et al., 2017) and AraPheno (Togninalli et 77 al., 2019) and compared these estimates with measures of gene co-expression network centrality. 78 Simply counting the number of associated traits may overestimate pleiotropy when a gene has 79 one or a few strong associations but many additional weak associations that are unlikely to 80 reflect broad biological influence. To account for this, we quantified GWAS-derived pleiotropy 81 using the Hill number of order 𝑞 = 1, an entropy-based metric adapted from ecological diversity 82 theory that incorporates both trait richness and the distribution of genetic effects among traits 83 (Alberdi & Gilbert, 2019; Chao et al., 2014; Jost, 2006). Materials and Methods 98 

Data sources 99 

Genome-wide association data were obtained from the AraGWAS Catalog, a curated repository 100 of SNP-trait associations in Arabidopsis thaliana (Togninalli et al., 2017, 2019). Trait metadata, 101 including trait descriptions and classifications, were obtained from the AraPheno database, 102 which provides standardized phenotypic information linked to AraGWAS entries. Gene co-103 expression network statistics and tissue specificity estimates ( 𝜏 ) were obtained from Nocchi et al. 104 (2024), who calculated these metrics using Arabidopsis gene expression data from previously 105 published datasets (Papatheodorou et al., 2018). Co-expression metrics included node degree, 106 node strength, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality. Tissue specificity ( 𝜏 ) was used as 107 a measure of expression breadth, where lower values indicate broader expression across tissues 108 and higher values indicate greater tissue specificity. A genome-wide background set of protein-109 coding genes was obtained from the TAIR10 Arabidopsis thaliana protein annotation available 110 through Ensembl Plants (Cheng et al., 2017; Yates et al., 2026). Gene identifiers were extracted 111 from all annotated protein-coding loci and used as the genomic background for enrichment 112 analyses involving co-expression network genes and BUSCO genes. To evaluate whether highly 113 conserved genes were disproportionately represented among AraGWAS genes, Benchmarking 114 Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCOs) were identified from the embryophyta lineage 115 dataset (Manni et al., 2021; Tegenfeldt et al., 2025). Genes classified as Complete or Duplicated 116 BUSCOs were retained. Transcript identifiers were converted to TAIR10 gene identifiers by 117 removing transcript-specific suffixes, and duplicate entries were removed prior to analysis. 118 119

Results and Discussion 152 

Gene-level GWAS pleiotropy, quantified using the Hill number of order 𝑞 = 1, varied widely 153 across the genome. The distribution of pleiotropy was slightly right-skewed, with most genes 154 exhibiting low to moderate pleiotropy and a smaller subset showing substantially higher values 155 (Figure 1A). Across the 2,179 genes analyzed, the mean Hill number was 9.26 and the median 156 was 8.97. The 5 most pleiotropic genes exhibited a Hill number of 18.79 - 20.62 (Table 1). This 157 pattern suggests that the majority of genes influence relatively few traits, whereas a minority 158 contribute to broad, multi-trait effects. Highly pleiotropic genes were associated with diverse 159 trait categories, including flowering time, root morphology, growth and development, ionomics, 160 and stress or disease resistance. 161

Given the expectation that highly pleiotropic genes occupy central positions within biological 182 networks, we compared GWAS-derived pleiotropy with measures of gene co-expression network 183 centrality. Unexpectedly, GWAS-derived pleiotropy exhibited weak but statistically significant 184 negative correlations with multiple measures of network centrality, including node degree 185 (Figure 1D; Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈ -0.152, 𝑝 = 6.02 × 10 -10 ), node strength (Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈ -0.154, 𝑝 =186 3.15 × 10 -10 ), closeness (Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈ -0.108, 𝑝 = 1.10 × 10 -5 ), and betweenness (Spearman’s 187 

𝜌 ≈ -0.051, 𝑝 = 0.039). Although modest in magnitude, these consistent negative relationships 188 indicate that genes influencing many traits tend to occupy slightly less connected positions 189 within the co-expression network. This result contrasts with network-based models in which 190 highly connected genes are expected to participate in a broader range of biological processes and 191 therefore exhibit greater pleiotropy (Mähler et al., 2017). However, it is broadly consistent with 192 previous analyses showing that GWAS-associated genes occupy relatively peripheral positions 193 within biological interaction networks (Barrenas et al., 2009). 194 195 To determine whether this pattern depended on how pleiotropy was quantified, we examined its 196 relationships with trait richness and effect size. As expected, the Hill number was strongly 197 associated with the number of traits linked to each gene (Figure 1B and 1C; Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈198 0.99, 𝑝 < 2.2 × 10 -16 ), demonstrating that genes associated with more traits generally exhibited 199 greater pleiotropy. This result is consistent with ecological applications of Hill numbers, where 200 richness often dominates diversity estimates when abundance distributions are highly uneven 201 (Chao et al., 2014; Jost, 2006). Consequently, GWAS-derived pleiotropy should be interpreted 202 primarily as a measure of the breadth of traits influenced by a gene rather than the evenness of its 203 effects across traits. 204 205 Likewise, total effect magnitude increased with pleiotropy (Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈ 0.65, 𝑝 < 2.2 × 10 -

206 16 ), reflecting the cumulative effects of genes influencing larger numbers of traits. In contrast, 207 after standardizing effect size by the number of associated traits, mean effect size per trait 208 showed no meaningful relationship with pleiotropy (Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈ -0.04, 𝑝 = 0.09), indicating 209 that highly pleiotropic genes do not exert stronger effects on individual traits. Instead, their 210 greater cumulative effects arise primarily from influencing more traits rather than from stronger 211 per-trait effects. Mean effect size per trait also showed only a weak positive relationship with co-212 expression network degree (Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈ 0.07, 𝑝 = 0.005), indicating that genes occupying 213 more central network positions do not generally exert substantially stronger effects on individual 214 traits. Moreover, the weak negative relationship between co-expression network degree was also 215 observed when trait richness, rather than the Hill number, was used as the measure of pleiotropy 216 (Spearman’s 𝜌 ≈ -0.16, 𝑝 = 1.0 × 10 -10 ), indicating that the negative association with network 217 centrality was robust to alternative measures of gene-level pleiotropy. Together, these findings 218 suggest that highly pleiotropic genes primarily influence many traits through numerous modest 219 effects rather than a small number of exceptionally large ones, consistent with previous empirical 220 work (Paaby & Rockman, 2013; Wang et al., 2010; Zhang, 2023). 221

222 The unexpected negative relationship between GWAS-derived pleiotropy and network centrality 223 could have arisen if genes under the strongest purifying selection were underrepresented in 224 GWAS datasets, consistent with previous suggestions that the relative peripherality of GWAS 225 genes reflects stronger selective constraints on highly central genes (Barrenas et al., 2009). To 226 explore this possibility, we first examined whether genes represented in the co-expression 227 network were more likely to be detected by GWAS. Of the 18,571 genes represented in the co-228 expression network, 1,646 (8.9%) were associated with at least one AraGWAS trait. In contrast, 229 only 523 of 9,057 genes absent from the co-expression network (5.8%) were associated with a 230 GWAS hit. Co-expression network genes were therefore significantly enriched for GWAS-231 associated genes ( odds ratio = 1.59, 95% CI = 1.43 – 1.76, 𝑝 < 2.2 × 10 -16 ), with the network 232 containing 1,646 of 2,169 (75.9%) of all GWAS-associated genes. 233 234 We next examined whether tissue expression breadth influenced GWAS detectability. Genes 235 with at least one GWAS association exhibited lower mean tissue specificity ( 𝜏 = 0.505) than 236 genes without GWAS associations ( 𝜏 = 0.571), indicating that broadly expressed genes were 237 more likely to be detected by GWAS. Logistic regression supported this pattern, with tissue 238 specificity showing a significant negative association with GWAS detectability ( 𝛽 = -0.774 ±239 0.080 SE , z = -9.65, 𝑝 < 2 × 10 -16 ). A one-unit increase in 𝜏 reduced the odds of GWAS detection 240 (odds ratio = 0.46, 95% CI = 0.39 – 0.54). 241 242 Finally, we tested whether highly conserved genes were disproportionately represented among 243 GWAS-associated genes using BUSCO annotations. Of the 1,630 complete or duplicated 244 BUSCO genes, 156 (9.6%) were represented in AraGWAS. In contrast, 7.7% of non-BUSCO 245 genes were represented in AraGWAS. BUSCO genes were therefore modestly but significantly 246 enriched among GWAS-associated genes ( odds ratio = 1.26, 95% CI = 1.06 – 1.50, 𝑝 = 0.009). 247 Together, these analyses indicate that although GWAS preferentially detects broadly expressed 248 and evolutionarily conserved genes, these biases are unlikely to explain the unexpected negative 249 relationship between GWAS-derived pleiotropy and co-expression network centrality. 250 251 Therefore, the negative relationship between pleiotropy and network centrality cannot be 252 explained simply by the underrepresentation of constrained genes in GWAS datasets (Mähler et 253 al., 2017; Barbitoff et al., 2025). Instead, our results extend previous observations that GWAS-254 associated genes occupy relatively peripheral network positions (Barrenas et al., 2009) by 255 demonstrating that this pattern persists even among genes with the highest levels of GWAS-256 derived pleiotropy. Although both approaches are widely used to estimate gene pleiotropy, they 257 appear to quantify complementary rather than equivalent aspects of gene function. More broadly, 258 these results challenge the assumption that different estimates of pleiotropy necessarily capture 259 the same underlying biological phenomenon (Paaby & Rockman, 2013; Zhang, 2023) and 260

emphasize the importance of considering multiple approaches when investigating the genetic 261 architecture of complex traits. 262 263 Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. GWAS-based 264 analyses are subject to internal constraints including limited statistical power, linkage 265 disequilibrium, and confounding among correlated traits (Reinert, 2022; Schaid et al., 2016). 266 Although trait aggregation reduced inflation of pleiotropy estimates arising from repeated 267 measurements of the same biological trait, this methodological choice may also obscure 268 biologically meaningful differences in gene effects across different developmental stages, 269 environmental conditions, or other experimental contexts. In addition, the present framework 270 does not explicitly account for trait correlations, as implemented in alternative approaches such 271 as HOPS (Jordan et al., 2019). Consequently, some degree of statistical pleiotropy may remain in 272 the dataset. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the 273 observed relationships but are unlikely to alter the overall conclusion that GWAS-derived 274 pleiotropy and network centrality capture distinct aspects of gene function. 275 276 Future research should determine whether the disconnect observed here between GWAS-derived 277 pleiotropy and network centrality is a general feature of genetic architecture or is specific to 278 

Arabidopsis thaliana and the datasets analyzed. Applying comparable analyses across additional 279 species, trait datasets, and network resources would help determine the extent to which network 280 position predicts observable pleiotropic variation. More broadly, integrating GWAS, expression, 281 and network data may provide a useful framework for distinguishing genes that are functionally 282 central from those that contribute disproportionately to standing phenotypic variation. 283 284
