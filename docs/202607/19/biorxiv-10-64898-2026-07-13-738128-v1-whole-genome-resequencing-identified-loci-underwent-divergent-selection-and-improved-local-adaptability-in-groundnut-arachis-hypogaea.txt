Abstract 20 

There is an urgent need to expand groundnut genomics knowledge base to improve yield and 21 

adaptability in the target environments. Whole-genome sequencing can discover selective sweeps 22 

in the genomic regions distinguishing adaptive from non-adaptive germplasm within target 23 

environments. When combined with genome-wide association studies (GWAS), this approach 24 

can reveal genes underpinning local adaptability and yield advantage. The objective of this study 25 

was to establish a genome-wide quantitative framework form identifying genomic regions under 26 

selection, with a particular focus on narrowing down regions associated with important yield and 27 

adaptive traits. Moreover, validation of elite haplotype distributions in an independent fully 28 

sequenced groundnut panel. A panel of 197 groundnut accessions was subjected to whole-29 

genome sequencing and phenotypic evaluation to dissect the collection into adaptive and non-30 

adaptive subsets to uncover the genomic regions under selection. This was then combined with 31 

GWAS to uncover genetic variants governing agronomic traits associated with yield and 32 

adaptability. The stringent single and multi-trait analysis identified 60 loci for 12 agronomic 33 

traits, of which seven loci controlled multiple yield related traits and were pleiotropic. Within our 34 

diversity panel, 48 genomic regions showed signs of selection. Among these selective sweeps, 35 

six positively selected loci were co-localized with trait associated loci. A large genomic region on 36 

chr2 spanning ~78 Mb was under divergent selection and harbored genes underpinning yield and 37 

20-pod length. A F-box transcription factor, Arahy.37HYKA, on chr9, and an alanine transferase 38 

protein gene, Arahy.E9MTVL, on chr12 carried peak SNPs associated with yield and related 39 

traits. We further cross-validated our results in another groundnut355 panel, where the 40 

corresponding genes within LD blocks showed significant effects on HKW, pod length and pod 41 

weight. The genomic resources developed here provide a high-resolution variation map to 42 

delineate the genes underpinning yield and developmental traits in groundnut, improved our 43 

understanding of the genetic basis of important agronomic traits, and provide a valuable resource 44 

for further functional genomics studies and groundnut improvement programs. 45

Introduction 49 

Groundnut commonly referred to as peanut ( Arachis hypogaea L.) is an important oil and food 50 

legume crop worldwide (Cason et al., 2023). Groundnut is not only important source for 51 

vegetable oil but is also a major cash crop for elevating farmers income. The globally cultivated 52 

area of groundnut was registered 32.7 m ha with 53.9 m tons annual production, out of which 53 

90% of both groundnut areas and global production is from developing countries (FAOSTAT, 54 

2024). Groundnuts offer a rich source of healthy mono-, and poly-unsaturated fatty acids that 55 

plays vital role in cholesterol regulation as well as supporting cellular health (Shah et al. 2023). 56 

The demand of groundnut and groundnut based products is high due to its high oleic and linoleic 57 

acid, fiber, folic acid, and certainly digested protein, as well as the presence of resveratrol (Raza 58 

et al. 2024). Groundnuts originated from tropical and subtropical regions of South America, 59 

introduced to Spain in 16 th century and were widely cultivated in many European countries until 60 

1840. Groundnut production remains low in several parts of the world, and several reasons of 61 

low productivity are biotic and abiotic stresses, ineffective seed distribution techniques, lack of 62 

scientific knowledge, economic growth, low-input utilization, and political and social facilities 63 

(Varshney et al. 2017). 64 

Population genomics facilitates uncovering the numerous loci involved in the evolutionary 65 

processes and breeding progress, thereby unfold the mechanisms of inbreeding and outbreeding 66 

depressions, population demographic history and genetic basis of local adaptation (Bhat et al 67 

2021). Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have been used in the past for linking 68 

molecular markers and QTLs associated with significant attributes in groundnut (Otyama et al. 69 

2022; Pandey et al. 2014). In this context, the availability of a good quality reference genome, 70 

and genome resequencing at the population level are important strategies to underpin the loci 71 

associated with local adaptability. The international groundnut genome initiative and other 72 

groups have released reference genomes of cultivated groundnut (Bertioli et al. 2019; Chen et al. 73 

2019; Zhuang et al. 2019), and their progenitors (Bertioli et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2016b). 74 

Likewise, the whole-genome resequencing of large groundnut populations has resulted in 75 

identification of loci underpinning agronomic traits evolutionary history (Lu et al. 2024; Zheng 76 

et al. 2024). The development of whole-genome re-sequencing knowledge has made to secure 77 

high-throughput SNP markers progressively more feasible, providing robust support for 78 

inspecting the molecular-level genetic basis of quantitative traits (Zhang et al., 2023). 79

Previously, several GWAS have been conducted for consolidate mapping of very complex traits 80 

in populations like pod weights (Gangurde et al. 2020), yield (Guo et al. 2024), growth habit (Li 81 

et al. 2022), resistance to leaf spot (Oteng-Frimpong et al. 2023), and fatty acid composition 82 

(Otyama et al. 2022) are some of significance of GWAS in groundnuts. A major limitation in 83 

these studies is the use of low-density SNP platforms like reduced representation sequencing or 84 

SNP arrays. The use of whole-genome resequencing could solve this issue. Modern crop 85 

breeding activities select favourable alleles in new cultivars, and these signatures of selection can 86 

be detected by a cross-population comparison approach like EigenGWAS (Chen et al. 2016a). 87 

Several lines of evidence have shown that that genomic regions that exhibit selection signatures 88 

are also enriched for genes associated with biologically important traits (Xie et al. 2015). 89 

Therefore, detection of selection signatures is emerging as an additional approach to identify and 90 

validate novel gene-trait associations in the modern or adapted germplasm in wheat (Afzal et al. 91 

2019), barley (Sharma et al. 2021), and maize (Li et al. 2019; Li et al. 2021). 92 

This is the first study on the whole-genome resequencing of groundnut germplasm adapted in 93 

Pakistan and provide deep insight into the genomic regions underpinning agronomic traits and 94 

local adaptability. 95

Materials and Methods 97 

Plant material and field trials 98 

A diversity panel comprised of 197 accessions were used in this study (Supplementary 99 

Table S1). These 7 cultivars and 128 accessions from four local breeding programs, and 68 100 

exotic accessions from USDA, and ICRISAT. The local accessions were collected from Oilseed 101 

Research Program (NARC), Plant Genetic Resources Institute (NARC, Pakistan), Groundnut 102 

Research Station (Attock, Pakistan) and Barani Agriculture Research Institute (Chakwal, 103 

Pakistan). 104 

The research was conducted over two consecutive years (2021 and 2022). The experiment was 105 

performed at the Oilseeds Research Program at NARC Islamabad (33.6701° N, 73.1261° E) 106 

under optimal field conditions recommended for groundnut cultivation. The seed were planted in 107 

Alfa Lattice Design with three replications. The sowing dates were April 7 and May 5 during the 108 

Kharif seasons of 2021 and 2022, respectively. The experimental plot size was consisted of three 109 

rows of 4 meters length with row-to-row distance of 45 cm, and plant-to-plant distance was 110 

maintained at 10 cm for proper plant growth. The standard agronomic practices were followed. 111 

The data was recorded on 12 morphological traits related to architecture including plant height 112 

(cm), plant width (cm), number of branches, stem girth (mm), leaflet length (unit), leaflet width 113 

(unit), and the remaining six were yield related traits including 20-pod length (cm), shelling 114 

percentage (%), 100-kernel weight (g), kernel length (cm), kernel width (cm), and yield (kg/ha). 115

Whole-genome resequencing and variant calling 153 

The paired-end sequencing libraries were constructed with inserts of approximately 300 bp and 154 

sequenced with Illumina HiSeq 6000 platform with PE150. The raw data generated were cut 155 

with average coverage of 5x per for further analysis. The reads were trimmed and subjected to 156 

quality-control for further alignment to reference genome. The subsequent high-quality reads 157 

were aligned to the genome of cultivated A. hypogaea cv. Trifrunner version 2 158 

(https://data.legumeinfo.org/Arachis/hypogaea/genomes/Tifrunner.gnm2.J5K5/) using burrows-159 

wheeler alignment tool (BWA) V0.7.17 software with default parameters. The SNPs and InDels 160 

were called using Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK) pipeline with the haplotype caller pipeline 161 

(Poplin et al. 2017). Briefly, the SAMTOOLS V1.7 software to convert the sequence 162 

alignment/map (SAM) file generated in the first step into binary alignment map (BAM) format 163 

and perform sorting, and genome analysis toolkit (GATK) V4.2.1.0 software was used to analyze 164 

and classify the SNP. To generate the integrated VCF data, we merged SNP marker using a 165 

custom R script, according to overlapped chromosome and physical position. Moreover, SNP 166 

data were further filtered using VCFtools V0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011). The SNPs and InDels in 167 

the above steps were again filtered for further analysis. All the biallelic SNPs and InDels with 168 

missing rate >0.05, minor allele frequency <0.05, and number of heterozygous genotypes >20 169 

were filtered using bcftools (v 1.10.2) and a final vcf file was generated for subsequent analyses. 170 

Linkage disequilibrium and population structure 171 

Population structure and cryptic relatedness of the 197 groundnut accessions were inferred using 172 

admixture analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), and a neighbor-joining phylogenetic 173 

tree. The population structure was calculated using a Bayesian approach-based program 174 

implemented in fastSTRUCTURE with the number of population clusters (K) set from 2 to 20 175 

(Raj et al. 2014). The optimal K (5) was determined using the chooseK.py script in 176 

fastSTRUCTURE. The highest estimated membership coefficient criterion was employed to 177 

assign unknown accessions to cluster. PCA based on the same dataset was performed at the 178 

individual level using the SNPRelate R package (Zheng et al. 2012). The top two principal 179 

components were used to visualize the genetic relationships among groundnut accessions. The 180 

PCA biplot was generated using the ggplot2 package in R with pre-defined subgroups. In 181

The patterns of genome-wide linkage disequilibrium (LD) among SNPs were studied as an 184

each pair of SNPs using a custom R script. The r2 estimates were transformed using a nonlinear 189 

square root transformation according to the Hill and Weir model (Hill and Weir 1988). The 190 

average genome-wide LD decay plot was visualized using R software (R Core Team 2016). 191 

Selective sweep analysis by XPCLR 192 

To detect the loci that may have undergone selection in two sub-populations, XP-CLR along 193 

with population fixation statistics, FST , using vcftools was used. A 100-kb sliding window and a 194 

10-kb step size was used for XP-CLR analysis. To ensure comparability of the composite 195 

likelihood score in each window, we fixed the number of SNPs assayed in each window to five 196 

with the setting ‘--maxsnps 5 --minsnps 5 (Chen et al. 2010). Meanwhile, to keep the used 197 

genomic windows consistent in the XP-CLR analysis, the weighted FST values were estimated in 198 

each window that required at least five SNPs with the setting ‘--fst-window-size 100,000 --fst-199 

window-step 10,000. Pairwise differentiation between populations ( FST ) was calculated using the 200 

“hierfstat” package of R (Goudet 2005). Evidence for selection across the genome we merged 201 

the adjacent windows with top 10% values into a single window, and the top 0.5% outliers were 202 

determined to represent putative selection signals. In addition, adjacent sweeps separated by a 203 

physical distance of < 100 kb were merged into a single selected locus. 204

Results 215 

Phenotypic diversity and heritability of key agronomic traits 216 

Comprehensive phenotypic characterization of 12 morphometric traits across 197 217 

groundnut accessions for two consecutive years revealed high phenotypic variation within the 218 

panel (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure 1) . Most traits showed a normal distribution, 219 

except for leaflet width, number of branches and shelling percentage. The plant height averaged 220 

25.5 cm, ranging from 12.7 to 47.2 cm, corresponding to a 3.7-fold variation across years. The 221 

yield displayed pronounced diversity, with a mean of 2164 kg ha -1 and a range of 453.4-5509 kg 222 

ha -1 , representing a 12-fold variation among accessions. Kernel width, was the least variable 223 

trait, varying only 1.4-fold and ranged from 7 to 9.9 mm. The analysis of variance (ANOVA) 224 

confirmed highly significant differences among genotypes, years, and their interactions 225 

(Supplementary Table S2) . Broad-sense heritability varied among traits, ranging from 0.4 for 226 

yield (Yld) to 0.88 for kernel width (KW), indicating a substantial genetic distribution to the 227 

observed phenotypic variation. Detailed descriptive statistics (Table) revealed that yield 228 

exhibited the highest coefficient of variation in both years (45.7-49.6%), indicating strong 229 

environmental influence, while kernel width showed the lowest CV (12.9-13.0%), demonstrating 230 

greater stability across environments. Skewness values confirmed normal distribution for most 231 

traits, except number of branches and seeds per pod, which showed positive skewness (1.35-232 

1.38), suggesting improvement potential through selection. Shelling percentage consistently 233 

displayed negative skewness (-0.86), indicating favorable clustering toward higher values. Year 234 

to year comparisons revealed marginal improvements in yield (2155.5 to 2173.6 kg ha -1 ) and 235 

shelling percentage (58.7 to 60.5%), while seed germination showed the most pronounced 236 

increase from 4.43 to 5.25. 237

Population structure and LD decay in the diversity panel 284 

To elucidate the genetic relationship and population structure among 197 groundnut 285 

accessions, the fastSTRUCTURE and principal component analysis (PCA) was used. The 286 

population structure analysis revealed two main genetic groups, which were further subdivided 287 

into 8 sub-populations (coded: population 1- population 8) (Supplementary Table S1) . Group 1 288 

included 143 accessions while Group 2 contained 54 accessions. Most groundnut cultivars bred 289 

in Pakistan were included in Group 1, however 26 accessions, including advanced breeding lines 290 

developed at NARC, Pakistan were included in Group 2. Notably, most of the accessions from 291 

Uruguay, Paraguay and Brazil were included in Group 2. Among the 8 sub populations, 292 

population 1 contained the highest number of accessions (n=57), whereas population 3 contained 293 

the lowest (n=6) (Figure 4A) . All accessions within the main Group 2 were assigned to the sub-294 

populations 2, 3 and 5, while sub-populations 1, 4, 6-8 were specific to Group 1. 295 

The PCA plot revealed that the first two principal components (PCs), accounted for 296 

49.61% of the total genetic variation (Figure 4D) . The broad distribution of accessions along the 297 

PC axes indicated a high genetic diversity within the panel. The phylogenetic tree further 298 

supported the population structure analysis, consistently resolving two main groups with eight 299 

subpopulations. Notably, the two groups exhibited distinct phenotypic profiles (Figure 5). Group 300 

1 was distinguished by significantly higher values for yield related traits, like 100-kernel weight, 301 

kernel width, kernel length, 20-pod length, and overall yield. While Group 2 had significantly 302 

higher values of plant architecture related traits plant height, stem girth and leaflet length and 303 

width (Figure 5A, D, E and F) . Traits like plant width, shelling percentage and number of 304 

branches did not differ significantly between the two groups (Figure 5B, C and I) . The extent of 305 

linkage disequilibrium (LD), estimated by r 2, varied among subpopulations. Genome-wide 306 

analysis revealed that the average distance at which LD decayed to a threshold of r 2 = 0.3 was 307 

approximately 250kb across subpopulations. Subpopulation 6 exhibited the slowest LD decay, 308

Discussion 506 

Phenotypic variation in groundnut diversity panel 507 

In the present study a groundnut diversity panel was assembled and characterized for yield and 508 

plant architecture traits. The phenotypic variation in the diversity panel was more pronounced in 509 

yield, 100-kernel weight and 20-pod length. This can be mainly attributed to the diverse 510 

geographic origin of the groundnut population. A global collection of 390 groundnut accessions 511 

collected from China, USA and India was characterized for 28 agronomic traits and substantial 512 

amount of phenotypic diversity was observed (Lu et al. 2024). The geographic background is a 513 

key factor in delivering morphological variation for economically important vital traits such as 514 

pod yield, grain yield, number of pods per plant, and oleic acid percentage. Our diversity panel 515 

showed near normal like distribution for plant height, plant width and leaflet length which 516 

demonstrates potential of utilization of this material while breeding for plant architecture and 517 

canopy structure. Broadly all the quantitative traits showed divergence in the population except 518 

for kernel width, stem girth and leaflet length. Banla et al. (2020) justified the lesser extent of 519 

variation in these quantitative traits because of a directional selection which reduced cover-wide 520 

phenotypic variance, and the results were also supported in other studies (Upadhyaya et al. 521 

2003). Previously studies have reported significantly lower yield for groundnut cultivars in 522 

Pakistan where in some cases the yield obtained is nearly half as low as that of major groundnut 523 

growing countries (Jahanzaib et al. 2019). Nearly a fifth of the diversity panel reported mean 524 

yield of more than 3000 kg ha -1 which makes these accessions as suitable candidates for 525 

selection under target environmental conditions as high yielding cultivars. The Pearson’s 526 

correlation showed strong positive associations between various yield related traits, whereas Yld 527 

negatively correlated with stem girth, leaflet width, and plant height. The characteristics 528 

associated with grain size and shape play a direct role in determining yield, with these traits 529 

collectively regulated by both maternal and nuclear genes. This implies the potential for their 530 

concurrent selection (Venuprasad et al. 2011). The vegetative traits such as number of branches 531 

positively contributed towards the grain yield in groundnut germplasm (Amoah et al. 2023). 532

The genome variation map developed for our diversity panel showed high SNP density across 534 

genome with variable number of SNPs on different chromosomes. For example, the highest 535 

density of 350 SNPs/Mb (3.5 SNPs/kb) was observed on chr16 whereas SNPs were not dense for 536 

chr1 and chr8. Also, there were 1.4-fold greater number of SNPs on B subgenome (~0.3 million 537 

SNPs) compared to A subgenome (0.41 million SNPs). Similar results were reported earlier and 538 

1.36-fold number of SNPs were observed in a global groundnut collection (Lu et al. 2024). The 539 

higher SNPs density rate at certain chromosomes like chr16 suggests potential hotspots of 540 

recombination or historical selection pressure (Renaut et al. 2014). Lai et al. (2015)Lai et al, 541 

(2014) attributed variable number of SNPs on different chromosome in wheat because of gene 542 

flow and evolution because of domestication and rigorous breeding. 543 

The lower mean heterozygosity rate was anticipated due to self-pollinated nature of groundnut. 544 

However, parallel lower SNPs heterozygosity rate of 0.04 was recently reported in Kirstings 545 

groundnut (Akohoue et al. 2020) suggesting narrow genetic base of the studied population. 546 

These findings agreed with the previous report on groundnut SNPs variation (Bertioli et al. 2019; 547 

Sun et al. 2022). 548 

FastStructure elucidated a total of two major groups which can be further divided into eight sub-549 

populations. On the contrary recent molecular studies on groundnut populations have reported 550 

relatively small genetic structure with two (Bhawar et al. 2020), three (Abady et al. 2021) four or 551 

five sub-populations (Otyama et al. 2020). These studies have examined comparatively less 552 

diverse regional or interrelated populations suggesting that genetic structures get complex as the 553 

size of populations increases. This was further confirmed by (Kafoutchoni et al. 2021) as they 554 

observed eight sub-groups in the population comprising of 217 Kersting’s groundnut genotypes. 555 

The subsequent phylogenetic analysis of the genomic data revealed that the grouping was in 556 

harmony with phenotypic characteristics of the individuals. For example, the first major group 557 

comprising of subgroup-I, II, V and VI was characterized with higher oil content, kernel width 558 

and shelling percentage whereas the second group housed subgroup-III, IV, VII and VIII which 559 

fared low for these traits. The population structure in plants and animals shape the phenotypic 560 

variation and likely the natural selection is a major force driving population differentiation 561 

(Pokrovac and Pezer 2022). 562

LD decay was estimated across population and within subpopulations. The higher LD decay rate 563 

in subpopulation 6 showed that individuals tend to be more homozygous for various alleles, and 564 

individuals in this subpopulation included approved cultivar (BARD-19) and advanced lines 565 

from Pakistan. Conversely the lower LD decay rate in subpopulation 2 consisting of diverse lines 566 

from different countries indicated the somewhat frequent shuffling of the alleles during 567 

recombination. This could potentially be explained by the possibility that the group represents an 568 

admixed and outbred population. LD decay is function of size of population and genomic 569 

regions, hence variable LD decays rates have been reported for different crops by different 570 

studies (Li et al. 2022) (Rafalski and Ananiev 2009). For example, maize shows rapid LD 571 

decay after 5–10 kb, but in certain regions of the genome, LD can last hundreds of kb (Andrade 572 

et al. 2019). This was confirmed in groundnut as they discovered rapid decline in LD with 573 

distance, in the ICRISAT groundnut mini-core collection, with LD dropping below 0.2 at 41.6 kb 574 

(Shaibu et al. 2020). The recent whole-genome resequencing of global groundnut population 575 

showed LD decay 92.3 kb (r 2 ~0.16) (Lu et al. 2024), and LD decay distance was 240 kb in our 576 

diversity panel for r 2=0.3 which is comparable. 577 

Genes underpinning local adaptability 578 

Modern crop cultivars are consequence of artificial selection for yield and quality traits which 579 

resulted in the dramatic improvement of many agronomic traits. However, it has resulted in 580 

narrow genetic diversity generally in food crops. Therefore, it is important to assess the selection 581 

pressure on specific loci to identify the opportunities for future crop breeding. We identified 582 

4525 SNPs within selective sweeps spanning the whole genome. The distribution of sweeps 583 

across A and B genome was almost symmetrical as the number of sweeps for both types was 584 

2348 and 2177, respectively. The highest number of selective sweeps between two groups were 585

pod length, 100 kernel weight, and grain yield co-localized, indicating an important instance of 588 

selective sweep primarily driven by selection pressure aimed at developing high-yielding 589 

varieties over a century-long period (Gangurde et al. 2020). 590

size and growth habit of the groundnut. On chr10, a locus represented by tag SNP 10_113549798 592

is an important plant enzyme playing its role in nitrogen metabolism. Research has confirmed the 672 

significance of AlaAT enzymes in amino acid synthesis pathways, thereby enhancing nitrogen 673

enzymes, including AlaAT2 homologs found in plant peroxisomal glyoxylate aminotransferases, 675

seedling development and resilience to climate extremes (Agrawal et al. 2024). Overall, AlaAT2 680 

plays a critical role in nitrogen metabolism and amino acid synthesis pathways in plants, thereby 681 

influencing plant growth and development. 682
