## Abstract 40 

Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) supports precision medicine in cancer care, but 41 

accurate assessment of missense variant pathogenicity, especially for variants without 42 

established consensus, remains challenging. Various computational tools have been developed 43 

for variant functional prediction, but most current tools rely solely on variant-level features and 44 

do not capture the clinical context of individual patients. To address this limitation, we 45 

developed MARiO (Missense Alteration Risk for Oncogenicity), a machine-learning model that 46 

integrates variant-level features and patient-level clinical and genomic contexts to effectively 47 

predict the pathogenicity of missense variants in cancer. We collected a total of 10,642 missense 48 

variants from 1271 patients, and evaluated candidate features for their association with variant 49 

pathogenicity, identifying informative features including in silico functional predictions, 50 

population allele frequency, variant allele frequency, and tumor mutational burden. Using these 51 

selected features, MARiO was developed with extreme gradient boosting. The model integrates 52 

multiple in silico prediction tools and patient-specific genomic contexts while accommodating 53 

missing values frequently observed in real-world CGP datasets. MARiO outperformed existing 54 

tools, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.942. The model 55 

demonstrated strong generalizability across multiple external datasets and showed consistency 56 

with real-world molecular treatment proposals. MARiO offers a robust and clinically relevant 57 

approach for missense variant pathogenicity assessment by integrating variant- and patient-level 58 

features and serves as a valuable tool to support clinical decision-making. 59

## Introduction 82 

Cancer is a disease driven by alterations in the genome. Therefore, targeting cancer somatic 83 

variants is an effective treatment strategy [1]. For example, patients with lung cancer who have 84 

epidermal growth factor receptor ( EGFR ) mutations have responded to EGFR tyrosine kinase 85 

inhibitors [2], demonstrating the utility of genomically matched treatments. Moreover, advances 86 

in next-generation sequencing have enabled rapid and comprehensive analysis of cancer 87 

genomes. Consequently, comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) tests, which assess multiple 88 

cancer-related genes from patient tumors, have been approved in many countries [3–6]. CGP is 89 

typically used to enable detailed molecular profiling in patients with limited treatment options, 90 

including those with rare cancers or who have exhausted standard therapies. Upon identification 91 

of actionable alterations via CGP, molecular tumor boards (MTBs) can propose matched 92 

treatments, facilitating the targeted therapies or enrollment in clinical trials. Advancements have 93 

been made in such precision oncology strategies, and genomically matched therapies have 94 

demonstrated clinical benefit and improved patient outcomes [7–9]. 95 

96 

CGP is increasingly used in cancer precision medicine to identify actionable variants. However, 97 

in clinical practice, variant interpretation is sometimes challenging, as not all reported missense 98 

variants have established clinical importance. CGP often reports both established variants and 99 

variants of uncertain significance (VUS) [10]. VUS are variants with unclear clinical relevance, 100 

for which currently available evidence is insufficient for definitive clinical interpretation, 101 

making it difficult to determine their relevance to treatment decision-making. Generally, such 102 

interpretations depend on annotations by testing companies and expert opinion within MTBs, 103 

often supported by knowledge bases such as ClinVar [11]. At some institutions, advanced 104 

analyses are conducted by integrating evidence from the literature, recent clinical trials, and 105

expert opinions to refine variant interpretation. However, these processes are resource-intensive 106 

and not scalable across institutions. Thus, the development of standardized strategies for 107 

evaluating missense variants detected by CGP remains an unmet clinical need. 108 

109 

Recent advances in computational tools for predicting variant effects provide a valuable 110 

approach to variant interpretation [12,13]. These in silico methods generate scores that estimate 111 

the functional impact of mutations by leveraging features such as amino acid conservations, 112 

protein structures, and evolutionary constraints. They enable us to evaluate rare or previously 113 

unreported variants, offering an advantage over knowledge base-dependent approaches. 114 

However, these tools have several limitations, including limited coverage of certain protein 115 

regions and domain-dependent variability in predictive performance, owing to the differences in 116 

algorithms, modeling strategies, and training data. Reliance on any single in silico tool could 117 

reduce the robustness of variant interpretation. To address this, several ensemble methods have 118 

been developed and shown to improve predictive performance [14,15]. However, they estimate 119 

a variant’s functional impact rather than predicting its pathogenicity. In practice, clinicians 120 

synthesize information on not only the variants themselves but also clinical parameters. They 121 

also consider variant allele frequency (VAF), mutation type (e.g., somatic or germline), tumor 122 

type, and mutational backgrounds to assess pathogenicity. Existing approaches rely solely on 123 

computational predictions and fail to integrate such patient-level features. Thus, a gap remains, 124 

with the lack of a well-established framework that combines computational functional 125 

predictions with patient-specific clinical features derived from CGP testing .126 

127 

To bridge this gap, we developed MARiO (Missense Alteration Risk for Oncogenicity), a 128 

computational framework that can predict the pathogenicity of cancer missense variants. 129

MARiO integrates in silico variant functional predictions with patient-level genomic features, 130 

thereby bridging molecular functional evaluation and the clinical genomic context (Fig. 1). In 131 

this study, we used real-world CGP datasets to identify informative features, developed a 132 

machine learning model for pathogenicity prediction, and validated its performance across 133 

external datasets. MARiO has three key features. First, the model provides comprehensive and 134 

robust predictions by assembling multiple in silico tools rather than relying on a single model. 135 

Second, MARiO enables variant interpretation that reflects clinical practice by incorporating 136 

patient-specific variables (e.g., VAF). Third, although missing data frequently limit the 137 

evaluation of real-world clinical datasets, MARiO overcomes this challenge by using machine 138 

learning methods that tolerate missingness. Collectively, we present a new modeling framework 139 

with improved performance for evaluating the cancer variant pathogenicity. MARiO is designed 140 

to be accessible to a wide range of users, including non-experts, and has the potential to advance 141 

cancer precision medicine through improved interpretation of cancer missense variants. 142

## Material and methods 152 

153 

Data collection 154 

To prepare a dataset of missense variants, CGP results were retrospectively collected. We 155 

included patients whose CGP results were discussed by the MTB at the Institute of Science 156 

Tokyo (ISCT) Hospital from September 2017 to March 2024. Four CGP assay types were used 157 

in this study: FoundationOne CDx (F1) [3], FoundationOne Liquid CDx [4], OncoGuide NCC 158 

Oncopanel System [5], and Guardant360 CDx [6]. From the CGP results, missense variants 159 

were extracted for subsequent analysis. For liquid biopsy samples, variants in genes potentially 160 

arising from clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) [16] were excluded to 161 

ensure analytical consistency (Method S1). 162 

163 

Feature selection 164 

To identify informative features for our predictive model, we evaluated candidate features based 165 

on their association with variant pathogenicity. The annotations provided by testing laboratories 166 

served as the basis for our binary labels, comprising “known pathogenic variant” and “VUS.” 167 

Following previous studies, we formulated a binary classification task by treating pathogenic 168 

variants as positive and VUS as negative [15,17] (Method S2). We then evaluated the 169 

associations between candidate features and variant pathogenicity to select features for model 170 

development. Candidate features were grouped into two categories: in silico variant functional 171 

prediction features and patient-level genomic features. 172 

173 

in silico variant functional predictions 174

The in silico functional prediction features included scores from the following tools: 175 

AlphaMissense [18], ESM1b [19], EVE [20], PolyPhen2-HVAR [21], SIFT [22] and VEST4 176 

[23]. These tools were selected because they represent either recently developed high-177 

performance methods (AlphaMissense, ESM1b, and EVE) or widely used established tools 178 

(PolyPhen2-HVAR, SIFT, and VEST4). To assess their utility as predictive features for variant 179 

pathogenicity, we evaluated the performance of each tool in distinguishing pathogenic variants 180 

from VUS using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall (PR) curves. 181 

182 

Patient-level genomic features 183 

Candidate genomic features for each patient included VAF, population allele frequency (PAF), 184 

tumor mutational burden (TMB), and microsatellite instability (MSI) status. To examine 185 

whether they were statistically associated with variant pathogenicity, we performed logistic 186 

regression analysis with variant pathogenicity defined as the binary outcome, coding pathogenic 187 

variants as 1 and VUSs as 0. PAF were obtained from the Genome Aggregation Database 188 

(gnomAD) [24], specifically for East Asian populations. PAF and VAF were treated as ratio-189 

scale variables ranging from 0 to 1. TMB was analyzed as log-transformed values, and MSI 190 

status was treated as a categorical variable with three classes: 0 for microsatellite stable, 0.5 for 191 

MSI-low, and 1 for MSI-high. For VAF, polynomial regression was used to flexibly model its 192 

association with variant pathogenicity, as VAF is influenced by multiple factors and is expected 193 

to exhibit a non-linear distribution. Given the complex nature of TMB and VAF, we further 194 

visualized their relationships with variant pathogenicity to facilitate interpretation (Method S3). 195 

Based on these analyses, the feature set was determined for inclusion in the model. 196

Model development 198 

Using the selected features, we developed models for predicting missense variant pathogenicity. 199 

However, among the features, TMB requires careful consideration because it varies across 200 

cancer types and mutated genes [25–27]; therefore, inclusion of raw TMB values may introduce 201 

systematic biases driven by these factors. To mitigate these biases and normalize TMB as model 202 

input, we introduced the “adjusted TMB,” as described below, which is a normalized TMB 203 

value according to cancer type and mutated genes. 204 

205 

Cancer type- and gene-adjusted TMB modeling 206 

To characterize dependence of the TMB on cancer type and mutated genes, we analyzed The 207 

Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), a large-scale database of cancer somatic mutations [28]. 208 

Missense variant data, identified via the Mutect2 pipeline, were obtained from the UCSC Xena 209 

platform [29], and variants located within CGP target genes (Method S4) within 31 TCGA 210 

cohorts (Method S5) were analyzed to determine their impact on TMB. TCGA TMB data were 211 

retrieved from the National Cancer Institute Genomic Data Commons [30,31]. We first 212 

examined the distribution of TMB across cancer types to assess cancer type effects. We also 213 

evaluated whether mutations in specific genes were associated with an increased or decreased 214 

TMB using LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression. 215 

216 

The analyses showed that the TMB is influenced by both cancer type and mutated genes; we 217 

therefore introduced the “adjusted TMB,” defined as the deviation of the observed TMB from 218 

its expected value given a specific cancer type and mutated gene effects. Specifically, we 219 

estimated the expected TMB for each cancer type and mutated gene and used the residual as the 220 

adjusted TMB. 221

We used nested cross-validation (CV) to evaluate model performance. Specifically, an outer 5-248

Development and interpretation of MARiO 257 

Based on the results, we selected the best method for final model construction. Using the best 258 

hyperparameters identified via nested CV, we trained the final model on the entire CGP dataset. 259 

The model, named Missense Alteration Risk for Oncogenicity (MARiO), provides assessment 260 

of missense variant pathogenicity. The optimal decision threshold for binary classification was 261 

determined using the Youden index, based on predictions obtained via 5-fold CV. 262 

263 

To interpret the model, feature contributions to the model’s predictions were evaluated using 264 

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis [35]. An ablation study was performed to 265 

validate the need for the selected feature sets. Analyses were conducted using Python 3.11 with 266 

scikit-learn (v1.5.1), xgboost (v2.0.3), lightgbm (v4.4.0), catboost (v1.2.7), and shap (v0.46.0). 267

Model validation using external databases 269 

The performance of MARiO was validated using three independent external datasets: TCGA 270 

[28], Cancer Hotspots [36,37], and Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center’s Precision 271 

Oncology Knowledge Base (OncoKB) [38,39]. 272 

273 

External validation in TCGA 274 

MARiO, which was trained on CGP data, was evaluated on TCGA dataset. Missense variants in 275 

CGP target genes (Method S4) across 31 TCGA cohorts (Method S5) were analyzed, and a 276 

MARiO score was calculated for each variant. To benchmark MARiO’s performance, its 277 

predictions were compared against the ground-truth from ClinVar, alongside evaluations of the 278 

established in silico tools mentioned previously. For this validation, only variants with 279 

definitive ClinVar labels (i.e., “Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic” as positive and “Benign/Likely 280 

Benign” as negative) were included; variants with conflicting interpretations or those lacking 281 

annotations were excluded to ensure rigorous evaluation. To assess potential cancer type-282 

dependent differences in performance, confusion matrices and additional metrics were 283 

evaluated, stratified by cancer type. 284 

285 

External validation using Cancer Hotspots 286 

Cancer Hotspots is a curated catalog of statistically significant “hotspot mutations” in cancer. 287 

We used its classifications as ground-truth to evaluate the MARiO’s performance. Among 288 

variants registered in Cancer Hotspots, we analyzed those located in CGP-target genes (Method 289 

S5). The distribution of MARiO scores was compared between hotspot and non-hotspot 290 

variants. 291

External validation in OncoKB 293

Model evaluation using real-world treatment-related information 299 

To assess the clinical utility of MARiO, we performed retrospective validation by examining 300 

the relationship between MARiO scores and real-world treatment recommendations. This 301 

information was obtained from MTB discussions of CGP results for patients who underwent F1 302 

testing (2019–2024) at ISCT Hospital. Each variant was classified into one of four labels: (1) an 303 

approved drug was recommended and administered, (2) an investigational drug (e.g., clinical 304 

trials, off-label use, or unapproved drugs in Japan) was recommended and administered, (3) a 305 

drug was recommended but was not administered, or (4) no therapeutic recommendation was 306 

provided. Exclusion criteria were lack of consent to participate in the research, death before 307 

results disclosure, and loss to follow-up. Treatment-related data were analyzed for associations 308 

with MARiO scores. 309

Patient and variant characteristics 316 

From September 2017 to March 2024, 1274 patient cases were discussed by the MTB at ISCT 317 

Hospital. Among them, 1271 patients harbored at least one missense variant. Among them, 318 

1021 patients (80.3%) were tested using F1, 166 patients (13.1%) using FoundationOne Liquid 319 

CDx, 62 patients (4.9%) using NCC Oncopanel System, and 22 patients (1.7%) using 320 

Guardant360 CDx (Table 1). In total, 10,687 missense variants were collected (Tables S1, S2). 321 

Of these, 462 CHIP-suspected variants were excluded, leaving 10,225 variants for subsequent 322 

analyses (Fig. S1). 323

Feature characterization and selection 326 

We examined whether in silico functional prediction scores could predict pathogenic missense 327 

variants. All tools demonstrated the ability to distinguish pathogenic variants from VUS, with 328 

AlphaMissense performing the best; this suggested their utility for pathogenicity assessment 329 

(Fig. 2a). We then assessed the proportion of missing values for each tool (Fig. 2b, Table S3). 330 

Notably, EVE failed to generate scores for approximately half the variants, resulting in a 331 

substantially higher rate of missing values compared with the other tools. Based on these 332 

observations, we selected five in silico prediction tools, excluding EVE, as features to include. 333 

334

TMB were significantly associated with variant pathogenicity (Fig. 2c). Variant pathogenicity 337 

showed a strong inverse association with PAF (OR 1.68 × 10 −98 , 95% 1.54 × 10 −113 –1.84 × 10 −83 , P338

Among these features, the PAF’s importance can be readily explained from a biological 341

VAF exhibited a complex distribution, which differed according to variant type (pathogenic or 345

frequently observed at a VAF of approximately 50%, consistent with a germline-like 347 

distribution. By contrast, pathogenic variants clustered around VAF = purity and VAF = 1/2 348 

purity. Pathogenic variants in oncogenes were accumulated around VAF = 1/2 purity, consistent 349 

with heterozygous activating mutations. However, tumor suppressor gene variants were 350 

accumulated at both VAF = purity and VAF = 1/2 purity. In this case, VAF = purity variant 351 

likely reflects a loss of heterozygosity event, and VAF = 1/2 purity would represent compound 352 

heterozygosity [40] (the mutations in different codons of homologous genes) or multiple 353 

heterozygous aberrations in the same signaling pathway (like BRCAness) [41]. These 354 

observations suggest that VAF harbors biologically meaningful information, but the relationship 355 

is not linear. To model this complex association, we assessed VAF using polynomial regression, 356 

which enables non-linear modeling. This revealed that the second- and third-degree VAF terms 357 

were significantly associated with pathogenicity (Fig. 2e), suggesting a non-linear contribution 358 

of VAF to variant pathogenicity. Collectively, these results indicate that VAF is an informative 359 

feature for prediction, although its effect is non-linear and context dependent. 360

Comparison of machine learning models 393 

Using the adjusted TMB and other features, we compared the three machine-learning models. 394 

XGBoost performed the best, with a mean AUROC of 0.942 (Fig. 3c). All models outperformed 395 

conventional tools. These models, which can inherently handle missing data, have an advantage 396 

over other machine-learning methods that do not tolerate missingness. This is because real-397 

world datasets contain a substantial proportion of missing values (Fig. 3d), so models that can 398 

handle missing data would fail to evaluate approximately one in every several patients. We also 399 

compared these models with other conventional models that cannot handle missing data, on a 400 

complete case (8731 variants). In this setting, gradient boosting methods (XGBoost, LightGBM, 401 

and CatBoost) consistently ranked as the top-performers, followed by other methods such as 402 

random forest (Fig. S2). An ablation study revealed that the base model showed the highest 403 

AUROC among the tested feature sets (Fig. S3). 404

revealed that AlphaMissense and VEST4 contributed strongly and positively to prediction 413 

whereas PAF had a negative impact on the model output. The pathogenicity classification 414 

threshold was set at 0.166, determined by maximizing the Youden index on the ROC curve 415 

(Fig. 3h); this was applied to all subsequent performance evaluations and comparative analyses. 416

Validation on ClinVar labels using TCGA data 420

accuracy and recall (Fig. 4a, 4b) and maintained consistent performance across different cancer 423 

types (Fig. S4). Additionally, MARiO produced fewer false negatives than other tools, leading 424 

to a reduced risk of overlooking truly pathogenic variants, which is important in clinical 425 

settings. These results support MARiO’s generalizability to independent external datasets and 426 

demonstrate its robustness across heterogeneous cancer types. As a supplementary analysis, we 427 

compared MARiO with a model excluding the adjusted TMB; MARiO outperformed this 428 

ablated model, demonstrating the efficacy of the adjusted TMB as a feature (Fig. S5). 429

## Discussion 474 

In this study, we first analyzed real-world CGP data and explored features to predict 475 

pathogenicity of missense variants and identified in silico functional predictions and genomic 476 

features related to variant pathogenicity. Based on these findings, we developed a new variant 477 

> (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 10, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.07.736919 doi: bioRxiv preprint

22 

pathogenicity prediction model, MARiO, by integrating in silico functional predictions and 478 

genomic features with machine learning. This model significantly outperformed conventional 479 

tools, showed generalizability to external datasets, and demonstrating its clinical utility in 480 

treatment proposals. 481 

482 

MARiO integrates several features that capture distinct biological perspectives. First, the 483 

contribution of in silico functional predictions suggests the effectiveness of computational 484 

approaches in assessing the variant-level functional impact. Second, the negative contribution of 485 

PAF was observed, which could be explained by purifying selection. Third, VAF could provide 486 

complementary information from genomic perspectives. As described in the results, VAF was 487 

differentially distributed depending on whether variants were germline or somatic, oncogenic or 488 

tumor-suppressive, and pathogenic or non-pathogenic. These observations indicate that VAF 489 

encodes biologically meaningful information about the role of variants in cancer. Furthermore, 490 

VAF may reflect clonal dominance within tumors. Driver mutations are typically subject to 491 

positive selection during oncogenesis and therefore tend to become clonal events with higher 492 

VAF. Consequently, VAF reflects the functional relevance of a variant within the tumor’s 493 

evolutionary context. Finally, our results revealed the contribution of TMB. In TMB-high cases, 494 

numerous passenger mutations reduce the relative contribution of any single variant to tumor. 495 

By contrast, TMB-low tumors are often driven by a limited number of strong driver mutations, 496 

so individual variants may exert a greater biological impact. Interestingly, the presence of 497 

mutations in IDH1/2 was associated with lower TMB; this trend has been reported in previous 498 

studies [26,42,43]. Although further study is needed to explore the reasons, one possible 499 

explanation is that these potent driver mutations can promote oncogenesis with minimal 500 

additional mutational burden. By contrast, several patterns were observed among genes 501 

> (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 10, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.07.736919 doi: bioRxiv preprint

23 

associated with TMB-high tumors. One group includes genes known to drive hypermutation, 502 

such as POLE and POLD1 . Another pattern is characterized by an accumulation of variants in 503 

genes frequently classified as VUS, such as SPEN , TRRAP , and KMT2D . Notably, these are all 504 

exceptionally large genes, which are prone to accumulating random passenger mutations with 505 

increased TMB, thereby inflating the total variant count without necessarily increasing 506 

pathogenic drive. This inverse relationship between TMB and individual variant significance 507 

could explain the contribution of TMB in our model. 508 

509 

In model development, we introduced the adjusted TMB to account for cancer type- and gene-510 

specific effects on TMB. For example, cancers such as melanoma and non-small cell lung 511 

cancer are known to exhibit a globally elevated TMB at the cohort level, and mutations in DNA 512 

replication and repair-associated genes, like POLE , are well established as dramatically 513 

increasing the TMB [44]. By adjusting for these background effects, the model can use the 514 

mutational context in a biologically meaningful way, with TMB providing information beyond 515 

cancer type- and gene-specific background effects. 516 

517 

In validation analysis using Cancer Hotspots, MARiO scores were generally higher for hotspot 518 

variants, with APC being an exception. This likely reflects APC ’s unique molecular patterns, 519 

where nonsense variants are more prevalent than missense variants [45,46]. Such gene-specific 520 

distributions of variant types intrinsically influence the model’s predictive performance. 521 

522 

This study has several limitations. The first is data bias because all data were from patients with 523 

cancer, which potentially introduces selection bias. Additionally, Japanese insurance coverage 524

for CGP is restricted to patients with no remaining standard therapies or rare cancers, so most 525 

variants are derived from late-stage cancers. This could introduce molecular patterns that may 526 

differ from those in early-stage cancers, suggesting that the current model may not fit cancers in 527 

the early stage. Additionally, potential biases may exist within the datasets used for training. We 528 

used gnomAD SNP data as the PAF reference, but SNP patterns vary across populations. Model 529 

retraining with appropriate data could help address these issues. 530 

531 

Another limitation lies in the ground-truth labels used in this study. We relied on the company’s 532 

annotations and used them as the ground truth, but they may not always be definitive. For 533 

instance, variants classified as VUS could be pathogenic but remain unconfirmed owing to a 534 

lack of experimental validation. Therefore, using more appropriate ground truth could 535 

contribute to improving the model. 536 

537 

Finally, this study was limited to retrospective validation. Prospective validation is desirable to 538 

assess the clinical impact of our framework. For example, MARiO may predict some variants 539 

classified as VUS in existing databases as being pathogenic, and by confirming that such 540 

variants are truly oncogenic, we would further support our method. Indeed, we experienced a 541 

case in which only VUS and no established pathogenic variants were reported in CGP, but one 542 

of these VUS was predicted to be pathogenic by MARiO. In this case, a matched drug was 543 

administered for the predicted pathogenic variant, and tumor shrinkage was observed. We are 544 

currently accumulating such cases, and these findings will be reported in future case reports. 545
