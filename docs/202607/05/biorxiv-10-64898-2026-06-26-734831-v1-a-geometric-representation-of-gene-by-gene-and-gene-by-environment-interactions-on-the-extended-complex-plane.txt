Abstract 14 

The relationship between  genotypic  and phenotypic variation is determined by the  complex 15 

interaction  of  genetic and environmental factors. While statistical methods capable of 16 

detecting  such  interactions  exist , an axiomatic mathematical framework that seamlessly 17 

describes the  combined  effects of genetic modifications and environmental exposures on a 18 

common scale is lacking. In this report, buffering concepts are used to construct a19 

measurement system that enables the geometric representation of  both  gene -by -gene  and 20 

gene -by -environment  interactions on the extended complex plane (i.e., as projections on the 21 

Riemann sphere). In this manner , any such interaction , or combination thereof , can be 22 

precisely defined and quantified as the deviation from the neutral value calculated through the 23 

applicable  complex  transformation. When  thus conceptualized , the framework’s 24 

parameterization defines the “ state  space“ of a given measurable phenotype  along both the 25 

real and imaginary dimensions , thus  establishing  an unambiguous and broadly applicable 26 

method for  determin ing  the phenotypic value expected  upon  combinatorial  changes in genetic 27 

and/or environmental variables.  Remarkably , b y applying these methods, it is possible to 28 

quantify the  effects of  any  gene -by -environment  interaction  using  the equation, 29

Introduction 33 

The discipline of  genetics  has long strived to develop the analytical tools necessary to 34 

systematically  and accurately  describe  the relationship between genotypic and phenotypic 35 

variation [1 –6] . The significance of  these  tools lies , not only  in their potential  to  aid  in the 36 

genetic analysis of disease phenotypes,  but also  in  how they  might  be applied to better 37 

understand the mechanisms driving phenotypic diversity in populations (and thus the 38 

evolutionary history of living organisms). Three factors are  thought to be  key in limiting 39 

progress towards this goal : 1)  the effects of  interaction s between  gene s, 2)  the effects of 40 

interaction s between genes and  changing  environment s, and 3)  the effects of  stochastic noise. 41 

While  the  underlying  thermodynamic basis  of cellular biochemistry makes the  effects  of noise 42 

unavoidable , an improved methodology for  the  unambiguous  characteriz ation of  the 43 

combinatorial effects of  gene -by -gene  (GxG) and  gene -by -environment  (GxE) interactions 44 

may be a critical step  in the evolution of fully quantitative methods of genetic analysis. 45 

A necessary prerequisite for the quantitative analysis of  GxG interactions is the establishment 46 

of a set of principles that can be used to defin e the “neutral” phenotype (i.e., the phenotype 47 

predicted to occur in the absence of interaction ). While an interaction between two genes can 48 

indeed be defined and measured as the deviation from th is neutral expectation , the notion of 49 

neutral requires an a priori hypothesi s as to how its value should be calculated . Thus, even 50 

though a variety of neutrality models have been developed,  the ir  validity , together with  the 51 

circumstances under which  each  should be applied remains  equivocal  [7 –9] .52 

The analysis of G xE interactions , on the other hand, is typically based upon the definition of 53 

C. H. Waddington, who reserved the use of the term , g ene -environment interaction , to 54 

“designate cases in which phenotypic effects of different genotypes are differently affected 55 

by a given environmental change”  [10] . In practice , such interactions are determined by 56

comparing the “reaction norms ” of individual genotypes. A reaction norm describes the 57 

phenotype of a single genotype across a range of environments. Thus, G xE interactions can 58 

be inferred when the phenotypic response of two genotypes to the same environmental 59 

variation statistically differs  [4,11 –13] . Although conceptually simple, difficulties in defining 60 

and interpreting G xE interactions nonetheless exist. For example, using the previously 61 

described statistical definition, the inference of an interaction depend s on whether an additive 62 

or multiplicative scale of measurement is used  [13] . Thus, while the analysis of both G xG and 63 

GxE interactions have a long and rich history, ambiguities with respect to how each should be 64 

defined and/or conceptualized have not been resolved  [9,12 –14] . Furthermore, attempts at 65 

consolidating the analysis of G xG and G xE interactions within a common schema remain for 66 

the most part unexplored. 67 

In this report , I present a harmonized mathematical framework capable of seamlessly 68 

describing the effects of genetic modifications and environmental exposures on a common 69 

scale. As will be described in detail below, I contend that the use o f complex numbers , together 70 

with the parameterization previously developed in  [15] , permit s the unambiguous 71 

representation of both GxG and G xE interactions on the extended complex plane (i.e., as 72 

projections on the Riemann sphere ). In this way, all relevant genetic and environmental 73 

elements of the system are represented by intuitive, yet precise, geometric equivalent s, thereby 74 

establish ing a comprehensive and broadly applicable method for the quantitative analysis of 75 

both G xG and G xE interactions .76

A mathematical  representation  of  the buffering parameters using complex numbers 196 

While possible to view and manipulate  the elements of the current framework  using  only  real 197 

numbers  (ℝ), they are more easily dealt with mathematically when conceptualized as complex 198 

numbers  (ℂ) on the Argand plane . Essentially, since complex numbers form a field (in the 199 

mathematical sense of the term)  they provide a powerful means by which to represent rotations 200 

and scaling within two -dimensional space,  thereby simplifying calculations and  allowing the 201 

use of well -developed  methods for the ir visualization and analysis  [19 –22] . As will be shown 202 

in subsequent sections, these tools  become  necessary for further developing the framework  so 203 

that it is able to  encompass the characterization of G xE interactions.  Thus, in the same way 204 

that  complex numbers  are  used to  aid in  the analysis of systems  in physics and engineering, 205 

they  too can  be used to aid in the analysis of genetic systems . However, before th ese 206 

extensions  can be  properly  explored , it is necessary  for the framework to be conceptualized 207

Discussion 478 

The ability to measure  – i.e., the ability to describe and communicate the attributes of natural 479 

phenomen a through assigning constrained numerical values to associated objects or processes 480 

– is essential to the scientific  method . In its absence, there is no mechanism by which to 481 

translate the natural world into unambiguous, comparable, and communicable descriptions 482 

and thus no simple way to uncover patterns, define rules,  test hypotheses,  or ultimately 483 

develop comprehensive theories. The assigned numerical values are said to be constrained 484 

since they are determined through the application of distinct rule  sets  as described by Stevens 485 

[24] . These rules vary depending on the “resolution“ needed and manifest themselves as the 486 

scale of measurement  that emerges from their application .487 

The simplest  class of  scale, referred to as  the  “nominal“  class , resolves  the  equality  of values 488 

and is  thus  only  capable of  categorization  (Table 2 ). A more sophisticated class of scale, 489 

referred to as  the  “ordinal“  class , resolves whether a given value is greater than, or lesser than 490 

another, allowing it to  rank, in addition to  categorize . However, t he  size of the intervals 491 

between  values  in these scales  are not known , making  it impossible to  determine  the equality 492 

of  difference s. For this  attribute , one must utilize the  “interval“  class  of scales , which  does 493 

indeed exhibit equal intervals and  can thus  be used to  resolve  such  differences . Interval  scales, 494 

though , lack a  “true “ zero in the sense that  the zero  value  that is  used  act s only as  an arbitrary 495 

reference  point  and do es  not indicate the complete absence of the thing being measured.  In 496

Defining a “relational“ scale of measurement 520 

When viewed through the lens  provided by  Stevens  (summarized  in the previous section ), the 521

their meaning in the face of any one -to -one substitution  of values . Ordinal scales , on the other 541 

hand,  retain their meaning in the face of any transformation that preserves the original order 542 

of values . Interval scales retain their meaning in the face of any transformation that shifts or 543 

scales (i.e., transformations defined by  x‘= ax+b).  In contrast, r atio scales retain their meaning 544
