ABSTRACT 9

> 10

Probabilistic prognosis of the evolution of population requires the knowledge of the fitness 11 

effects of mutations at different genomic sites. However, the signature of natural selection is 12 

eclipsed by strong noise in data, because the common ancestors of different sites render their 13 

evolution inter-dependent. Together with mutation and recombination, genetic linkage also 14 

makes evolution stochastic and requires averaging over many independent populations. Here 15 

we develop a method designed to work in the presence of strong linkage effects. For testing, 16 

we apply it to simulated genomic sequences generated by a Monte Carlo algorithm with 17 

known selection coefficients. Results demonstrate the good accuracy of the estimates of 18 

relative selection coefficients, if more than ten independent populations are used for 19 

averaging. Infrequent recombination partly compensates linkage effects and improves the 20 

accuracy. The method is then used to estimate the selection coefficients of 10,000 genomic 21 

sites of E. coli . These findings enable the inference of adaptive landscape under the 22 

conditions of strong multi-site linkage. 23

AUTHOR SUMMARY 30 

> 31

Probabilistic prognosis of evolution of an organism requires the knowledge of fitness effects 32 

of mutations at different genomic sites. However, genetic linkage between different sites due 33 

to their common phylogenetic history obscures the effects of natural selection. Here we 34 

develop a linkage-resistant method to infer fitness effects and test its accuracy on mock 35 

sequences generated by an evolutionary algorithm with known fitness effects. Results 36 

demonstrate fair accuracy when averaging is performed over ten or more independently-37 

evolving populations. After application of this program to genomic data for E. coli , relative 38 

selection coefficients for thousands of genomic sites are obtained. 39

INTRODUCTION 41 

> 42

Evolutionary dynamics of a population of DNA sequences is controlled by the 43 

interplay of various evolutionary factors, including random mutation, natural selection, 44 

genetic drift, and recombination. Of central interest is adaptation to a new environment, 45 

which occurs due to emergence of rare mutations that increase the fitness of the organism 46 

(average progeny number). Deleterious mutations emerge during adaptation as well. The 47 

advantage of each favorable or deleterious mutation is measured by the relative change it 48 

causes in fitness. Thus, the knowledge of fitness effects for different mutations is essential for 49 

predicting the evolutionary trajectory. 50 

Measuring fitness effects of mutations directly is possible only in some cases, for 51 

example, in viruses and bacteria, but even then it requires rather elaborate experiments [1-5]. 52 

Therefore, a great effort has been invested in their estimation from genomic data using 53 

simplifying models. The average fitness effect of a beneficial mutation in HIV genome was 54 

estimated using genetic samples from infected patients and an one-site model [6]. Selection 55 

coefficients in the hemagglutinin gene of influenza A H3N2 were estimated by fitting the 56 

predictions of one-site and two-site models to the time-series sequence data on the 57 

hemagglutinin gene of influenza virus A H3N2 [7]. Another group [8] proposed a method of 58 

estimating selection coefficients of deleterious mutations based on an assumption about the 59 

form of fitness effect distribution among loci. In SARS-CoV-2, fitness effects of mutations 60 

were computed as the natural logarithm of the ratio of their actual to expected counts, with a 61 

correction for the codon founder effect [9]. One-locus Wright-Fisher model was used to 62 

estimate selection coefficients from genomic data for Sindbis virus [10]. 63 

A major complication in predicting the evolutionary trajectory and estimating the 64 

fitness effects of mutations is that the evolution of different sites is coupled due to their 65 

common phylogeny. Because different sites share ancestors, genetic linkage effects between 66 

them arise, including clonal interference and the genetic background effect [11]. Linkage 67 

limits the use of one-site and two-site models for the inference of observable properties, and a 68 

more complex framework taking into account a large number of genomic sites must be used 69 

[12, 13]. The multi-site linkage modifies all evolutionary observables, from the evolution rate 70 

and fitness distribution[12, 14] to phylogenetic properties [15-17]. Recombination 71 

compensates linkage effects, but only partly [15, 18-20]. The multi–site models have been 72 

successfully applied to predict evolutionary parameters of several pathogenic viruses, 73 

including HIV [21] and influenza [22-26]. 74

Of great practical importance is the ability to predict virus evolution, in the 75 

probabilistic sense, with the goal of optimizing vaccines and antiviral drugs [27], with the 76 

caveat that vaccines can expedite the rate of virus evolution [28]. Based on multi-site models, 77 

methods of predicting the fitness of whole virus strains from their position on the 78 

phylogenetic trees [29] and from the immune memory of extinct strains [24, 30] have been 79 

developed. In addition to whole strains, the evolution of specific genomic sites represents 80 

biomedical interest as well, which brings us back to estimating their selection coefficients. 81 

A recent modeling work [31] demonstrated that, when many genomic sites adapt 82 

slowly, alleles are distributed among genomes and sites to maximize disorder at any one time. 83 

The average evolutionary dynamics of separate sites was mapped onto an one-site model, 84 

where time is effectively slowed down by a factor, the same for all sites [32, 33]. These 85 

findings allowed to develop a method estimating the relative fitness effects of mutations from 86 

the evolutionary dynamics, without neglecting linkage effects [33]. 87 

In the present work, we test the accuracy of this method [32, 33] on simulated 88 

sequences with known fitness effects of mutations. Then, the method is applied to the 89 

genomic data on Escherichia coli [34]. 90

Linkage-proof method to estimate the fitness effect of mutation 94 

We determined the relative influence of individual mutations on fitness (selection coefficient) 95 

by using a time series of allelic frequencies at many sites and averaging them across 96 

independently-evolving populations. Genomic samples were collected at multiple time points 97 

from several independent populations. All genomes were aligned, and the consensus of the 98 

entire dataset was found. At each polymorphic site and each time point, the frequency of 99 

minority allele was calculated. The relative value of the selection coefficient was estimated 100 

for each site, using a procedure described in Methods. 101

DISCUSSION 172 

> 173

The present work offers a software package implementing an algorithm for estimating 174 

relative selection coefficients from genomic data despite the presence of strong linkage 175 

effects caused by common phylogeny. A test on simulated data demonstrates good accuracy, 176 

if ten or more populations are used for averaging. Then the method is used to estimate 177 

selection coefficients for 10,000  evolving sites of E. Coli. 178 

The method relies on averaging over multiple independently-evolving populations. 179 

This is not a limitation of this specific method of measurement, but a fundamental limitation 180 

arising from the stochastic effects of genetic linkage due to the common phylogeny of 181 

different sites. Examples of systems where such data can be obtained include various 182 

microbiological experiments in multiple serial passages. Another example is the evolution of 183 

different metastases in a patient. When addressing human evolution, it is advisable to 184 

compare DNA samples of diverged populations from different geographic locations that 185 

evolved under similar conditions. 186

It has been known for a long time that the evolution of a population is strongly 192 

affected by the fact that the fates of alleles at different loci are linked unless separated by 193 

recombination [11]. Effects of genetic linkage include clonal interference [11, 35], 194 

background selection, genetic hitchhiking [36], enhanced accumulation of deleterious 195 

mutations (Muller's ratchet) [12, 37], and the increase of genetic drift at one locus due to 196 

selection at another [38]. Genetic linkage also decreases the speed of adaptation due to clonal 197 

interference [12, 39]. For this reason, one-locus models usually do not apply in real 198 

populations, and multi-locus models based on traveling wave methods must be used [13, 40]. 199 

Importantly, genetic linkage makes evolutionary dynamics at different sites stochastic and 200 

interdependent, which is a serious obstacle to the inference of fitness landscape from genetic 201 

data [41 ]. 202

To fill this gap, a method based on the approximation of quasi-equilibrium was 207 

recently proposed [32, 33]. The cited work demonstrated that the entropy of the distribution 208 

of alleles across genomes and sites is maximal conditioned on the value of average 209 

population fitness. This approximation, formulated for multi-site adaptation [12], was tested 210 

by Monte Carlo simulation [31-33]. The present work is the first application of this method to 211 

real genomic data. 212 

After successfully applying to data on E. Coli , we also made an attempt to apply this 213 

method to data on SARS-CoV-2 and HIV. We found out, however, that the method works 214 

only for the systems with a unique best-fit sequence. The method requires averaging allelic 215 

frequencies over an ensemble of independently-evolving populations with the same or similar 216

Indeed, the best-fit sequence for HIV varies between individuals due to the variation of HLA 218

of generalization of this approach to the system with multiple best-fit sequences remain 223

Other groups developed a number of methods to estimate selection coefficients 225 

neglecting linkage effects. In one work, selection coefficient was estimated from a hidden 226 

Markov chain assuming a single-locus model[42]. Another team [43] developed a method for 227 

measuring the selection coefficient considering a single locus with two alleles and using data 228 

on time series. In their model, backward Kolmogorov equation is used to describe transition 229 

probabilities. Another group [7] presented a method for detecting selection signature from 230 

population history, by maximizing a likelihood function derived from a two-locus 231 

evolutionary model. A likelihood estimation method was used to jointly estimate the 232 

selection coefficient and the allele age based on time series data [44]. All these methods 233 

neglected linkage effects. 234 

Note that the proportion of beneficial mutations predicted is higher than previously 235 

estimated for E. coli [45, 46]. The proportion of beneficial alleles is not a universal property 236 

of the organism. It varies broadly depending on the state of a population (i.e., time of 237 

evolution) and external conditions. Hence the probable reason for discrepancy is that the 238 

cited authors propagate E. Coli. under different conditions than in [34] whose data we used 239 

here. The proportion of deleterious alleles decreases in the process of evolution. If an 240 

organism is well–evolved, their deleterious alleles are very rare. Also, the previous method of 241 

estimating   is based on a 1-locus model. 242

Conclusions. Using a method developed to withstand linkage effects, we estimate the relative 244 

values of selection coefficient for several thousand sites of the evolving E. Coli. genome. 245 

These results demonstrate the feasibility of the measurements of selection coefficients from 246 

genomic data in populations evolving under directional selection in the presence of strong 247 

linkage effects. 248

the distribution of alleles across sites and genomes to reach the state statistical quasi-316 

equilibrium at any one time. 317
