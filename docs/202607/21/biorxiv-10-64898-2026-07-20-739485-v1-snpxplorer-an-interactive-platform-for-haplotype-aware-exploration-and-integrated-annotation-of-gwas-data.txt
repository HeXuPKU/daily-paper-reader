Abstract 12 

Background 13 

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of loci associated with complex traits 14 

and diseases, yet translating these signals into biological insight remains challenging. Most associated variants 15 

are non-coding and reside in linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks, where multiple correlated variants jointly 16 

contribute to association signals. These clusters, or haplotypes, may capture shared regulatory and functional 17 

contexts. Interpreting GWAS signals thus requires approaches that integrate regulatory, functional, and cross-18 

trait evidence, while preserving the broader haplotypic context of disease-associated loci. At the same time, 19 

the rapid growth of publicly available GWAS summary statistics has enabled large-scale cross-trait analyses, 20 

but also introduced redundancy across closely related phenotypes. Efficient interpretation of GWAS data 21 

therefore requires tools that integrate heterogeneous data sources while preserving genomic and biological 22 

contexts. 23 

Results 24 

We present snpXplorer, an interactive web platform for haplotype-aware exploration and annotation of GWAS 25 

data. The platform incorporates >10,000 GWAS datasets from OpenGWAS and enables multi-scale analysis 26 

across variants, haplotypes, genes, and traits. Key features include ( i) a haplotype-based representation of 27 

association signals derived from LD structure, ( ii ) a unified variant annotation framework integrating clinical 28 

annotations (ClinVar), allele frequencies (gnomAD), functional predictions (CADD, AlphaGenome), quantitative 29 

trait loci (GTEx), structural variation, and GWAS associations, and ( iii ) cross-trait exploration using semantic 30 

similarity-based clustering of phenotypes. Use cases centered on Alzheimer’s disease illustrate this utility: for 31 

example, at the TMEM106B locus, snpXplorer identified a haplotype linked to eleven distinct traits, revealing 32 

synergistic pleiotropy across neurological and behavioral phenotypes alongside antagonistic pleiotropy with 33 

height. 34 

Conclusions 35 

snpXplorer allows users to browse, filter, and inspect variant-, haplotype-, gene- and trait-level evidence, 36 

lowering the barrier to biological interpretation of GWAS results. Compared with existing tools that focus on 37 

specific aspects of GWAS interpretation, the strength of snpXplorer is that it reduces the need for fragmented 38 

queries across databases. 39

## 43 

Background 44 

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of genetic loci associated with complex 45 

traits and disease. However, translating association signals into biological insight remains challenging. Most 46 

Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNP) identified through GWAS lie in non-coding regions of the human 47 

genome, and often consist of multiple correlated variants in linkage disequilibrium (LD). These clusters of 48 

linked variants, termed haplotypes, may represent more biologically relevant units than single variants alone, 49 

as they capture the combined effects of multiple variants and may help identify the genes likely affected by the 50 

haplotype. In addition to SNPs, disease-associated haplotypes may also contain larger structural variants, 51 

such as transposable elements or tandem repeats, which can contribute to regulatory and disease 52 

mechanisms.[1,2] 53 

## 54 

At the same time, the rapid expansion of publicly available GWAS summary statistics has enabled large-scale 55 

cross-trait analyses, but has also introduced substantial redundancy, as closely related phenotypes frequently 56 

share overlapping association signals. An efficient exploration of these resources therefore requires tools that 57 

integrate information across studies while reducing complexity and preserving biological context. 58 

## 59 

In 2021, we introduced snpXplorer [3], a web server designed to support regional visualization and functional 60 

annotation of SNP association data. Since its initial release, snpXplorer has been widely adopted by the 61 

genetics community. Motivated by user feedback, advances in large-scale GWAS resources and variant 62 

annotation databases, as well as the increasing relevance of haplotype-based analyses, we have developed 63 

a substantially updated version of the platform. Compared with the original 2021 release, the updated 64 

snpXplorer extends beyond regional SNP visualization and batch annotation to support large-scale cross-trait 65 

GWAS exploration, haplotype-level aggregation of association signals, integrated single-variant interpretation, 66 

semantic phenotype clustering, and PRS-oriented workflows. These additions reposition snpXplorer from a 67 

regional annotation server toward a broader framework for interactive interpretation of complex disease 68 

genetics. 69 

## 70 

Here, we describe the design and functionality of the snpXplorer web server and demonstrate its utility through 71 

a set of use cases centered on the exploration of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) genetics. We illustrate trait-centric, 72 

gene-centric, and variant-centric workflows, highlighting how snpXplorer enables interactive, scalable, and 73 

biologically informed exploration and annotation of GWAS data. 74

## Implementation 77 

Overview 78 

SnpXplorer is an integrated, interactive platform for GWAS exploration and annotation ( Figure 1 ), comprising 79 

different modules that enable real-time, point-and-click analysis across variants, haplotypes, genes, and traits. 80

## 81 

GWAS exploration 82 

The original GWAS exploration module supported interactive visualization of a limited set of GWAS association 83 

signals across genomic regions of interest. We have now integrated the IEU OpenGWAS database [4,5], 84 

enabling users to browse and overlay association signals from over 10,000 GWAS studies without uploading 85 

local summary statistics. Users can visualize multiple traits simultaneously, explore LD patterns, inspect 86 

recombination rates and display structural variants [6] ( Figure S1 ). 87 

## 88 

Batch annotation 89 

The Batch annotation module annotates up to 10,000 variants to genes integrating clinical evidence from 90 

ClinVar [7], genomic position from RefSeq [8], quantitative trait loci (QTLs) from GTEx [9], and functional 91 

predictions from CADD [10] and AlphaGenome [11]. Cross-ancestry allele frequencies from gnomAD [12] as 92 

well as phenotype associations from OpenGWAS are also reported. Optionally, gene-set enrichment analysis 93 

can be performed with gProfiler [13]. Gene-set enrichment analysis is followed by clustering of significant terms 94 

based on semantic similarity, and clusters are annotated using word clouds to provide functional context 95 

(Figure S2 ). To provide haplotype context and improve variant annotation, snpXplorer now incorporates the 96 

annotation of variants in LD with the input variants. For example, an intergenic variant in LD with a coding 97 

variant can be linked to the affected gene through the shared haplotype. Batch annotation jobs can be 98 

submitted along with an email address, and results are sent to the user upon completion. 99 

## 100 

SNP annotation 101 

An important new component of snpXplorer is the SNP annotation module, which provides a unified interface 102 

for querying individual variants. The annotation engine is the same as in the Batch annotation module. For a 103 

given variant, snpXplorer returns allele information, population frequencies, predicted functional impact, 104 

nearby affected genes, QTL associations and previously reported associations with phenotypes from GWAS 105 

(Figure S3 ). Annotations are also extended to variants in LD (R 2>0.6) with the query variant, often improving 106 

the identification of the candidate affected gene(s). By aggregating heterogeneous sources of evidence into a 107 

single view, this module reduces the need for manual cross-referencing across databases and facilitates rapid 108 

variant interpretation, supported by interactive tables, graphs and cross-references, and with the possibility to 109 

download all content. 110 

## 111 

Haplotype exploration 112 

The Haplotype module is a new component of snpXplorer. It enables users to explore GWAS signals at the 113 

haplotype level. Haplotypes are precalculated in a two-step procedure. The genome is first split in subregions 114 

based on recombination rates (downloaded from UCSC genome browser, threshold=25) [14,15]. Then, 115 

pairwise LD matrices are calculated for all variants in each subregion, using genotyping data from 4,777 116 

unrelated and European individuals (an in-depth description of these individuals is available elsewhere [16]). 117 

Variants are finally clustered into haplotypes by complete-linkage hierarchical clustering, using LD as distance 118 

measure, with clusters designed to ensure all pairwise LD values are >0.1. Haplotypes can be queried by 119 

gene, or by individual variant. The platform supports interactive inspection of haplotype composition, LD 120 

structure, and cross-trait associations, and all resulting tables and graphs can be downloaded. 121

## 122 

Cross-trait exploration 123 

In addition to supporting haplotypes, the Cross-trait exploration module allows users to browse phenotypes 124 

across large GWAS repositories. To reduce redundancy among closely related traits, semantic similarity 125 

between phenotype descriptions (e.g. GWAS of Alzheimer’s Disease ) is approximated using pretrained 126 

biomedical language embeddings (BioLORD-2023, SapBERT). Embeddings produced by each model were 127 

independently L2-normalised and combined using weighted concatenation, with weights of 0.70 for BioLORD-128 

2023 and 0.30 for SapBERT. Pairwise cosine distances between trait embeddings were computed and used 129 

as input for agglomerative hierarchical clustering with average linkage. Clusters are defined by applying a 130 

cosine distance threshold of 0.5, such that traits within a cluster exhibit high semantic proximity. Singleton traits 131 

were reassigned to their nearest non-singleton cluster when cosine similarity was at least 0.4. For each cluster, 132 

a representative study is selected based on study characteristics such as sample size, data availability, and 133 

data completeness. To identify neighboring clusters, inter-cluster cosine distances are calculated based on the 134 

average pairwise distance between traits across clusters. When users query a phenotype of interest, an 135 

interactive UMAP showing the cluster containing the phenotype of interest and related phenotypes, as well as 136 

the five closest clusters are shown, along with the cosine similarity heatmap between all phenotypes. This 137 

strategy is intended to facilitate scalable navigation across large GWAS repositories rather than replace formal 138 

genetic-correlation analyses. 139 

## 140 

PRS module 141 

snpXplorer now directly supports Polygenic Risk Score (PRS) generation through the new PRS module. PRSs 142 

are weighted scores quantifying the individuals’ genetic risk for a given phenotype. As such, their calculation 143 

requires a set of variants to include in the PRS along with the effect size from a GWAS, and individuals’ 144 

genotyping data. Because sharing or uploading of individual genotyping data is limited by privacy regulations, 145 

this cannot be done in snpXplorer directly. However, for any trait from the OpenGWAS database, users can 146 

interactively select and download pre-calculated variant sets, at the p-value threshold of interest, to generate 147 

a PRS. Variant sets are derived from curated trait definitions or automated clumping-and-thresholding 148 

procedures (LD R 2=0.05, window=500 kbp). These outputs are designed for compatibility with PRS tools such 149 

as Jordan (https://github.com/TesiNicco/jordan ) and PolyGenius (https://polygenius.holstegelab.eu). 150

## Results 152 

Trait-centric haplotype discovery for Alzheimer’s disease 153 

To illustrate the trait-centric haplotype exploration, we queried Alzheimer’s disease (AD) within the Haplotype 154 

module of snpXplorer. The platform retrieved 18 AD-related GWAS studies from OpenGWAS ( Table S1 ). These 155 

included GWAS explicitly targeting AD as well as closely related phenotypes, such as maternal or paternal 156 

history of Alzheimer’s disease. Semantic similarity-based clustering grouped these traits together, and 157 

identified neighboring clusters associated with Parkinson’s disease (gold cluster), ADHD, autism spectrum 158 

disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder (green cluster), multiple sclerosis (cyan cluster), diseases of the 159 

nervous system and neuroticism (orange cluster), and macular degeneration (blue cluster) ( Figure 2A-B ). This 160 

similarity map highlights conceptual relationships mostly among neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric 161 

disorders. 162

## 163 

In the representative AD GWAS selected [17], snpXplorer first identified 5,505 individual variants reaching 164 

genome-wide significance (p<5x10 -8 , Table S2 ). These significant variants collectively map to 227 haplotypes 165 

based on LD structure in European individuals ( Figure 2C and Table S3 ). Interactive tables and plots enable 166 

the inspection of haplotype composition and the underlying variant associations, and all results can be 167 

downloaded for further investigation. This use case demonstrates how trait-centric haplotype analysis enables 168 

efficient summarization and interpretation of complex GWAS signals for a disease of interest. 169 

## 170 

Gene-centric cross-trait exploration of haplotypes at the TMEM106B locus 171 

To showcase the gene-centric, cross-trait haplotype exploration, we focused on the TMEM106B locus, a gene 172 

associated with Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, and other aging-related phenotypes based on 173 

GWAS [1]. At the TMEM106B locus, snpXplorer identified twenty-two common haplotypes overlapping the 174 

gene region, with sizes ranging 35-345 kbp (mean=182 kbp, Figure 3A-B and Table S4 ). Several haplotypes 175 

exhibited distinct cross-trait association profiles, ranging from broad to highly specific patterns of phenotypic 176 

association ( Figure 3C ). For example, haplotype B (7:12194394-12246599, Figure 3A-B-C ) spans ~52 kb, 177 

comprises 24 variants (LD structure shown in Figure 3D ), and was associated with 11 distinct traits, primarily 178 

related to neurological and behavioral phenotypes (neuroticism, stress, depression, sleep duration, Figure 3E ). 179 

This illustrates synergistic pleiotropic effects on nerve-related issues (ukb-b-6991, ukb-b-5664), neuroticism 180 

(ukb-a-230), depression (ukb-b-6991, ebi-a-GCST006475), mood swings (ebi-a-GCST006944) and sleep 181 

duration (ukb-b-4424), while antagonistic pleiotropy is reported on height (ukb-b-10787) ( Table S4 ). Another 182 

haplotype (haplotype A, 7:12207704-12246424) of ~38 kb in size contained 120 variants and was associated 183 

with 28 traits spanning neurological (Alzheimer’s disease, neuroticism, depression), metabolic 184 

(gastroesophageal disease, cholesterol, triglycerides), and cardiovascular conditions (hypertension, and 185 

diseases of the circulatory and respiratory systems) ( Table S4 ). 186

## 224 

Variants sets for PRS calculation 225 

Because the upload of genotyping data required for PRS generation is limited due to privacy regulations, we 226 

have pre-calculated variant sets for all traits in the OpenGWAS. Using Alzheimer’s disease as an example, a 227 

manually curated set of variants as well as automated clumping-and-thresholding-based variant sets are 228 

available. It is possible to choose the variant set from the study of interest and at the p-value thresholds of 229 

interest (p<5x10 -5 , <5x10 -6 , <5x10 -7 , <5x10 -8 ). These variant sets are fully compatible with external tools for 230 

PRS calculation (Jordan, PolyGenius), which can be downloaded and used to calculate PRS for the individuals 231 

of interest. We finally support also pathway-specific PRS. When the Batch annotation job is executed in 232 

combination with gene-set enrichment analysis, variant-pathway weights are automatically calculated. These 233 

weights estimate the effect of each individual variant on pathways [18], and can be directly used to calculate 234 

pathway-specific PRS with Jordan. 235

## Discussion and Conclusions 237 

snpXplorer is a freely available web platform that addresses important challenges in the interpretation of GWAS 238 

data by providing an integrated, haplotype-aware framework that combines large-scale GWAS resources with 239 

interactive visualization and multi-source annotation. This design allows users to click, browse, and inspect 240 

variants, haplotypes, genes, and traits directly within the interface. As GWAS continue to expand in size and 241 

complexity, tools that enable efficient summarization, comparison, and contextualization of association signals 242 

are increasingly needed, and snpXplorer was developed to meet this demand. By enabling direct navigation 243

## 246 

A central contribution of this new version of snpXplorer is its emphasis on haplotypes. While most existing 247 

variant interpretation tools remain focused on individual lead variants, haplotypes can provide a more 248 

biologically informative representation of correlated genetic variation and facilitate the identification of affected 249 

genes that may be difficult to capture using SNP-centric approaches alone.[1] This integrated framework is 250 

particularly relevant for complex diseases, where GWAS loci often span multiple correlated variants and 251 

candidate genes. In these settings, interpretation based solely on individual lead SNPs may overlook broader 252 

regulatory mechanisms, which also include larger structural variants. By enabling direct navigation between 253 

variants, haplotypes, genes and traits, snpXplorer facilitates the interpretation of disease-associated loci within 254 

their broader genomic and functional context. 255 

## 256 

In addition, snpXplorer addresses the fragmentation of variant-level annotation across multiple resources. 257 

Existing platforms typically provide partial views of genetic evidence, requiring users to query multiple datasets 258 

independently. For example, ClinVar has a strong focus on clinically relevant variants, while most GWAS 259 

variants are not considered; gnomAD and dbSNP [19] are valuable for allele frequency and predicted 260 

consequences, but lack QTLs, haplotype information, and GWAS associations; PheWAS [20,21] reports 261 

GWAS associations while lacking allele frequencies and QTLs; SNiPA [22] combines a rich variant annotation 262 

with GWAS associations, but remains primarily variant- and study-centric. In contrast, snpXplorer integrates 263 

clinical annotations, population allele frequencies, QTLs, LD structure, functional predictions, structural 264 

variants and GWAS associations within a unified interface. Several tools cover parts of this workflow, but we 265 

did not identify a single platform that combines real-time multi-source variant annotation, haplotype-aware 266 

context, and interactive cross-trait GWAS exploration in one interface. 267 

## 268 

snpXplorer facilitates cross-trait exploration by structuring large GWAS repositories using semantic similarity 269 

between phenotypes. Unlike existing resources that return lists of studies or associations, this approach 270 

reduces redundancy and enables efficient navigation across related traits, supporting the identification of 271 

shared genetic architecture and pleiotropic effects. At the same time, the GWAS exploration module allows the 272 

visualization of multiple GWAS datasets simultaneously to compare association density tracks across traits. 273 

## 274 

Several widely used tools support GWAS and variant interpretation, including LocusZoom [23] for regional 275 

visualization, and platforms such as FUMA [24] and Open Target Genetics [25] for GWAS downstream 276 

analyses, functional annotation and gene prioritization. Other resources, including PheWeb [26], 277 

PhenoScanner [27,28], and GWAS Atlas [29], facilitate querying of genotype-phenotype associations across 278 

studies. A comparison between snpXplorer and commonly used tools is provided in Table S10 . However, these 279 

tools typically rely on SNP-centric representations, provide static or list-based outputs, or focus on specific 280 

aspects of annotation. In contrast, snpXplorer combines haplotype-aware representation with integrated 281 

variant-level annotation and cross-trait exploration, enabling navigation between variants, genes, haplotypes, 282 

and traits within a single interactive framework. 283

## 8

Several limitations should be considered. snpXplorer relies on publicly available GWAS summary statistics 285 

and reference panels, and haplotype definitions are currently based on European individuals, which may limit 286 

generalizability across populations. As with other GWAS interpretation tools, results should be interpreted in 287 

the context of the underlying datasets. Future developments will focus on incorporating non-European 288 

reference panels expanding support for ancestry-specific analyses, and will increase the supported annotation 289 

datasets. In addition, the clustering of traits is based on semantic similarity rather than genetic correlation, 290 

which needs genome-wide summary statistics and could be explored in future work. 291 

## 292 

In summary, snpXplorer provides an integrated platform for the exploration and annotation of GWAS and SNP 293 

data, combining haplotype-aware analyses with multi-source variant annotation and cross-trait integration. By 294 

addressing the fragmentation of existing tools and introducing haplotype-level representation, snpXplorer 295 

facilitates the interpretation of genetic association signals and supports a wide range of applications in human 296 

genetics research. 297
