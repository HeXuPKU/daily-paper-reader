Abstract 24 

Motivation: Linkage disequilibrium score (LDSC) regression is a popular method to estimate 25 heritability for complex traits using summary statistics and linkage disequilibrium (LD) reference 26 panels, offering a practical alternative to methods requiring individual-level data. Despite its 27 widespread use, LDSC regression can produce biased heritability estimates. The properties of 28 LDSC regression were investigated using summary statistics from several large-scale Alzheimer’s 29 disease (AD) studies and a variety of LD reference panels. These heritability estimates were 30 compared with those obtained from individual-level data. 31 32 

Results: When LDSC regression was applied to summary statistics obtained from meta-analysis, 33 it led to an underestimation of heritability. This can occur if meta-analysis is used to combine 34 studies of different ancestries leading to the caveat of the lack of an appropriate LD reference panel. 35 Additionally meta-analyses often include studies with different phenotype definitions, that not 36 only impacts heritability estimates but also makes them uninterpretable. Summary statistics 37 generated from imputed variants, even those with high imputation accuracy, can lead to 38 underestimation of heritability. For example, the heritability estimates for AD were reduced from 39 0.265 (se 0.148) to 0.160 (se 0.041) when imputed variants (INFO>0.9) were included compared 40 to analyzing only genotype array variants. A decrease in heritability estimates was also observed 41 when individual-level imputed variant data were analyzed using GCTA-GREML. Our findings 42 highlight the caveats of estimating heritability using meta-analysis summary statistics or imputed 43 data instead of genotyped or sequence data. 44

Introduction 52 

For the estimation of heritability using summary statistics obtained from genome-wide association 53 studies (GWAS), Linkage Disequilibrium Score (LDSC) regression (Bulik-Sullivan et al. 2015) 54 has been widely used. Unlike methods such as Genome-wide Complex Trait Analysis (GCTA)-55 Genomic-relatedness-matrix REstricted Maximum Likelihood (GREML) (Yang et al. 2011) that 56 require individual-level data, LDSC regression provides a practical alternative, especially when 57 access to individual-level data are restricted. Additionally for biobank-scale data it can be 58 computationally prohibitive to estimate heritability using individual-level data. Despite the 59 development of other methods to estimate heritability using summary statistics, e.g., Popcorn 60 (Brown et al. 2016), GWASH (Schwartzman et al. 2019), HESS (Shi, Kichaev, and Pasaniuc 61 2016), and SumHer (Speed and Balding 2019), LDSC regression remains the preferred choice due 62 to its straightforward implementation. LDSC regression has been used to estimate the heritability 63 for a wide-variety of complex traits including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (Wightman et al. 2021), 64 type 2 diabetes (Jang et al. 2021), peripheral artery disease (Zuydam van et al. 2021), peptic ulcer 65 disease (Wu et al. 2021), autism spectrum disorder (Grove et al. 2019), bipolar disorder (Mullins 66 

et al. 2021), etc (Sakaue et al. 2021). 67 68 There are discrepancies between narrow-sense heritability (h 2) estimates derived from genetic data 69 and those obtained from twin studies which are typically broad-sense heritability (H 2) estimates. 70 Unlike H 2, h 2 only includes additive genetic effects, therefore factors such as interaction will not 71 contribute to the estimates. Additionally, usually only common single nucleotide variants (SNV) 72 are used to estimate h 2, although rare and structural variants may also play a role. For AD, twin 73 studies consistently estimate H 2 to be 0.6-0.8 (Bergem, Engedal, and Kringlen 1997, Gatz et al. 74 1997), while AD h 2 estimates obtained from individual-level data range from 0.38-0.66 (Escott-75 Price and Hardy 2022, Baker et al. 2023). In contrast, recent large-scale GWAS (Alzheimer 76 Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC), et al. 2019, Jansen et al. 2019, Wightman et al. 2021, 77 Bellenguez et al. 2022) that estimate h 2 using LDSC regression, report much lower h 2 estimates 78 for AD, ranging from 0.018-0.106 (Table 1). These findings suggest substantial inconsistencies in 79 h2 estimates depending on the method used and/or study design. 80 81

4These discrepancies are not isolated to AD; similar issues are observed for various complex traits 82 (Jang et al. 2021, Mullins et al. 2021), raising broader concerns about the reliability of h 2 estimates 83 obtained from summary statistics. In this study, we examine several factors contributing to the 84 limitations of estimating h 2 using summary statistics through application of LDSC regression. 85 Issues addressed are discrepancies between the ancestry of the study subjects used to obtain 86 summary statistics and LD reference panels which can be more pronounced for meta-analyses, 87 variations in phenotype definitions across studies, and the impact of analyzing summary statistics 88 obtained from imputed variants. Using AD as a case study, we demonstrate how these factors can 89 result in inconsistent and potentially misleading h 2 estimates. Our analysis underscores the need 90 for careful consideration of these factors to improve the accuracy and reliability of h 2 estimates 91 derived from summary statistics using LDSC regression. 92 93 

Materials and methods 94 

White British UK Biobank AD cases and controls were identified using ICD10/ICD9 codes and 95 self-report. AD cases (N=3,720) were defined as individuals with a primary or secondary diagnosis 96 or cause of death including “AD” or “dementia in AD”. White British UK Biobank AD proxy 97 cases (N=51,080) were defined as individuals without AD but have at least one parent or sibling 98 with AD. There are 14,378 controls who are individuals >70 years-of-age without any record of 99 AD or dementia-related illnesses and do not report having a family history of AD, i.e., parents or 100 siblings. Since it was previously reported for LDSC regression that the analysis of summary 101 statistics that include related individuals can create a bias (Lee et al. 2018), the analysis was limited 102 to unrelated individuals. Related individuals (kinship coefficient >0.044) were identified and only 103 one member of a pair was retained in the sample. For the analysis there are 3,708 AD cases, 14,099 104 controls, and 47,052 AD proxies. 105 106 To obtain summary statistics, association analysis was conducted using an approximation of the 107 generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) as implemented in REGENIE (Mbatchou et al. 2021) 108 2.2.4. This multi-stage approach initially fits a whole-genome regression model using ridge 109 regression to estimate polygenic effects while accounting for structure and relatedness (Mbatchou 110 

et al. 2021). The single variant association model included covariates for sex, age, and five 111

Results 133 

For LDSC regression, h 2 was highest when estimated using a LD reference panel obtained from 134 the same study subjects which were used to generate the summary statistic. i.e., UK Biobank. This 135 h2=0.247 [standard error (se) 0.082] was lower than the h 2=0.361 (se 0.039) obtained from GCTA-136 GREML using the same 574,235 variants. For LDSC regression a further underestimation of 137 h2=0.161 (se 0.097) occurred when an external LD reference panel (GBR) was used even though 138 it matched the ancestry of the UK Biobank study subjects (Table 3). 139 140 Not surprisingly, the inclusion of proxy AD cases led to a large reduction in the estimated h 2 from 141 0.247 (se 0.082) to 0.046 (se 0.023) (Table 3). Discussion and conclusion 161 

In this study, we investigated several factors contributing to discrepancies in h 2 estimates derived 162 from LDSC regression. Our findings reveal significant issues related to mismatched ancestries 163 between summary statistics and LD reference panels, inconsistent phenotype definitions, and the 164 inclusion of imputed genetic variants with varying INFO scores. 165 166 Our analysis demonstrates that using LD reference panels with differing ancestries from the 167 summary statistics can lead to substantial inaccuracies in h 2 estimates. Differences in phenotype 168 definitions, such as including proxies, clinical or autopsied cases, further complicates comparisons 169 across studies. For example, in Jansen et al., 2019 (Jansen et al. 2019) (Table 1), AD cases from 170 UK Biobank are defined by clinical diagnosis, self-reported records and family history, whereas 171 Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) has autopsy cases. 172 Additionally, the inclusion of imputed variants even those that are imputed with high accuracy, 173

Additionally, AD proxies are often included in the meta-181 analysis in addition to AD cases and controls. The exclusion of AD proxies can lead to a more 182 realistic estimation of AD heritability, e.g., h 2=0.106 (se 0.012) 24 . When AD proxy cases and AD 183 cases are combined the h 2 estimates are meaningless because heritability is being estimated for 184 two distinct phenotypes. The low h² estimates in Table 1 can be attributed to several factors, 185 including differences in ancestry between summary statistics, the inclusion of proxy cases, and the 186 use of imputed variants. 187 188 A limitation of our analysis is when the number of genotyped variants is low, the standard error of 189 h2 estimates becomes relatively high, particularly when the LD reference panel is filtered to only 190 include HapMap3 variants. For example, in Table 4, when only genotyped variants were used to 191 estimate h 2 and the LD reference panel is limited to HapMap3 variants from UK Biobank, the 192 standard error is 0.148, leading to reduced precision in heritability estimates. This reduces our 193 ability to detect subtle differences in heritability across methods or to make reliable inferences 194 about the genetic architecture of the trait. This often results in overlapping confidence intervals, 195 though the overall trend remains evident. 197 estimates. Key points 238 

• LDSC regression heritability estimates can be substantially biased downward when 239 summary statistics are derived from meta-analyses that combine studies with different 240 ancestries, phenotype definitions, or inclusion of proxy cases. 241 

• Including imputed variants in heritability estimation, even those with high imputation 242 accuracy (INFO > 0.9), leads to underestimation of heritability compared to using only 243 genotyped array variants — a bias observed with both LDSC regression and GCTA-244 GREML. 245 

• Mismatches between the ancestry of the study population and the LD reference panel 246 reduce heritability estimates; using an in-sample LD reference panel yields the highest 247 and most accurate estimates with LDSC regression. 248 

• Combining AD cases with proxy cases in the same analysis produces heritability 249 estimates that are not only lower but effectively uninterpretable, as heritability is being 250 estimated for two distinct phenotypes simultaneously. 251 

• To obtain accurate heritability estimates, researchers should use genotyped or whole-252 genome sequence variants, ensure ancestry-matched LD reference panels, and apply 253 consistent phenotype definitions across contributing studies. 254
