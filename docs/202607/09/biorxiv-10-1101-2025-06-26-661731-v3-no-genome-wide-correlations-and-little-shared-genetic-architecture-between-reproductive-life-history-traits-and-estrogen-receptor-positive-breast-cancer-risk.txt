ABSTRACT 25 

Estrogen receptor -positive breast cancer (ER+ BC) is one of the most prevalent cancers, but the evolutionary 26 

processes shaping genetic variation in ER+ BC risk are poorly understood. Both evolutionary life -history 27 

theory and evidence from studies of individual ER+ BC risk variants suggest that increased genetic ER+ BC 28 

risk is associated with faster maturation, earlier reproduction, and/or increased reproductive success (i.e., 29 

there is  a trade -off), but  it is unclear  how well this pattern is replicated  when  considering  the polygenic 30 

architecture  of these traits after  controlling for potential biases. Here, we estimate genome -wide genetic 31 

correlations between ER+ BC risk and three reproductive traits (age at menarche, age at first birth, and the 32 

number of children) using genomic restricted maximum -likelihood analyses on Lifelines biobank data and 33 

linkage disequilibrium score regressions on population and family -based genome -wide association study 34 

data. Regardless of the data or method used, genetic correlations were low and not statistically significant. 35 

Further analyses decompos ing  genome -wide genetic variance into local regions detected only three loci 36 

exhibiting significant pleiotropy between ER+ BC risk and age at menarche, suggesting little shared genetic 37 

architecture between ER+ BC risk and reproductive traits. Thus, the role of life -history trade -offs in shaping 38 

ER+ BC risk in European populations appears, at most, small, and the evolutionary processes giving rise to 39 

this life -threatening disease remain unclear.  Future studies  could examine the  impact  of  evolutionary 40 

mismatch es  in shaping ER+ BC risk , where conducting  longitudinal studies on populations transitioning to 41 

reproductive patterns observed in contemporary European populations  would be  most useful .42

genetics, direct genetic effects, population stratification, ρ -HESS 44

INTRODUCTION 45 

Breast cancer is the fourth most deadly cancer worldwide (GBD, 2021) and accounts for the greatest 46 

proportion of new cancer cases (Sung et al., 2021). Estrogen receptor -positive breast cancer (ER+ BC) is 47 

the most common subtype, accounting for ~80% of all breast cancer cases overall, and is most common 48 

after age ~50 (Yasui & Potter, 1999). In European ancestry populations, common germline genetic  variation 49 

explains ~14% of the variation in a woman’s risk of ER+ BC (Zhang et al., 2020), and over 200 genome -50 

wide significant risk variants have now been identified (Michailidou et al., 2013, 2017; Zhang et al., 2020). 51 

However, we have a poor understanding of why genetic variation promoting a life -threatening disease 52 

persists in contemporary populations. 53 

Cancer is common not only in humans, but across the tree of life (Aktipis, Boddy, et al., 2015; Boddy et al., 54 

2015), and organisms have been under selective pressure to evolve cancer suppression mechanisms since 55 

the dawn of multicellularity over half a billion years ago (Merlo et al., 2006; Nunney, 2013). Evolutionary 56 

biology hypothesizes that genetic risk variants for diseases such as cancer may persist in populations due to 57 

their beneficial effects at younger ages i.e., because of life -history trade -offs ( life history theory  and the 58 

antagonistic pleiotropy theory of aging , Jacqueline et al., 2017; Promislow & Harvey, 1990; Roff, 1992; 59 

Stearns, 1977, 1992; Williams, 1957). For example, genetic variants promoting a faster life -history (earlier 60 

maturation and reproduction) could be selected for if they result in higher reproductive success, even if they 61 

increase the risk of cancer in later life (Figure 1, Dujon et al., 2022; Jacqueline et al., 2017). Altogether, 62 

these two non -mutually exclusive theories could explain why variation in the strength of cancer suppression 63 

covaries with life history, such as why cancer prevalence is higher in bird species with larger clutch sizes 64 

(Kapsetaki et al., 2024). Thus, due to reproductive traits’ close association with fitness, examining how 65 

genetic variation in ER+ BC risk is associated with reproductive traits could help us understand the 66 

evolutionary processes shaping ER+ BC risk variants, including those under contemporary directional 67 

selection (Haldane, 1954; Lande & Arnold, 1983; Mathieson et al., 2023; Robertson, 1966). 68 

In line with these evolutionary theories, individuals with genetic  predispositions to earlier maturation, 69 

reproduction, and/or higher numbers of children also show faster aging (Long & Zhang, 2023; Wang et al., 70 

2013) and increased risk for specific diseases (Byars et al., 2017; Perry et al., 2015; Trumble et al., 2023). 71 

Cancer appears no exception, with a host of tumor suppressor genes being linked to the regulation of puberty 72 

and fertility (reviewed in Boddy et al., 2015; Byars & Voskarides, 2020; Crespi & Summers, 2006; Dujon 73 

et al., 2022; Jacqueline et al., 2017) . These  includ e p53  genetic variants increasing conception likelihoods 74 

and overall cancer risk (Kang et al., 2009), and shorter repeats of the  CAG  repeat region of the androgen 75 

receptor promoting both increased fertility and prostate cancer risk (Summers & Crespi, 2008). Breast 76 

cancer (and in particular ER+ BC) is a good candidate for the maintenance of disease risk variants by life -77

involved in estrogen metabolism and signaling (Stone et al., 2025), and that these hormones play central 79 

roles in both fertility (through regulating processes such as conception, implantation, and pregnancy) a s well 80 

as  the etiology of ER+ BC (Ghosh et al., 2020; Jacqueline et al., 2017; Jasienska et al., 2017). Indeed, one 81 

genealogical study from Utah found that carriers of BRCA1/2 mutations also had more children (Smith et 82 

al., 2012), and contemporary studies examining all genome -wide significant loci for age at menarche found 83 

that genetic variants lowering age at menarche were also associated with higher overall breast cancer risk 84 

(Xiang et al., 2025) and, specifically, higher ER+ BC risk (Day et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2024). 85 

Contrary to these genetic studies, phenotypic associations from epidemiological studies are not entirely 86 

consistent with the predictions from evolutionary life -history theory (Figure 1), potentially owing to the 87 

influence of an evolutionary mismatch. In addition to life -history trade -offs, evolutionary mismatches are 88 

also important in cancer evolution, where populations experiencing rapid cultural, environmental, and/or 89 

lifestyle changes may experience an evolutionary mismatch between their life history and evolved cancer 90 

suppression mechanisms (Aktipis & Nesse, 2013; Nesse, 2011, 2019).  Relevant for ER+ BC in 91 

contemporary European populations  are  changes in diet and nutrition  which have caused  earlier ages at 92 

menarche (Duan et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2020; Rees, 1993) and recent changes in cultural preferences 93 

towards smaller families  driving  later ages at first birth and lower  numbers of children  (Aitken, 2022; Kirk, 94 

1996). Because amenorrhea (an absence of menstruation) is induced during breastfeeding, both lower family 95 

sizes and earlier ages at menarche may lead to women experiencing more menstrual cycles and a higher 96 

lifetime exposure to estrogen than women’s cancer suppression mechanisms have evolved to handle (Coe 97 

& Steadman, 1995; Eaton et al., 1994; Short, 1976; Strassmann, 1999) due to estrogen’s proliferative effects 98 

on mammary cells (Dall et al., 2018). Phenotypic evidence supports this theory and, while earlier ages at 99 

menarche have been associated phenotypically with higher ER+ BC risk  (in line with evolutionary life -100 

history trade -offs ),  earlier ages at first birth and higher numbers of children are actually associated with 101 

lower ER+ BC risk (Figure 1, Aktipis et al., 2015; Kelsey et al., 1993; Momenimovahed & Salehiniya, 102 

2019), potentially owing to their correlation with lifetime estrogen exposure (Aktipis, Ellis, et al., 2015; 103 

Bieuville et al., 2023). Consequently,  how genetic predispositions to ER+ BC are associated with 104 

reproductive traits  may deviat e from predictions made by life -history theory (Figure 1)  and remain unclear. 105 

A complete understanding of how genetic predispositions to ER+ BC are associated with reproductive traits 106 

in contemporary  European  populations is currently lacking  due  to  – first  and foremost  – the  importance of 107 

non -genetic  factors on both ER+ BC risk and reproductive traits. Because ER+ BC risk and reproductive 108 

traits are  influenced by  both  lifestyle factors and socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds (Barban et al., 109 

2016; Colditz et al., 2004), separating these environmental factors from genetic associations is crucial for 110

Hence, previous genetic studies focusing on genome -wide significant variants fail to capture the full scope 113 

of genetic variation in both ER+ BC risk and reproductive traits. Indeed, genome -wide significant variants 114 

explain less than 50% of the total variance in genomic risk for ER+ BC (Zhang et al., 2020), and only 25% 115 

of the total genetic variation in age at menarche (Day et al., 2017). Even fewer genome -wide significant 116 

variants for age at first birth and number of children born have been detected, despite significant heritability 117 

for both traits (Barban et al., 2016; Mathieson et al., 2023; Mills et al., 2021; Tropf et al., 2015). 118 

To resolve this knowledge gap, we need to quantify the overall extent and effects of shared genetic 119 

architecture between ER+ BC risk and  key  reproductive traits : age at menarche, age at first birth, and number 120 

of children. First, genome -wide genetic correlations  – which estimate the aggregated contribution of 121 

pleiotropy and linkage disequilibrium (LD) to the covariation of traits (van Rheenen et al., 2019)  – can be 122 

used to measure how genetic factors shape observed phenotypic associations, on average, across all loci. To 123 

the best of our knowledge, studies estimating genome -wide genetic correlations between ER+ BC risk and 124 

human life -history traits are limited to two studies estimating genetic correlations between ER+ BC risk and 125 

age at menarche (Jiang et al., 2019; Prince et al., 2024), one of which also estimated the genetic correlations 126 

between other life -history traits, including age at first birth and number of children (Prince et al., 2024). 127 

These studies revealed negligible genetic correlations between all life -history traits and ER+ BC risk, except 128 

for a small (r g=-0.048) and statistically non -significant (p = 0.062) genetic correlation between the number 129 

of children and ER+ BC risk. However, these studies did not fully account for confounding factors such as 130 

population stratification, which can bias genetic correlations (Berg et al., 2019; Border et al., 2022), 131 

particularly those involving reproductive traits (Howe et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2024). Second, because 132 

genome -wide genetic correlations estimate  average  genetic associations across all loci, they can miss 133 

smaller regions of shared genetic architecture that may have important influences on traits (Shi et al., 2017; 134 

van Rheenen et al., 2019). Therefore, it is also necessary to examine regional heritability and genetic 135 

correlations to understand the extent to which genetic predispositions to ER+ BC are shaped by human 136 

reproductive traits. 137 

Here we explore evidence for human reproductive traits shaping genetic predispositions to ER+ BC, 138 

accounting for environmental confounding and the polygenicity of these traits. We first estimate genome -139 

wide  heritabilities and  genetic correlations by performing a genomic restricted maximum -likelihood 140 

(GREML) analysis (Lee et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2010) using the Dutch biobank Lifelines (Scholtens et al., 141 

2015) linked to national pathology records (Casparie et al., 2007). We then perform linkage disequilibrium 142 

(LD) score regressions (LDSC, Bulik -Sullivan et al., 2015) on the largest available genome -wide association 143

RESULTS 169 

GREML analyses of Lifelines data 170 

We first performed GREML analyses on data from the Lifelines biobank ( n = 97,131 women) which 171 

leverages variation in genomic relatedness among non -relatives to derive heritability estimates and genetic 172 

correlations. Using the largest available genomic dataset in Lifelines (Global screening array,  n = 535,107 173 

SNPs), univariate analyses revealed statistically significant heritability (±SE) for age at menarche 174 

(43.1±5.8%,  n = 8,071 women) and age at first birth (24.2±7.4%,  n = 6,260 women), suggesting that genetic 175 

variance plays a significant role in shaping these traits (p < 0.05, Table 1a). The heritability of the number 176 

of children was estimated with less precision and was not statistically significant (12.8±9.8%, p = 0.337,  n177 

= 4,602 women, Table 1a). This was also true for ER+ BC risk, where the heritability was estimated as 178 

11.8% (±5.2%, p = 0.190,  n = 182 cases and  n = 8,700 controls, Table 1a). 179 

These heritability estimates were largely consistent when using other types of genomic data available in 180 

Lifelines (the Affymetrix,  n = 441,001 SNPs, and CytoSNP genomic array data,  n = 244,041 SNPs, Table 181 

S1). However, the heritability estimate for ER+ BC risk became statistically significant when using the 182 

Affymetrix array data (p = 0.04, Table S1), likely because these data contained a slightly higher number of 183 

individuals ( n = 198 cases,  n = 8,972 controls). Moreover, the heritability estimate for age at menarche was 184 

considerably lower (but remained statistically significant) when using the CytoSNP data (17.0% ±5.2%, p 185 

< 0.001,  n = 6,155 women, Table S1), most likely because of the lower genomic coverage of the CytoSNP 186 

data (Table S2). 187

We examined the extent and influence of shared genetic architecture between ER+ BC risk and three key 360 

reproductive traits (age at menarche, age at first birth, and number of children) to examine evolutionary 361 

theories explaining genetic predispositions to a major disease in contemporary European -ancestry 362 

populations. Our study builds on research looking at individual loci (Day et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2012; 363 

Xiang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024) and estimating genome -wide correlations (Jiang et al., 2019; Prince et 364 

al., 2024) by estimating genome -wide correlations while accounting for potential biases in genetic effects 365 

(Kong et al., 2018; Young et al., 2022; Young, 2024) and decomposing the genome -wide genetic variation 366 

into  1,703 local regions (Berisa & Pickrell, 2016; Shi et al., 2017). While our biobank analyses were 367 

underpowered, using the largest GWAS data available, we found no statistically significant genome -wide 368 

genetic correlations, regardless of the method used, suggesting that the importance of reproductive  traits  in 369 

shaping genetic ER+ BC risk is low and that common genetic variation does not underlie phenotypic trends 370 

observed in epidemiological studies (Aktipis, Ellis, et al., 2015; Kelsey et al., 1993; Momenimovahed & 371 

Salehiniya, 2019). Decomposing the genome -wide genetic variation into 1,703 approximately LD -372 

independent genomic regions (Berisa & Pickrell, 2016; Shi et al., 2017) revealed that these  results  are largely 373 

due to a lack of shared genetic architecture between ER+ BC risk and reproductive traits, with the exception 374 

of three regions with significant local covariance between age at menarche and ER+ BC risk (Figure 3). 375 

In particular, our analyses suggest that the role of life -history trade -offs in shaping ER+ BC risk is low: only 376 

one of the three regions with significant local covariance between reproductive traits and ER+ BC risk was 377 

in the direction predicted by life -history theory (i.e., earlier ages at menarche associated with higher ER+ 378 

BC risk, chromosome 16: 53,382,572 -55,903,773). This suggests that the difference in results between 379 

studies focusing only on individual genome -wide significant loci (which show some evidence for life history 380 

trade -offs shaping (ER+) BC risk ; Day et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2012; Xiang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024) 381 

versus studies focusing on whole -genome genetic correlations (which find no evidence, Jiang et al., 2019; 382 

Prince et al., 2024) is due to the largely independent genetic architectures of these traits and not due to a 383 

symmetrical distribution of significant local genetic covariance (Jiang et al., 2019; van Rheenen et al., 2019). 384 

Future studies could further explore GWAS data to identify smaller pleiotropic loci missed in our regional 385 

approach and explore which genetic variants causally drive these local covariances using colocalization 386 

analysis (Giambartolomei et al., 2014). However, our analyses, which capture all regions of significant local 387 

heritability, suggest that even if these variants exist, they will play a small role in shaping genetic 388 

predispositions to the disease.  Our study focused exclusively on reproductive traits, but body size  – due to 389 

its impact on an organism’s number of cells  – represents another important dimension of evolutionary 390 

constraints affecting cancer susceptibility (Aktipis et al., 2013; Nunney, 2018, 2024; Seluanov et al., 2018; 391 

Tollis et al., 2020; Vazquez et al., 2022). Height has been identified as a potential risk factor for breast 392

Our results also provide no evidence for strong, directional selection across ER+ BC risk loci owing to the 395 

lack of global and local genetic correlations with ages at first birth and number of children, which are closely 396 

related to fitness (Mathieson et al., 2023; Tropf et al., 2015). Thus, while previous phenotypic associations 397 

would suggest that ER+ BC risk variants are under negative selection, our results suggest that these 398 

phenotypic trends do not translate to the genetic level (Haldane, 1954; Lande & Arnold, 1983; Robertson, 399 

1966). Importantly, while the statistically significant genome -wide heritability for age at first birth and 400 

number of children reiterates that contemporary European populations retain the ability to evolve through 401 

natural selection (Byars et al., 2010; Fisher, 1930; Haldane, 1954), the heritability was low overall and few 402 

regions of significant local heritability were detected (two and zero loci, respectively). As with age at 403 

menarche, future studies could explore the role of individual genetic variants with these traits 404 

(Giambartolomei et al., 2014), but few loci of large effect have been detected (Barban et al., 2016; Mathieson 405 

et al., 2023; Mills et al., 2021) and our approach using HESS (Shi et al., 2017) should be well placed to 406 

detect any regions of strong covariance between these traits and ER+ BC risk. It is possible that future 407 

methods and larger datasets are better able to detect pleiotropic loci, but if current trends continue, 408 

methodological innovations are more likely to remove artificial inflation of heritability estimates rather than 409 

discover overlooked genetic variance  (Howe et al., 2022; Tropf et al., 2015, this paper, Table 1). Thus, 410 

current research suggests that there is little statistical power to detect directional selection on traits by 411 

searching for genetic covariation with numbers of children and ages at first birth among common variants 412 

in contemporary European populations, although recent work studying rare variants appears  more  promising 413 

(Souaiaia et al., 2026). 414 

Therefore, it remains unclear which evolutionary processes gave rise to genetic predispositions to ER+ BC. 415 

Beyond  reproductive  traits, pleiotropy between diseases acting in early - versus late -life may also maintain 416 

genetic variation in disease risk, also in line with the antagonistic pleiotropy theory of ageing (Rodríguez et 417 

al., 2017). One potential candidate for ER+ BC risk is Alzheimer’s disease, with higher cancer risk being 418 

protective against Alzheimer’s (Ospina -Romero et al., 2020), and recent work illuminating potential 419 

underlying mechanisms (Li et al., 2026),  which may include  genetic factors (Debnath et al., 2026; Ren et 420 

al., 2024). However, ER+ BC also has a relatively late age at onset (Yasui & Potter, 1999), suggesting that 421 

genetic ER+ BC risk variants may also have accumulated in populations due to weak selection against 422 

mutations manifesting at older ages, in line with the mutation accumulation theory of ageing (Charlesworth, 423 

1980; Haldane, 1941; Medawar, 1952). Thus, while our results suggest a minor role for reproductive life -424 

history trade -offs in maintaining genetic variation in ER+ BC risk, studies exploring pleiotropy between 425

An important challenge highlighted in this study is the detection of genetic life -history trade -offs in the 428 

presence of traits influenced by evolutionary mismatches, as exactly how evolutionary mismatches have 429 

influenced both phenotypic and genetic associations between ER+ BC risk and reproductive traits remains 430 

unclear. Firstly, while the low heritability of these traits and phenotypic evidence suggest that the phenotypic 431 

associations predicted by the mismatch theory are largely acting through environmental factors (e.g., 432 

changing cultural preferences), this does not rule out evolutionary mismatches influencing genetic 433 

associations through gene -by -environment interactions (Lea et al., 2023). The recent environmental changes 434 

in contemporary European populations (Corbett et al., 2018) mean that genetic associations measured  in 435 

these  population s may not represent  associations over  evolutionary  timescales . While the only study  – as far 436 

as we are aware  – lookin g at gene -by -environment interactions with ER+ BC suggested little overall 437 

importance of these effects, the analyses were constrained to the variation in environments experienced 438 

within the contemporary European population studied and, even still, detected some loci associated with 439 

both ER+ BC risk and reproductive traits  which  exhibit ed  gene -by -environment interactions (Middha et al., 440 

2023). Thus, studies are needed  which quantify the effects of gene -by -environment interactions  on 441 

reproductive traits and disease  beyond  the scope of environments experienced by contemporary Europeans. 442 

Conducting longitudinal genomic studies on populations undergoing fertility transitions and other lifestyle 443 

changes provides an opportunity to measure the role played by these gene -by -environment interactions and 444 

evolutionary mismatches in driving genetic risks for diseases such as cancer (Lea et al., 2023). Moreover, 445 

the se studies  may help unravel the causal mechanisms causing the phenotypic associations between 446 

reproductive traits and ER+ BC risk.  To  the best of our knowledge, the strongest evidence for the role of the 447 

evolutionary mismatch remains indirect: for example, reproductive traits  are  stronger risk factors for ER+ 448 

than for  estrogen receptor -negative breast cancer ( ER - BC , Aktipis, Ellis, et al., 2015) and differences in 449 

birth rates among countries  predict  breast cancer prevalence (You et al., 2018). Moreover, pleiotropic genes 450 

involving fertility are likely more easily detected in populations with higher birth rates (e.g., Trumble et al., 451 

2023; and, for breast cancer, Smith et al., 2012). Thus, longitudinal studies on populations undergoing these 452 

shifts in reproductive behavior would enable us to gain an understanding of both the role of evolutionary 453 

mismatches in shaping genomic associations for important life -history traits and disease, while unravelling 454 

potential causal mechanisms of why reproductive traits shape ER+ BC risk. Unfortunately, this research is 455 

currently limited by the lack of human diversity reflected in GWAS studies (Mills & Rahal, 2020) and 456 

research on understudied populations is needed. 457

Our study suffers from a few key limitations. First, although we found little evidence in support of life -458 

history trade -offs for ER+ BC risk, exploring genetic correlations between estrogen receptor -negative breast 459 

cancer (ER - BC) risk and life -history traits may find different results. While ER - BC risk is not as strongly 460 

linked to reproductive life -history traits as ER+ BC risk at the phenotypic level (Aktipis, Ellis, et al., 2015), 461 

ER - BC risk does show higher heritability than ER+ BC (Bauer et al., 2007), and there is some evidence 462 

that ER - BC risk is genetically correlated with earlier reproduction (Prince et al., 2024),  i.e.,  a fast life -463 

history, as previously hypothesized (Hidaka & Boddy, 2016). Second, we could not account for age at 464 

menopause in our analysis due to insufficient sample sizes. Age at menopause is an important life -history 465 

trait, and late ages at menopause are a risk factor for ER+ breast cancer (Kelsey & Bernstein, 1996), with 466 

previous studies showing some evidence for genetic variants underlying age at menopause also influencing 467 

cancer risk (Benonisdottir et al., 2024). Finally, despite using both individual -level biobank data and the 468 

largest available GWAS data for life -history traits and ER+ BC risk, some of our genetic correlations were 469 

still somewhat imprecise. These power limitations were mostly constrained to the GREML analyses of 470 

Lifelines biobank data, where some traits in some analyses did not show significant heritability (e.g., ER+ 471 

BC risk and number of children when using the Global screening array and CytoSNP array data). However, 472 

even when some of these traits showed higher and statistically significant heritability (e.g., using the 473 

Affymetrix data), the finding of non -significant genetic correlations was replicated, although we could only 474 

rule out genetic correlations larger than ±0.29 -0.52 for the Global screening array data. Estimating genetic 475 

correlations with GREML is computationally demanding but further studies could conduct similar analyses 476 

using larger biobanks. Nevertheless, our analyses of GWAS data were less power -limited, more precise, and 477 

likely less biased. On the whole, our study therefore provides a robust assessment of the strength of genetic 478 

correlations  and extent of shared genetic architecture  between ER+ BC risk and  reproductive  traits, given 479 

current GWAS data and methods available. 480 

In summary, our study uses a multifaceted approach to provide  a better  understanding of the genetic and 481 

evolutionary processes shaping ER+ BC risk in European ancestry populations, which is a deadly form of 482 

cancer with increasing prevalence. We encourage future studies to also validate findings across different 483 

datasets and approaches using the wealth of methods and genomic data available, but it is important to also 484 

broaden the diversity of human genomics and conduct longitudinal studies if we are to unravel the 485 

evolutionary process and the underlying mechanisms shaping ER+ BC risk. In particular, our study 486 

highlights the challenge of quantifying the influence of evolutionary mismatches on genomics data in 487 

contemporary European populations, and assessing the influence of gene -by -environment interactions 488 

remains an important line of future research. 489

Lifelines biobank 492 

Lifelines is a multi -disciplinary  prospective population -based cohort study examining, in a unique three -493 

generation design, the health and health -related behaviors of 167,729 persons living in the North of the 494 

Netherlands. It employs a broad range of investigative procedures in assessing the biomedical, socio -495 

demographic, behavioral, physical and psychological factors which contribute to the health and disease of 496 

the general population, with a special focus on multi -morbidity and complex genetics. We focused on data 497 

from 97,131 women born 1919 -1979 and recruited 2007 -2013 ( https://data -catalogue.lifelines.nl/ , accessed 498 

08 -05 -2024,  Lifelines , 2024). 499 

Life -history traits 500 

Age at menarche describes the age in which a female first started menstruating (Rees, 1993), can vary among 501 

individuals due to both environmental (e.g., nutritional status) and genetic effects, and is linked to future 502 

disease risk, including breast cancer (Parent et al., 2003; Perry et al., 2015). We quantified age at menarche 503 

using the lifelines baseline question: “How old were you when you had your first menstruation?” (N=85,210 504 

women). For quality control, we removed 153 values >3 standard deviations from the mean. This gave a 505 

mean age at menarche of 13.1 (SD = 1.7, range = 8 -18, N = 85,057, Figure S4A), representative of the 506 

Netherlands (Parent et al., 2003). 507 

Age at first birth and number of children born are likewise influenced by both genetic factors and 508 

environmental factors which are then further constrained by the social environment, cultural background, 509 

and any remaining individual preferences (Barban et al., 2016; Mills et al., 2021). We quantified age at first 510 

birth and the number of children ever born using questions for a participant’s birth date and the years of 511 

birth of her children focusing on full -term pregnancies. For these variables, we used data from baseline 512 

questionnaires, supplemented with follow -up questions during wave two of the Lifelines data collection 513 

(2014 -2018). For age at first birth, we screened the questionnaire data removing any years of birth of 514 

children before an individual was born and after an individual had answered the questionnaire (n = 16). We 515 

used the birth dates of participants to calculate ages at first birth (mean = 26.9, 95% CIs = 18.9 -36.3, Figure 516 

S4B) which occurred 1942 -2017 (N = 64,779). The number of children ever born was then calculated only 517 

for women who were asked for the years of birth of their children after age 45 to avoid biasing the number 518 

of childless women and the total number of children born. This gave a mean number of children born of 2.0 519 

(range 0 -8, N = 51,672, Figure S4C). All data were handled in R 4.2.2 (2022 -10 -31, R Core Team, 2020) 520 

using  dplyr  1.1.4 (Wickham et al., 2019). 521
