## Abstract 18 

Background: Structural variants (SVs) are most effectively identified using long-read (LR) sequenc-19 

ing. However, such data remain scarce, and sequenced samples often lack associated phenotypic 20 

information. To overcome this limitation, we integrated pangenome-based (variation graph-based) 21 

and imputation approaches to enable large-scale SV association studies in the three main French 22 

dairy cattle breeds. 23 

Results: A variation graph was constructed using 69,892 deletions, 89,900 insertions, and 17,402 24 

duplications detected in 176 LR samples. We subsequently genotyped 939 samples for each SV in 25 

the panel by realigning their short read (SR) sequences to the graph. Validation analyses showed 26 

high genotype concordance rates for deletions (0.79) and insertions (0.79); however, concordance 27 

for duplications was low (0.14), leading to their exclusion from further analyses. The retained SVs 28 

were combined with single nucleotide variants (SNVs) to build a sequence-level imputation 29 

reference panel. Using SNP genotyping array data, we imputed SVs and SNVs for 11,902 Holstein, 30 

3,753 Montbéliarde, and 3,053 Normande bulls. After quality control, more than 14 million SNVs 31 

and 40 thousand SVs were retained for within-breed genome-wide association studies (GWAS) us-32 

ing daughter yield deviations for stature and four milk production and composition traits. The 33 

GWAS results reveled genetic architectures consistent with previous findings and identified 40 34 

genome-wide significant associations between structural variant and key phenotypes. Conditional 35 

analyses showed that ten of these SVs as strong candidates associated with milk fat and protein 36 

contents, as well as stature. 37 

Conclusions: By integrating LR, SR, and SNP genotyping data within a unified pangenome and 38 

imputation framework, we demonstrate a scalable strategy to systematically interrogate the 39 

contribution of SVs to complex traits. The resulting genetic architectures were highly consistent 40 

with previous findings, validating both the robustness and transferability of our approach. Our 41 

findings highlight the added value of integrating SVs into routine genomic analyses and provide a 42 

scalable framework for incorporating SVs into genomic selection in dairy cattle. 43 

Keywords: Structural variants, genotyping, imputation, pangenome, genome-wide association stud-44 

ies, cattle 45 

## Background 46 

The advent of long-read (LR) sequencing technologies has enabled more comprehensive detection 47 

of structural variants (SVs), commonly defined as genomic variants larger than 50 base pairs, that 48

were previously inaccessible using short-read (SR) data [1,2]. The limitation of SR-based SV 49 

detection is primarily related to variant size, which frequently exceeds the sequence read length of 50 

SR data [1]. In contrast, LR data offer a major advantage for SV detection due to their substantially 51 

longer read lengths, which can range from a few to hundreds of kilobases [1,3]. Consequently, mul-52 

tiple large-scale initiatives have been launched to generate LR data and characterize SVs across di-53 

verse species, including humans, model organisms, and agriculturally important animals and plants, 54 

with the aim of better capturing genome-wide genetic diversity [4–7]. 55 

Currently, LR data are generally produced by two platforms, Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) and Ox-56 

ford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), each employing distinct sequencing approaches [1]. Using ei-57 

ther of these LR data, SV detection can be categorized into assembly and alignment-based ap-58 

proaches [2]. The former involves sequential steps including de novo read assembly, alignment to a 59 

reference genome, and SV calling. An evolution of this methodology is the pangenome approach, in 60 

which multiple de novo assemblies are integrated into a unified reference graph,thereby enabling 61 

high-resolution SV discovery across diverse genomic backgrounds [6]. 62 

On the other hand, the alignment-based approach directly aligns reads to the reference genome, fol-63 

lowed by SV calling using mapping-based evidence [8]. A benchmark study with human datasets 64 

indicated that the vast majority of SVs (more than 80%) can be detected using both methods [8]. 65 

However, the alignment-based approach can assign SVs in their heterozygous or homozygous state, 66 

whereas the assembly-based method assigns SVs as present or absent in diploid individuals, unless 67 

haplotype-resolved assemblies are available to detect heterozygosity [9,10]. Recently, benchmark-68 

ing of several alignment-based methods indicated consistent performance of PBSV [11] for 69 

detecting cattle-specific SVs across two LR data types of PacBio and ONT [12]. 70 

Before the adoption of LR technologies, several studies relied on SR data to characterize SVs in the 71 

cattle genome [13–15]. For example, one study identified 6,462 SVs from 62 bulls of three major 72 

French breeds [13]. Subsequently, DNA primers were designed based on the boundaries of selected 73 

SVs affecting important genes and embedded in the custom-designed Single Nucleotide Polymor-74 

phism (SNP) genotyping chip of BovineLD BeadChip of Illumina [16,17]. This allowed a more ac-75 

curate estimation of SV frequencies in a much larger population panel [16]. This SNP-chip data has 76 

further facilitated the association of specific SVs, whose genomic positions were later confirmed 77 

using LR-based pangenome approaches, with economically relevant traits. One such example is a 78 

deletion within the MATN3 gene associated with stature in cattle [18]. 79

Nevertheless, as previously stated, SV detection using SR was not optimal to detect a wide spec-80 

trum of SVs compared to LR approaches [1,19]. The disadvantages were mainly due to the limita-81 

tion of read length, which hindered the resolution of regions with high repetitive-element contents 82 

and complex genomic architecture, where read mapping is either unspecific or tends to be biased 83 

[1,19]. In contrast to LR data, SR are more abundant in public repositories, due to relatively lower 84 

cost and simplified library preparations [3,20]. 85 

Based on this discrepancy, several pangenome-based methods have been developed to allow geno-86 

typing of known SVs using SR data [21]. Similar to SV calling, genotyping known SVs can also be 87 

grouped into assembly- and alignment-based approaches. The assembly-based method genotypes 88 

SVs by comparing k-mer distributions found within sequence reads and along the paths of a graph 89 

representing true SVs [22,23]. Although capable of genotyping a wide range of SVs irrespective of 90 

their size, these methods are generally less memory-efficient and may suffer from uncertainty in 91 

breakpoint resolution [21]. In the alignment-based approach, tools are categorized into whole-92 

genome [24] or local graph alignment-based [25,26] methods. The main difference between the two 93 

is that reads are either directly aligned to a whole-genome graph or first aligned to the linear refer-94 

ence genome followed by re-alignment of these reads onto a regional graph [21]. In this context, a 95 

graph comprises a set of nodes and edges that represent alternative paths and known SVs, providing 96 

an alternative representation to the linear reference genome [21,24]. Alignment to graph structures 97 

has been shown to be comparable in efficiency to alignment against linear references [12,21]. Re-98 

cently, the variation graph toolkit (VG) [27] has been shown to be more efficient than several 99 

alignment-based variation graph tools, such as GRAPHTYPER [25], PARAGRAPH [26], and 100 

SVTYPER [28], for genotyping SVs in the cattle genome [12]. 101 

While both LR and SR data enable comprehensive SV detection and genotyping, datasets often lack 102 

phenotypic records and/or remain limited in size, thereby reducing statistical power for association 103 

analyses [21,29]. To address this limitation, an imputation framework can be applied to leverage 104 

phenotyped samples that have been genotyped using SNPs arrays. Imputation strategies enable ex-105 

trapolation from a sparsely genotyped set of samples to a more densely genotyped set by maximiz-106 

ing linkage-disequilibrium (LD) information among variants and/or pedigree-based relationships 107 

[30,31]. Imputation accuracy is influenced by several factors, including haplotype phasing quality, 108 

number of reference samples and their relationships to target samples, marker density between tar-109 

get and reference panels, and minor allele frequency (MAF) [32]. A two-step imputation strategy 110 

has commonly been applied to enable detailed association studies in the cattle genome [33–35]. The 111

first step consists of within-breed imputation from medium-density SNP chip (~50K SNPs; MD) to 112 

higher-density genotype data (~700K SNPs; HD) using FImpute, taking advantage of large sets of 113 

major ancestors genotyped at HD level. The second step aims at increasing marker density to se-114 

quence level (millions of variants across the genome) from the imputed HD data using a multi-115 

breed panel of sequenced animals, thereby enabling detailed dissection of the genetic architecture of 116 

complex traits [34–36]. 117 

Holstein (HOL), Montbéliarde (MON), and Normande (NMD) are the main French cattle breeds in 118 

terms of population size [37]. These breeds have undergone selection for improved dairy traits and 119 

are maintained in separate herdbooks [38,39]. Because several key dairy phenotypes can only be 120 

directly measured in females, phenotypic information from bulls is typically inferred from the aver-121 

age adjusted performances of their daughters, referred to as daughter yield deviations (DYD) [40]. 122 

Using DYD as inferred phenotypes enables genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to be 123 

conducted in bulls with large progeny groups, thereby increasing the reliability of estimated genetic 124 

effects [17,18,34]. 125 

In the SV context, the successive steps of calling, genotyping, imputation, and GWAS have been 126 

implemented in human datasets to assess the effects of SVs on the occurrence of several chronic 127 

diseases [41]. To our knowledge, such an integrated framework has not yet been applied in cattle. 128 

In this study, the primary objective was to evaluate the role of SVs in relation to five important 129 

agronomic traits in French dairy cattle. This was achieved through the integration of alignment-130 

based SV calling, pangenome-based SV genotyping (variation graph), and imputation 131 

methodologies. 132 

## Methods 133 

The analysis workflow employed in this study is depicted in Figure 1a. We combined several data-134 

sets with complementary properties: 176 LR-sequenced, 939 SR-sequenced, and several thousand 135 

phenotyped samples also genotyped with the medium-density (MD) SNP array. LR-derived SVs 136 

served as the basis for the creation of a variation graph. SVs from 939 SR samples across 17 breeds 137 

were then genotyped using the variation graph to expand the dataset. The association study could 138 

not be performed directly on the LR and SR datasets because phenotypes were available for only a 139 

subset of individuals. To address this issue, we imputed genotypes of SVs in a larger dataset of 140 

animals genotyped with SNP arrays, for which phenotypic data were available. 141

SV detection from LR samples 144 

The 176 samples from 14 French dairy and beef cattle breeds were sequenced using different LR 145 

technologies (Figure 1b, Suppl. Table 1), as described in a previous study [12]. For each sample, 146 

LR data were first aligned to the ARS-UCD1.2 bovine reference genome [42] using the PacBio 147 

PBMM2 aligner tool [43] with default parameters to generate BAM files. SVs were then called us-148 

ing PBSV v2.6.2 [11] with default parameters. Filtering steps were applied to retain biallelic dele-149 

tions, insertions, and duplications ranging from 50 bp to 100 kb in length, with well-defined 150 

breakpoints (i.e., not flagged as IMPRECISE), and located on the 29 autosomes or the X chromo-151 

some. Duplications were subsequently converted to insertions using JASMINE v1.1.5 [44]. Then, 152 

converted duplications were merged with deletions and insertions across all 176 samples using the 153 

JASMINE merged function with parameters --ignore_strand --mutual_distance --154 

allow_intrasample. 155 

Genotyping SVs from SR samples 156 

The multi-samples VCF file was served as the reference panel to create a genome-wide variation 157 

graph using the VG toolkit v1.56 [45]. The variation graph was first constructed separately for each 158 

chromosome using the vg construct command with default parameters. Individual chromosome 159 

graphs were then concatenated according to Bos taurus (BTA) autosome order using vg ids --join 160 

<graph_BTA1>… <graph_BTA29> . Subsequently, the graph was re-indexed and converted using 161 

vg index and vg autoindex --workflow giraffe . SR data from 939 samples across 17 breeds were 162 

available. For each SR sample, reads were aligned to the variation graph using GIRAFFE [27]. SV 163 

genotypes were then called separately for each chromosome using vg call with the “-Aa” option to 164 

genotype all bubbles (i.e., SVs) present in the graph. The genotyped SVs were subsequently con-165 

catenated across chromosomes and merged across individuals using BCFTOOLS [46] with default 166 

parameters. 167 

Benchmarking and assessing informativity of genotyped SVs 168 

To assess genotyping accuracy, benchmarking was performed using TRUVARI [47] on two ran-169 

domly selected individuals from each of HOL, MON, and NMD breeds for which both LR and SR 170 

data were available. Performance evaluation was conducted independently for each SV type 171 

(deletions, insertions, and duplications) to ensure category-specific accuracy. SVs initially detected 172 

by PBSV were used as the truth set, and VG outputs from the variation graph were used as the 173 

comparison set. Visualization of bubbles in the variation graph was performed using GFAVIZ [48]. 174

SNV calling 179 

Small genomic variants, referred to as SNVs (mainly SNPs and small InDels), were also available 180 

for the 939 SR samples used for SV genotyping with the variation graph approach. SR data were 181 

first aligned to ARS_UCD1.2 bovine reference genome [42] using BWA-MEM [52]. Then, SAM-182 

TOOLS [46] was used to mark and remove duplicate reads and to sort alignments by chromosome. 183 

Subsequently, base recalibration, variant calling, and joint genotyping across samples were carried 184 

out using GATK [53], with additional known variants from the “1000 bull genomes” project, as a 185 

recalibration panel. Using BCFTOOLS [46], biallelic SNVs with MAF > 0.01, missing rate < 0.1, 186 

and quality score > 30 were retained as high-confidence variants. 187 

Benchmarking of imputation tools 188 

To identify the most suitable tool for SV imputation in the larger SNP-genotyped dataset, we as-189 

sessed the performance of BEAGLEv5.4 [54], MINIMACv4 [55], and GLIMPSEv2.0.1 [56] on 190 

BTA25. SNVs overlapping deletions were identified using BEDTOOLSv2.3 [57] and subsequently 191 

removed using BCFTOOLS. The remaining SNVs and SVs from the 939 samples were merged us-192 

ing BCFTOOLS, resulting in a sequence-level variant dataset with increased marker density. Link-193 

age disequilibrium (LD)-based pruning (PLINK, r² = 0.5) was applied to generate a target dataset 194 

mimicking a high-density (HD) SNP array. 195 

For BEAGLE, the reference panel was phased internally using default parameters and imputation 196 

was performed on unphased target samples. For MINIMAC and GLIMPSE imputations, SHAPEIT 197 

v5.1.1 [58] was used for phasing the reference panel prior to imputation. For MINIMAC, the 198 

SHAPEIT-phased reference panel was converted into MSAV format for computational efficiency. 199 

For GLIMPSE, the target file was chunked using GLIMPSE2_chunk --sequential --window-mb 30 .200 

Consecutively, the SHAPEIT-phased reference panel was divided into chunks, which were then im-201 

puted according to the target window. Concordance between true (SR-based) and imputed geno-202 

types was assessed using an in-house script for SNVs and TRUVARI for SVs. 203

Genotyping SVs from SR samples using a variation graph 243 

SV genotyping using variation graph 244 

SVs detected from LR samples were retained regardless of the number of supporting individuals 245 

and used as a reference panel to construct a genome-wide variation graph. Within the variation 246 

graph, SVs were represented as bubbles, with biallelic SVs having two possible paths and multialle-247 

lic bubbles having more than two alternative paths. A total of 171,451 bubbles were generated, of 248 

which 161,237 were biallelic and 10,214 were multiallelic. SR data from 939 individuals were 249 

aligned to the graph using GIRAFFE [27], followed by SV genotyping using the VG-toolkit [27] 250 

pipeline. These 939 samples represented 17 French breeds, with an uneven distribution dominated 251 

by major breeds such as HOL, MON, NMD, and Limousine (LIM) (Figure 2a). After genotyping, 252 

only biallelic SVs were retained for downstream analyses. 253 

Benchmark of SV genotyping 254 

To evaluate the performance of SV genotyping from SR data, we conducted a benchmarking analy-255 

sis. The SV recall rate was high across all SV types (Figure 2b). Insertions had the average highest 256 

recall rate (0.94), compared to 0.83 for deletions and 0.84 for duplications. These findings 257 

demonstrate that variation graph-based genotyping successfully recovers the majority of SVs 258 

initially identified from LR data (Suppl. Table 3). 259 

The recall rate was computed based on breakpoint positions only, without considering genotype as-260 

signment. Using the -Aa option in vg-call , homozygous reference genotypes (0/0) were assigned 261 

when evidence supported the reference haplotype, whereas missing genotypes (./.) were assigned 262 

when no sufficient evidence was available. Of note, only heterozygous (0/1) and homozygous alter-263 

native (1/1) genotypes were reported in the initial LR-based SV detection. 264 

The average genotype concordance rates were 0.79 for deletions, 0.14 for duplications, and 0.79 for 265 

insertions (Figure 2b). The low concordance observed for duplications is likely due to difficulties in 266 

read alignment and insufficient evidence to correctly assign genotypes, given the complexity of 267 

graph structures at these loci. For instance, biallelic duplications had more strangled graph paths 268 

(Figure 2c) compared to biallelic deletions and insertions (Figure 2d, 2e). Given their concordance, 269 

duplications were excluded from subsequent analyses. 270

Informativity of genotyped SV 272 

After filtering on MAF and missing genotypes, 36,402 deletions and 43,290 insertions were re-273 

tained. The informativeness of these SVs was supported by the clear population structure observed 274 

in the PCA of the 939 samples (Figure 2f). PC2 (16.9% of variance) separated HOL from the other 275 

breeds more distinctly than PC1 (25.0%). PC4 (9.2%) further separated NMD from other breeds. 276 

Overall, the PCA results were consistent with previous studies [12,13]. 277 

We then computed FST to contrast allele frequencies between dairy (HOL) and beef (LIM) cattle 278 

(Figure 2g). Sixteen SVs showed FST values higher than 0.75 (Suppl. Table 4). The top SV (FST = 279 

0.98) was a 6,880 bp insertion on BTA6, located approximately 114 kb upstream of KIT . This SV 280 

had a frequency of 0.97 in LIM but was fixed as homozygous reference in HOL. The KIT gene is 281 

well known for its role in coat color, explaining the differentiation between the spotted patterns ob-282 

served in HOL, MON, and NMD and the uniform coat of LIM [60,61]. The second most differenti-283 

ated SV (FST = 0.91) was a 516 bp insertion on BTA2 located within an intron of COL5A2 . This 284 

SV had a frequency of 0.85 in LIM but was absent in HOL. This gene has previously been associ-285 

ated with muscle development in independent population of Charolais (CHA) and LIM [62]. 286 

Benchmarking of imputation tools 287 

We performed benchmarking on BTA25 to evaluate the performance of imputation tools. This 288 

benchmarking focused on the second step of the two-step imputation procedure. To reduce redun-289 

dancy, SNVs located within deletion regions were removed . Ultimately, 365,807 SNVs and 1,278 290 

genotyped SVs were retained and merged on BTA25 (Figure 3a). Six samples were used as a target 291 

set and 933 as the reference panel. Merging SNVs and SVs increased marker densities at the se-292 

quence level. Linkage disequilibrium pruning (PLINK, R 2 -pruning = 0.5) was applied to select 293 

31,426 SNVs, which served as the target dataset for imputation. This number is comparable to the 294 

number of SNVs on the HD SNP genotyping array for BTA25. 295 

Imputation of SNVs generally showed higher concordance rates than SV imputation across all three 296 

tools (Figure 3b). For instance, SNV concordance rate reached up to 0.99 for BEAGLE and 297 

GLIMPSE, while MINIMAC showed a lower concordance (0.95). Similarly, genotype concordance 298 

of imputed SVs was higher with BEAGLE and GLIMPSE compared to MINIMAC. 299

## Discussion 411 

Genotyping SVs from variation graph 412 

We combined variation graph-based genotyping and imputation approaches to investigate the con-413 

tribution of SVs to economically important traits in the main French cattle breeds. Building on pre-414 

vious work using variation graph for SV genotyping from SR data [12], we extended the framework 415 

to include duplications in addition to deletions and insertions. The construction of variation graphs 416 

requires explicit sequence information for both reference and alternative alleles. Therefore, 417 

inversions and complex variants were excluded, as their allele sequences are not directly reported in 418 

PBSV VCF outputs. Although such sequences could be inferred from breakpoint information, 419 

particularly for inversions, this approach was not implemented here in order to maintain a robust 420 

and streamlined analytical framework. 421 

Benchmarking indicated that duplication genotyping was substantially less reliable than for dele-422 

tions and insertions. Although duplications were converted into insertions, following JASMINE 423 

recommendations [44], their representation in the graph likely remained more complex, which may 424 

explain the low genotype concordance. As a result, duplications were excluded from downstream 425 

analyses. 426 

In contrast to previous recommendations suggesting a minimum number of supporting individuals 427 

(e.g., VARCALLS ≥ 7) [12], we retained all LR-detected SVs, including those observed in a single 428 

individual (~40% of variants). This choice reflects the still limited number of LR samples relative to 429 

SR datasets. Applying stringent thresholds at this stage could bias against rare variants and distort 430

more accurate estimation of their frequency and potential relevance.. 432 

Filtering thresholds (MAF > 0.01; missing rate < 0.1) were then applied to ensure data quality and 433 

compatibility with downstream imputation. PCA based on genotyped SVs confirmed their 434 

informativeness, as individuals were correctly clustered according to breed. While PC1 (25.0% of 435 

variance) did not clearly separate breeds—suggesting some redundancy—subsequent components 436 

(notably PC2 and PC4) captured clear population structure, consistent with a previous study [12]. 437 

This was further supported by the FST analyses, which highlighted highly differentiated SVs 438 

between dairy and beef breeds. Altogether, these results demonstrate that variation graph–based 439 

genotyping effectively captures both shared and breed-specific SV variation. 440 

Limited accuracy of SV imputation 441 

SV imputation was consistently less accurate than SNV imputation, as evidenced by both bench-442 

marking results on BTA25 and dosage-R 2 values in the full dataset. Several factors likely contribute 443 

to this limitation. First, marker density is substantially higher for SNVs than for SVs. In our dataset, 444 

approximately 20 million SNVs were available (~1 variant every 135 bp), whereas SVs were much 445 

less frequent. Moreover, SNVs overlapping deletion regions were removed, further reducing marker 446 

density in the vicinity of SVs and likely impairing imputation performance. [80]. 447 

Second, LD between SVs and nearby SNVs may be weaker or more difficult to capture than LD 448 

among SNVs. In our pipeline, SNVs and SVs were called independently (GATK vs. variation 449 

graph), which may introduce inconsistencies in genotype representation. Joint genotyping of SNVs 450 

and SVs within a unified graph framework could improve read alignment, enhance detection of 451 

heterozygous variants, and strengthen LD patterns, thereby improving SV imputation. However, 452 

this remains to be demonstrated empirically. Third, SVs tend to have lower MAF than SNVs 453 

(Suppl. Figure 11), which negatively affects imputation accuracy. This is consistent with the 454 

observed positive correlation between MAF and imputation R2 [81]. While population-specific 455 

reference panels have been shown to improve imputation of low-frequency variants [82] the number 456 

of sequenced individuals per breed in our study, particularly for the NMD and MON breeds, was 457 

insufficient to support accurate imputation using single-breed reference panels. Consequently, the 458 

use of breed-specific reference panels in the final imputation step from HD to sequence level, in-459 

cluding both SNVs and SVs, would likely not have provided any substantial improvement. 460 

In this study, HOL had the highest number of samples in the three datasets, i.e. LR, SR, and SNP-461 

genotyped samples. However, following the analysis process, HOL exhibited the fewest retained 462

The observed GWAS architectures across the five traits were consistent with previous studies using 472

and CYP11B1 improves the proportion of explained variance compared to considering DGAT1 486 

alone [85]. 487

SVs likely represent the primary variants underlying QTLs. Notably, SVs identified as lead variants 489 

tended to have higher allele frequencies in the breeds where their effects were detected, supporting 490 

the importance of accurate genotyping and imputation. 491

Future perspectives 496 

This study demonstrates the feasibility of integrating long-read, short-read, and SNP-array data 497 

through pangenome-based and imputation approaches to investigate SV associations at scale. How-498 

ever, several limitations remain, particularly the progressive reduction in the number of usable SVs 499 

due to filtering and imputation constraints, which likely reduces GWAS power. 500 

Two main directions could improve future analyses. First, increasing the number of LR-sequenced 501 

individuals will enhance SV discovery and enable the construction of more representative reference 502 

panels. Second, refining the selection of SVs included in variation graphs, by prioritizing variants 503 

with sufficient support and intermediate allele frequencies, may improve genotyping accuracy and 504 

downstream imputation performance. 505 

Overall, these developments should enhance the integration of SVs into genomic analyses and im-506 

prove the resolution of GWAS, ultimately facilitating their incorporation into genomic selection 507 

programs. 508 

## Conclusions 509 

By integrating long-read (LR), short-read (SR), and SNP genotyping data within a unified 510 

pangenome and imputation framework, we establish a scalable strategy to systematically interrogate 511 

the contribution of structural variants (SVs) to complex traits. The resulting genetic architectures 512 

were highly consistent with previous findings, validating both the robustness and transferability of 513 

our approach. 514 

We identified 36 distinct SVs associated with key agronomical traits across three major French 515 

dairy breeds, including four pleiotropic variants, highlighting the pervasive and multifaceted impact 516 

of SVs to phenotypic variation. Notably, conditional analyses pinpointed 10 SVs as strong 517 

candidate causal variants within their respective QTLs, providing direct evidence of their functional 518 

relevance. 519 

Taken together, these results establish SVs as an important yet still underexplored component of the 520 

genetic architecture of complex traits and provide a practical roadmap for their integration into 521 

high-resolution genomic analyses and future breeding programs. 522
