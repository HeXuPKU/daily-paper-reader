# Abstract 25 

We present Nemo 2.4, an advanced forward-time individual-based simulation framework 26 

designed to model the complex eco-evolutionary dynamics and genetic basis of quantita-27 

tive traits. This tool addresses current challenges in evolutionary quantitative genetics 28 

by providing unprecedented flexibility and computational efficiency. Nemo 2.4’s modu-29 

lar architecture allows researchers to design custom life cycles by combining specialized 30 

Life Cycle Event (LCE) modules, from reproduction and dispersal to selection, crossing, 31 

and phenotype expression. The software supports diverse population models, including 32 

both Wright-Fisher (WF) and non-WF dynamics, spatially explicit models, and varying 33 

demography. Nemo 2.4 handles a wide range of genetic architectures, including both 34 

multi-allelic Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) for general trait studies, and dense di-allelic 35 

Quantitative Trait Nucleotides (QTN) implemented with highly optimized bit-wise data 36 

structures. Crucially, it allows the simulation of QTNs on comprehensive genetic maps 37 

that incorporate other genetic elements, providing genomic-scale resolution. Key biolog-38 

ical complexities are integrated natively: the model accommodates modular pleiotropy, 39 

dominance, and pairwise epistasis across multiple traits, facilitating the study of com-40 

plex genotype-phenotype mappings. Furthermore, Nemo 2.4 models phenotypic plastic-41 

ity through reaction norms and incorporates underlying liability thresholds, enabling the 42 

simulation of environmental influences on trait evolution with various forms of selection 43 

(e.g., Gaussian, linear, truncation). Due to its compiled design and memory-efficient 44 

data representations for large numbers of loci, Nemo provides a robust platform for run-45 

ning high-throughput simulations critical for testing theoretical predictions in polygenic 46 

adaptation and understanding evolutionary responses to changing environments. 47 

# Introduction 48 

We present here a new version of the individual-based, forward-in-time simulation soft-49 

ware Nemo (Guillaume and Rougemont, 2006) with improved capacity to model the 50 

genetic basis and eco-evolutionary dynamics of quantitative traits. Nemo 2.4 meets the 51 

current advances and challenges in evolutionary quantitative genetics to investigate the 52 

eco-evolutionary dynamics of populations in changing environments. 53 

Quantitative traits constitute the majority of traits studied by ecologists and evolu-54 

tionary biologists, making it important to improve our capacity to model their genetic 55 

basis and better understand their eco-evolutionary dynamics. Examples of such traits 56 

cover most aspects of the morphology ( e.g. , height, weight, colour), behaviour ( e.g. , dis-57 

persal, cooperation) and life histories ( e.g. , egg number, lifespan) of organisms. As such, 58 

quantitative traits play a fundamental role in how we conceptualize and investigate a 59 

species’ ability to adapt to its environment and evolve. They are similarly important for 60 

human health and agriculture, where diseases like Alzheimer, schizophrenia or diabetes 61

Understanding the genetic basis and evolution of quantitative traits in natural popu-63 

lations has long been a central goal of evolutionary biology. Quantitative genetics theory, 64 

building on the work of Fisher and Wright, provided a powerful analytical framework 65 

relating phenotypic evolution to the underlying genetic components under selection, drift, 66 

mutation, and gene flow (reviewed in Walsh and Lynch, 2018). However, these models 67 

mostly assume additive gene action, equilibrium conditions, and simple population struc-68 

ture, assumptions that enabled major theoretical advances, but which limit the range 69 

of questions that can be addressed analytically. In reality, the genetic architecture of 70 

complex traits involves non-additive effects: dominance, epistasis, and pleiotropy, which 71 

are widespread properties of quantitative trait loci (Mackay et al., 2009; Pickrell et al., 72 

2016; Mackay and Anholt, 2024), and whose interactions shape trait evolvability in ways 73 

that additive models cannot fully capture (Hansen and Pelabon, 2021). Moreover, many 74 

pressing questions in evolutionary biology concern inherently non-equilibrium situations. 75 

Species responding to rapid environmental change, experiencing range shifts, or recover-76 

ing from demographic bottlenecks are subject to complex feedbacks between demographic 77 

and evolutionary processes, called eco-evolutionary dynamics, where population growth, 78 

spatial structure, life history, and adaptive trait evolution are tightly coupled. In such 79 

settings, stochastic processes, particularly genetic drift and demographic stochasticity in 80 

small or fragmented populations, interact with selection and gene flow in ways that chal-81 

lenge analytical treatment. Forward-in-time, individual-based stochastic simulations ad-82 

dress these limitations by explicitly tracking genotypes, phenotypes, and the demographic 83 

fates of populations through realistic life cycles, capturing the non-linear and stochastic 84 

dynamics that analytical models often simplify away. In practice, such simulations serve 85 

three broad purposes: testing and expanding theoretical predictions beyond the range 86 

of analytical models, generating realistic patterns of genetic variation as benchmarks for 87 

genomic inference methods, and building complex eco-evolutionary scenarios to evaluate 88 

hypotheses against empirical data. 89 

Nemo was developed to meet these needs, proposing models that encompass the ge-90 

nomic to the geographic scales of genetic and environmental details. Nemo 2.4 accommo-91 

dates models requiring different degrees of genetic realism, from few unlinked multi-allelic 92 

quantitative loci (QTL) to many di-allelic quantitative trait nucleotides (QTN) set on 93 

a genetic map, with flexible genotype-phenotype (G-P) maps incorporating dominance, 94 

epistasis, and pleiotropy. QTL-based traits efficiently model eco-evolutionary dynamics 95 

in large populations where fitness depends on the match between traits and local optima, 96 

as illustrated by studies of alpine species’ range dynamics under changing climatic con-97 

ditions in existing populations (Cotto et al., 2017), the feedback of phenotypic plasticity 98 

on genetic divergence between populations (Schmid and Guillaume, 2017), or evolution-99 

ary rescue (Schmid et al., 2022). Models requiring finer genetic detail benefit from a 100 

large number of QTNs placed on chromosomal recombination maps, enabling ground-101

The quantitative trait model would be incomplete without accounting for environmen-135 

tal effects. Nemo 2.4 models phenotypic values that can be affected by both random 136 

environmental variation and deterministic environmental reaction norms. Phenotypic 137 

values can thus change randomly and plastically depending on the natal (developmental 138 

plasticity) or lifetime (labile plasticity) environments. In addition, in Nemo 2.4 the slope 139 

of a reaction norm can be tied to a quantitative trait and evolve, making plasticity an 140 

evolvable trait. These elements allow for in-depth modelling of quantitative traits with 141

and their evolving threshold traits effectively allow for the modelling of polyphenism and 145 

its evolution. Nemo 2.4 thus proposes the largest variety of quantitative trait architecture 146 

models available to date, and is an improvement over software derived from the original 147 

publication (Guillaume and Rougemont, 2006), including quantiNEMO (Neuenschwander 148 

et al., 2008, 2019) and Nemo-age (Cotto et al., 2020). 149 

Program architecture 150 

The backbone of the program is the meta-population, a container of sub-populations 151 

(patches) containing individuals and their genetic elements. Nemo 2.4 then uniquely 152 

proposes a flexible and modular design organized around the trait and life-cycle-event 153 

(LCE) modules, that are optional and can be freely assembled. The traits encode dif-154 

ferent genotype-phenotype mappings, including quantitative trait loci, neutral loci, dele-155 

terious loci, and pairwise incompatibility loci. Each of them are encoded by different 156

Interface and I/O 196 

The user interface is a simple text file containing parameters and values for chosen mod-197 

ules, which is passed as an argument to Nemo on the command line. To handle large 198 

parameter grid sets, we developed a mini language that allows encoding hundreds of sim-199 

ulations within single configuration files (see Supplementary Example 1.11). The resulting 200 

simulations can be distributed across CPUs, either locally or on HPC infrastructures. For 201 

job submission in large simulation campaigns, we developed nemosub , a helper program 202 

that uses the same configuration file as Nemo and automatically submits each simulation 203 

as an independent job allocation to computer clusters. In addition, Nemo natively sup-204 

ports running multiple replicates of the same simulation in parallel via its MPI layer (see 205 

Supplementary Examples, and online documentation). 206 

In output, Nemo can write many component-specific files containing genotype and 207 

phenotype information in various formats (for quantitative traits: PLINK .ped file, SNP 208 

genotypes in 0/1/2 format), allelic values and frequencies, and summary statistics com-209 

puted during a simulation run. Files can contain trait-specific information of sampled 210 

individuals or the whole meta-population. Nemo also exports and imports snapshots of 211 

the whole meta-population in binary format, which is useful as backups or to start a 212 

simulation from a burn-in (see Supplementary Examples 1.9-10). 213 

Implementation of the quantitative trait 214 

The quantitative trait component in Nemo 2.4 implements two paradigms of the genetic 215 

architecture of quantitative traits: multi-allelic quantitative trait loci (QTL) and dial-216 

lelic loci, which best represent quantitative trait nucleotides (QTN). The two models 217 

are toggled using a single parameter, quanti_allele_model with options continuous or 218 

diallelic (and continuous in place , diallelic asymmetrical alternatives, see Sup-219 

plementary Example 1.9 , and Nemo’s manual). 220 

QTL – the continuum-of-allele model 221 

For studies focused on trait evolution and eco-evolutionary dynamics rather than detailed 222 

genomic data, Nemo efficiently models multi-allelic QTL using the continuum-of-allele 223 

model (Crow and Kimura, 1964). This model assumes that mutation can produce an 224 

infinite number of alleles at each locus. Allelic variation at a QTL is generated via a 225 

Gaussian mutational process, defined by its variance (effect size) and initial allelic values. 226 

The number of segregating alleles depends on the balance of evolutionary forces, with 227 

QTL becoming near-diallelic under strong selection or low mutation rates and highly 228 

multi-allelic under weak selection or high mutation rates (see e.g., Chebib and Guillaume, 229

QTN – di-allelic model 251 

Chromosome-scale dense genetic data can be more easily modelled with QTNs using the 252 

diallelic locus model. In this model, the allelic effects and map positions are specified for 253 

each locus. Mutations then land at pre-defined locations on the genome, with back mu-254 

tations allowed (i.e., each mutation event flips between the two pre-defined allelic values). 255 

By default, the additive allelic effects ai are set symmetrically around zero as ±ai at each 256 

locus, but any two values can be passed. For instance, the diallelic_asymmetrical 257 

model encodes allele values {0, b }, with 0 as the default ancestral state, and b the derived 258 

state provided in input. The diallelic model allows for better monitoring of the effect of 259 

recurrent mutations at specific rather than random locations in the genome, easing the 260 

distinction between adaptive and non-adaptive genomic regions. 261

Recombination 327 

Variation in recombination rates among loci and along chromosomes is ubiquitous in 328 

nature and a central feature of most modern forward-time IBS software. Nemo 2.4 imple-329 

ments a multi-trait recombination or genetic map on which all mappable genetic elements 330 

can be placed, including QTL and QTNs. Several options exist in Nemo to set the locus 331 

positions either randomly, regularly, or following existing empirical genetic maps. By de-332 

fault, no genetic map is built and loci are independent, with a recombination rate of 0.5. 333 

Alternatively, genetic maps can be composed of chromosomes of varying size where locus 334

Heritability 353 

To simulate traits with heritability less than 1, environmental variance ( VE ) can be added 354 

to genotypic values when calculating phenotypes. This is achieved using the parameter 355 

quanti_environmental_variance , which specifies trait-specific VE values. Phenotypes 356 

are then computed by adding a normally distributed deviate with mean 0 and variance VE357 

to the final genotypic value (including potential dominance and epistasis). The narrow-358 

sense heritability is defined as h2 = VA/V P where VP = VA +VI +VE is the total phenotypic 359 

variance, VA is the additive genetic variance and VI is the interaction variance due to 360 

dominance and epistasis. During a simulation, the heritability h2 varies stochastically 361 

around its expected value. A specific h2 value can be reached by setting VE when VA362 

is approximately known, such as after a burn-in simulation (note that if VI̸ = 0, then 363 

adding VE to the genotypic value changes broad-sense heritability, H2 = VG/V P )(see 364 

Supplementary Example 1.8). 365 

Nemo 2.4 newly introduces a method to maintain constant h2 during simulations by 366 

adjusting VE as VA evolves. This approach works best for purely additive traits with 367 

precisely computed VA. For traits with non-additive effects (e.g., dominance or epistasis), 368 

broad-sense heritability ( H2) can be set instead (Falconer and Mackay, 1996). Heritability 369 

parameters are controlled via the quanti modifier LCE (see Manual section 5.10). Main-370 

taining constant heritability is useful when comparing crossing design scenarios or testing 371 

statistical methods with traits that have similar properties (e.g., Fraimout et al., 2024). 372

Crossing design 416 

Quantitative genetics seeks to dissect phenotypic variation into its genetic and environ-417 

mental components to understand trait inheritance and predict their responses to selec-418 

tion. The methodology relies on controlled crosses in a common environment to produce 419 

individuals with known genetic relationships (Falconer and Mackay, 1996). Nemo 2.4 420 

implements the cross LCE to model simple crossing designs with half-sib/full-sib crosses 421 

within patches (see Supplementary Example 1.7), as well as between-patch hybrids to 422 

estimate heterosis. A cross followed by dispersal and selection can simulate a reciprocal 423 

transplant to assess local adaptation. The cross LCE also accepts a pedigree file as in-424 

put information, enhancing the toolkit available to quantitative geneticists to model trait 425 

inheritance on known pedigrees from monitored natural populations or to encode more 426 

complex breeding designs than implemented with the set of basic parameters in the cross 427 

LCE (see Supplementary Example 1.8). Simulating the inheritance of quantitative traits, 428 

and other genetic elements, on a known pedigree can help assess the accuracy of statistical 429 

methods (see e.g., Nietlisbach et al., 2017). 430

Comparison with other software 464 

Nemo and quantiNemo are the two main IBM software proposing native implementa-465 

tions of quantitative traits. Yet, they differ markedly in their allelic models. Whilst 466 

Nemo proposes di-allelic and continuous models, quantiNemo proposes a discrete K-allele 467 

model where up to 256 allelic values per locus can be specified. It then approximates 468 

the continuum-of-allele model by discretizing normally distributed allelic values over the 469 

number of alleles chosen with a default span of ±20 α (α2 = mutational variance). While 470 

this procedure doesn’t fully capture the tails of the normal distribution, it provides good 471 

approximation of the trait dynamics with 256 alleles (Fig. S6). A direct comparison is 472 

also possible with SLiM, one of the most widely used forward-time IBMs. Because quan-473 

titative traits are not built-in in SLiM, a fixed QTL or di-allelic QTN panel must be 474 

emulated in script, and as each locus is carried by individually tracked mutations, both 475 

memory use and runtime grow with the number of segregating loci. To quantify this, 476 

we benchmarked the three programs on an identical Gaussian stabilizing-selection model 477 

in one WF population. The three models provide identical equilibrium results for mean 478 

phenotype and additive genetic variance for both 100 unlinked QTL and 1000 di-allelic 479 

QTNs linked on a genetic map with five chromosomes (Fig. S6). They, however, perform 480 

very differently (Fig. 5), with Nemo being the fastest in all configurations and leanest in 481 

most cases. SLiM, being a “sparse” data simulator, is particularly less efficient at mod-482 

elling fix sets of di-allelic QTNs, where its memory use and runtime are far beyond Nemo’s 483 

(Fig. 5B, S5). This is the very regime in which Nemo’s dense, bit-wise QTN model is 484 

most efficient. quantiNemo does worst memory-wise than the other software, especially 485 

for linked QTNs, for unclear reasons (with ∼4.5 GB RAM for N = 10000, L = 5000, 486 

vs. ∼51 MB for Nemo and ∼679 MB for SLiM, see Fig. S5, note that Nemo uses least 487 

memory even with byte-wise QTN data type). Since SLiM stores mutation effect in a 488 

single object per mutation, its memory consumption for QTL is less than Nemo’s, which 489 

stores floating-point allele values in each individual (Fig. 5A, S4). 490

This new version of Nemo provides highly optimized, native implementations of complex 492 

genetic architectures and complex life cycles, thereby meeting the current and future 493 

needs of evolutionary quantitative genetics. The software’s rigorous validation against 494 

theoretical expectations, its memory-efficient bit-wise data structures, and its modular 495 

Life Cycle Event system make it a robust instrument for high-performance simulations of 496 

polygenic adaptation. Specifically, the native implementation of the continuum-of-alleles 497 

and di-allelic models, with matrices for variable pleiotropy, multi-trait dominance, and 498 

built-in Hansen–Wagner multilinear epistasis, makes this framework particularly powerful. 499 

Nemo 2.4’s genetic data architecture permits two main domains of application, each 500 

with a distinct data structure. The QTL continuum-of-allele model is best suited when 501 

trait dynamics are more important than fine-grained genomic data generation. A typical 502 

model based on this architecture would implement 10 to 100 unlinked QTL with various 503 

degree of pleiotropy to model eco-evolutionary dynamics in complex demographic and 504 

spatial settings, for instance to understand species’ range shifts under climate change 505 

in multiple phenotypic dimensions (Cotto et al., 2017) or with the evolution of reaction 506 

norms (Schmid et al., 2019). The continuous model imposes a high memory cost for large 507 

numbers of QTL ( L > 1000) and limits its usefulness in a genomic context. The second 508 

domain of application relies on the bit-wise genetic data architecture and applies best to 509 

the modelling of dense genetic maps with reduced recombination at the chromosome level. 510 

It allows for the simulation of very large numbers of di-allelic genetic markers (QTN) to 511 

generate genomic-scale data with empirically estimated recombination rate variation along 512 

chromosomes. The di-allelic model is thus ideal to investigate genomic signals of selection 513 

on polygenic traits when coupled with densely distributed neutral markers (Kemppainen 514 

and Guillaume, 2026). At the phenotypic level, QTL-based traits have no imposed limit to 515 

their possible values, since the continuum-of-allele model allows accruing allelic values over 516 

evolutionary time, unless specified otherwise (i.e., with allele model continous in place ). 517 

In contrast, QTN-based traits have clearly defined phenotypic boundaries set by the sum 518 

of allele effects. 519 

Nemo’s data architecture achieves high throughput for quantitative traits because it 520 

naturally accommodates the dense genetic architectures typical of polygenic adaptation, 521 

where thousands of alleles fluctuate at intermediate frequencies. As described above, the 522 

sparse approach used by other simulators tracks only segregating mutations, which is effi-523 

cient when few variants segregate but slows down computation as their number increases 524 

(Matthey-Doret, 2021, see also SLiM benchmarks in Figures 5 and S5). The sparse ap-525 

proach additionally imposes a heavy computational load when calculating genotypic or 526 

phenotypic values across thousands of mutations, unless specific optimizations are im-527 

plemented. This computational cost increases further when alleles at specific loci have 528 

dominance or epistatic effects, particularly when phenotype calculations rely on scripted 529

When choosing a simulation tool, performance, ease of use, and flexibility are usu-537 

ally considered together, and often trade-off with each other. While SLiM offers simple 538 

parametrisation of population genetics models based on the infinite-site model with high 539 

performance and high scripting flexibility, it is not well geared to model polygenic traits 540 

the way Nemo does, as exemplified by our benchmark. Similar endpoint results can be 541 

obtained but at a higher computational and scripting price for SLiM. Scripting-based 542 

simulation software may suffer from lower computational efficiency when large parts of 543 

the process are based on interpreted rather than pre-compiled code, for instance for phe-544 

notype calculations involving non-additive interactions across many loci. That said, the 545 

differences in performance between SLiM and Nemo that we have shown are more likely 546 

caused by the underlying difference in data structure between the “sparse” and “dense” 547 

genetic architectures than by the execution of interpreted code. Sparse models are gener-548 

ally better suited for population genetics than quantitative genetics models, where Nemo 549 

is a strong contender. For ease of use, Nemo facilitates model implementation through a 550 

set of standardized parameters, and pre-compiled code. This reduces the risk of coding 551 

error when scripting large and complex models and ensures the consistency of model out-552 

comes necessary for standardization and comparison across studies, since each scripted 553 

implementation may differ in subtle, sometimes undetected ways. The flexibility in Nemo 554 

comes from its modular design and an extended set of features: the metapopulation back-555 

bone, the LCEs, and the traits (i.e., the different genotype-phenotype-fitness mappings). 556 

Nemo is designed around the metapopulation container and the modular Life Cy-557 

cle Events. Older simulators often enforced a hard-coded succession of events (i.e., 558 

reproduction-selection-migration, as in quantiNEMO; Neuenschwander et al., 2019), a 559 

choice mainly derived from the Wright-Fisher model. In contrast, Nemo allows users to 560 

directly define the sequence of events, a flexibility present since Nemo 2.0 (Guillaume and 561 

Rougemont, 2006). This enables variation of population models with seamless transitions 562 

between soft selection (relative fitness determines reproductive output) and hard selection 563 

(absolute fitness determines patch size), or between Wright-Fisher (soft) and non-Wright-564 

Fisher (hard) models. Nemo 2.4 internally capitalizes on optimisations for the Wright-565 

Fisher population model, activated by a single parameter switch. Non-Wright-Fisher 566 

models, on the other hand allow for a wider variety of population dynamics essential to 567 

eco-evolutionary models with stochasticity. In Nemo, population regulation is explicit 568 

and parametrized as a ceiling model (carrying capacity caps patch size), a Beverton-569

a common genetic map encompassing multiple chromosomes. This allows researchers 584 

to mix the genetic elements of the available traits–neutral, deleterious, incompatibility 585 

(BDMI), and quantitative trait loci–on a shared map and utilize empirically-determined 586 

recombination maps to generate realistic patterns of polymorphism at the genomic level. 587 

This design enables the study of linkage disequilibrium (LD) between different classes 588 

of genomic elements without the need for elaborated scripting. The shared genetic map 589 

ensures that recombination events affect all traits consistently. This integration is vital for 590 

simulating “hitchhiking” effects (e.g., Kemppainen and Guillaume, 2026), where neutral 591 

diversity is eroded by selection on linked functional loci, or “background selection,” where 592 

purifying selection on deleterious mutations reduces diversity at linked neutral sites (e.g., 593 

Grossen et al., 2020). By treating these elements as distinct modules that interact via 594 

the recombination map, Nemo 2.4 facilitates the generation of synthetic genomic datasets 595 

that mirror the complexity of empirical data used in GWAS (e.g., Chebib and Guillaume, 596 

2021; Kemppainen and Guillaume, 2026). Moreover, several built-in file formats for saving 597 

and retrieving genetic data ensure interoperability with empirical genomics pipelines, in 598 

particular PLINK 1.9 format for GWA and other genomic analyses (Chang et al., 2015). 599

high flexibility in freely assembling existing modules, LCEs and traits, its mini-language 607 

developed for parameter grid specification, and its higher across-the-board computational 608

1.11 Encoding a large sequence of simulations in the configura-912 

tion file with parameter substitution and recall 913 

Many simulations can be encoded in a configuration file when parameters receive mul-914 

tiple values. Such parameters are called sequential parameters in Nemo’s jargon. The 915 

mini language we developed in Nemo allows us to then recall the parameter values using 916 

a substiution string with simple syntax. The substitution string starts with ‘ %‘ symbol 917 

followed by the number of the sequential parameter: %4 refers to the fourth sequential 918 

parameter found in the file. The parameters are ordered in alphabetical order. To facil-919 

itate parameter recall and value assignment, it is possible to define dummy parameters, 920 

assign them multiple values, and recall those values for the relevant parameters in the 921 

file (see below). Furthermore, it is possible to link the values of two or more sequential 922 

parameters when they need to vary in sync, instead of generating the complete set of 923 

combinations of those values. Linked sequential parameter values are encoded in square 924 

brackets: param_1 [v1 v2 v3] and param_2 [a b c] . This setting will generate 3 sim-925 

ulations, while the same parametrization without the bracketing would generate 3 × 3 = 9 926 

simulations. 927 

In addition, it is possible to save parameter values to separate files and provide the 928 

path to the file as argument value when using the ‘ &’ symbol. The example below uses 929 

this feature to separately store very large dispersal matrices, environmental maps (values 930
