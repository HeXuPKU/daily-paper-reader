Abstract 

Distributed multiple testing asks N sites to control a global false discovery rate (FDR) under a tight communication budget. The greedy interval-aggregation algorithm of Pour-naderi and Xiang (2024) solves this asymptotically but can violate FDR ≤ α at finite samples. We trace the violation to a winner’s-curse bias in the selected density statistics, of exact order Θ( m−1/4√log m) at the standard bandwidth ε ≍ m−1/2, with m the total number of p-values in the network. Cross-Fit Greedy Aggregation (CFGA) eliminates the curse by selecting the nested rejection family on one half of each node’s data and scoring it on the other, achieving finite-sample FDR ≤ α when per-node null rates are known; an inflated variant covers the plug-in setting at a vanishing η = 1 /m slack. BONuS-GA instead masks a bag of synthetic uniform nulls calibrated by counting knockoffs, so every p-value serves both selection and inference; a per-node budgeted variant removes all oracle input, controlling FDR ≤ α for any data-independent bag. Aggregating the CFGA folds by e-values over random splits (e-CFGA) removes the Bonferroni factor and averages the split randomness. All variants keep the O(√m log m) communication budget, up to e-CFGA’s 

O( ¯R log m) reporting round; empirically, BONuS-GA dominates at moderate-to-large per-node samples and CFGA with adaptive bandwidth at small ones. 

Keywords: false discovery rate, distributed multiple testing, knockoffs, e-values, com-munication efficiency 

1 Introduction 

1.1 Distributed multiple testing and asymptotic FDR control 

Sensor networks, federated learning systems, scientific collaborations, and large-scale ob-servatories routinely produce hypothesis tests partitioned across sites that cannot freely share their raw data. Centralizing every p-value at one node may be infeasible because of communication budgets, privacy constraints, or sheer volume. The distributed multiple testing problem asks: how should the sites collaborate so that the network as a whole con-trols a global error criterion—most commonly the false discovery rate (FDR) of Benjamini and Hochberg (1995)—at a prescribed level α, while staying within a small communication budget? A line of work starting with the broadcast schemes of Ermis and Saligrama (2010) and continuing through QuTE (Ramdas et al., 2017), its quantized version (Xiang, 2019), and the sample-and-forward method (Pournaderi and Xiang, 2022) has aimed to reproduce the centralized FDR-controlling procedure (typically BH) over a network without transmitting all p-values. A complementary line takes an asymptotic view: rather than insisting that each finite-m trace match the centralized procedure exactly, one designs algorithms that converge in some metric to the centralized rule and proves the FDR bound in the limit.

The greedy aggregation algorithm of Pournaderi and Xiang (2024), the starting point of this paper, is a centerpiece of that line: it uses O(log m) bits per round, attains asymptotic FDR control, and achieves asymptotic power optimality among the class of procedures that reject finite unions of intervals. The present work departs from this asymptotic stance, keeping the algorithm’s communication efficiency while replacing its limiting guarantee with a finite-sample one.

1.3 Why finite-sample control is desirable, and contributions 

An asymptotic FDR bound describes the behaviour of an algorithm as m → ∞ , not at the value of m actually used in a particular run. For any specific application m is finite and the relevant question is whether the procedure controls FDR at the level α for that 

m. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure answers this in the affirmative: at level α, BH satisfies FDR ≤ α m 0/m for every m ≥ 1 under independence of the p-values, and more generally under positive regression dependence (PRDS), a monotone-dependence condition that requires P(P ∈ D | Pi ≤ t) to be non-decreasing in t for every non-decreasing set 

D ⊆ [0 , 1] m and every null index i (Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001). Greedy aggregation does not provide such a guarantee, and we will observe this empirically in the simulations section.

This brings up the following question: can the greedy aggregation algorithm be modified to achieve a finite-sample FDR bound FDR ≤ α for every m ≥ 1, with communication cost matching the original? We answer affirmatively with four contributions. (1) Non-asymptotic analysis of the original greedy. Section 3 pins down a winner’s-curse selection bias as the mechanism behind the finite-sample FDR violations of the original Storey greedy: at the standard bandwidth ε ≍ m−1/2, the order-statistic aver-age ¯hM that drives the FDP estimator carries an upward selection bias of exact order 

m−1/4√log m over the range of selection sizes M specified in Proposition 4—an upper bound for general configurations and every M (Proposition 3) with a matching lower bound over that range under the global null (Proposition 4)—strictly slower than the parametric m−1/2 rate and, unlike the mean-zero (εm )−1/2 sampling fluctuation of an unselected bin, systematically one-sided. Up to a conditional-mean factor made explicit in Section 3, the same bias measures the relative under-count of the predicted null mass 

M m ε against the actual nulls in the selected region; we do not claim a proved rate for the FDR excess at the algorithm’s random stopping index. This identifies the failure mechanism of the original procedure and motivates the constructions that follow. (2) CFGA. Section 4 introduces CFGA: we rank the intervals on a random half of the p-values ( ranking half ) and score the resulting nested regions on the held-out inference half , unioning the two folds at level α/ 2. The cross-fit rule controls FDR ≤ α when the true per-node null rates are known (Theorem 6). In Section 5 an inflated Storey estimator of null rates is used to handle unknown null rates: inflating the cross-fit null-rate estimate by a vanishing Hoeffding margin δ = O p log(2 N m )/m  (at η = 1 /m )yields FDR ≤ α+η at every finite m (Theorem 9), with η = 1 /m . An e-value aggregation of the folds over S random splits (e-CFGA, Theorem 12) removes the Bonferroni-2 factor and averages over the split randomness at no cost to the finite-sample guarantee, at the price of one extra O( ¯R log m)-bit reporting round (Section 6). (3) BONuS-GA: avoiding the split. Section 7 replaces sample splitting altogether by a bag-of-null-statistics (BONuS) construction (Yang et al., 2021): each node generates synthetic Uniform[0 , 1] nulls, masks them among the real p-values, and calibrates the greedy by a counting-knockoff FDP estimate, so that all m p-values are used for both selection and inference. With the bag drawn from the true lifted null law this controls 

FDR ≤ α at every finite m (Theorem 14); a per-node budgeted variant (Theorem 18) removes the oracle entirely—per-node tail calibration with masking-measurable bud-get shares gives FDR ≤ α for any data-independent bag, making the guarantee fully implementable. (4) Adaptive choice of ε. Section 8 selects the interval bandwidth ε by sub-split cross-validation on the ranking half (Algorithm 21). Because the choice depends only on the ranking half, the guarantees above are preserved (Proposition 22); empirically it delivers large power gains where the fixed default ε = α m −1/2 is suboptimal. Section 9 reports a monitoring-network study — a few signal-rich hubs among many quiet sensors — in which the naive decentralized baseline (per-node BH at level α) loses global

1.4 Related work 

The closest distributed-FDR precursor is the sample-and-forward algorithm of Pournaderi and Xiang (2022), by the same authors as the greedy method we build on. Rather than transmitting (quantized) p-values, each node samples its local empirical CDF of p-values at a few points in [0 , α ] and forwards those samples, together with its count m(i), to the center using O(log m) bits per node; the center aggregates them into an approximate pooled CDF bF and computes a BH-type global threshold bτ = sup {t : bF (t) = t/α }. Sample-and-forward is thus designed to approximate the pooled (centralized) BH rejection region over the network: it enjoys finite-sample FDR control under the same PRDS assumption as BH, while its power loss relative to pooled-BH is characterized only asymptotically , under a mixture model. It can be combined with QuTE (Ramdas et al., 2017) to handle general topologies. The greedy aggregation method that we make finite-sample (Section 1.2) pursues a different goal: it does not aim to reproduce BH, but to attain asymptotic power-optimality 

in the network setting, among procedures that reject finite unions of intervals (Pournaderi and Xiang, 2024). For a fixed bandwidth ε it too communicates only O(log m) bits per round; the O(√m log m) total reported in Section 1.2 is not a cost of FDR control but the price of power, arising from the O(1 /ε ) = O(√m) candidate intervals induced by the power-driven choice ε ∝ m−1/2. A final clarification, since both methods involve using “part of the data”: sample-and-forward evaluates the empirical CDF at a few points as a communication-compression device, whereas CFGA randomly splits the hypothesis indices at each node into two folds as a statistical device—cross-fitting that decouples greedy selection from inferential thresholding, which is what upgrades the greedy method’s asymptotic FDR control to a finite-sample guarantee. The two mechanisms are unrelated. The broader literature on distributed multiple testing with FDR control includes the QuTE algorithm (Ramdas et al., 2017), which forwards p-values across a general network, together with communication-efficient variants that avoid transmitting the raw p-values: quantized distributed FDR control (Xiang, 2019), the sample-and-forward method (Pour-naderi and Xiang, 2022), and distribution-free inference over networks (Pournaderi and Xiang, 2023). Related directions include FDR control under Byzantine corruption of the reported p-values (Zhang et al., 2025) and frameworks for spatial multiple testing (Gölz et al., 2022). The greedy aggregation method of Pournaderi and Xiang (2024) that we build on belongs to this line. The theoretical machinery we use—adaptive BH+ with finite-sample FDR control (Storey et al., 2004; Heesen and Janssen, 2016), sample-split selection-inference (Jin and Candès, 2023; Bates et al., 2023), cross-fitting (Chernozhukov et al., 2018; Ren and Barber, 2024; Ren et al., 2023), masked calibration with a bag of synthetic null statistics in the knockoff tradition (Barber and Candès, 2015; Yang et al., 2021), and e-value aggregation (Wang and Ramdas, 2022)—is well-established in non-distributed settings; the contribution here is to adapt it to the interval-based, communication-constrained greedy aggregation procedure.

3 Winner’s-curse bias of the Storey-greedy estimator 

Greedy aggregation (Pournaderi and Xiang, 2024) is a plug-in procedure: at each finite 

m it estimates a per-node grid of interval densities, ranks the intervals across the network by their empirical densities, and stops when its plug-in FDP estimate first crosses α. The asymptotic FDR guarantee of Pournaderi and Xiang (2024, Prop. 4) holds in the limit, when the empirical density estimates concentrate at their population counterparts and the plug-in is consistent. At any finite m those estimates carry noise, and crucially the procedure reuses the same counts both to rank the intervals and to form the FDP estimate. This creates a 

winner’s curse : the greedy admits the intervals whose empirical counts are largest, and the nulls inside those intervals contribute to exactly the same counts the procedure trusts as its calibrator. The failure mechanism admits two equivalent readings, both useful.

6 Derandomized e-value aggregation (e-CFGA) 

CFGA carries two residual costs. The first is the Bonferroni-2 split: each fold is stopped at level α/ 2 and the per-fold rejections are unioned, so a problem with signal in both folds effectively runs the procedure at half the target level. The second is the dependence of the output on a single random split: a different Bernoulli ( 12 ) draw produces a different, correlated rejection set, which is undesirable when the practitioner wants a reproducible decision. Both costs are removed by communicating across folds through generalized e-values

6.4 Choosing the fold level γ

The e-BH cut needs k⋆ ¯e(k⋆) ≥ m/α for at least one k⋆ ≥ 1; if no k satisfies this, e-CFGA makes no rejection. One boundary calculation shows that γ = α leaves no slack in the 

unanimous regime. Consider a stylized setting in which each fold stops with R rejections on its inference half (so ¯V ( cMs,b ) ≈ γR from the stopping rule) and a hypothesis is rejected by its inference fold in every split. Its average e-value is ¯e ≈ msb′ /(1 + γR ) ≈ m/ (2 γR ), and taking k⋆ equal to the 2R such hypotheses across the two folds gives k⋆ ¯e(k⋆) ≈ m/γ : the e-BH condition m/γ ≥ m/α holds exactly at γ = α, with nothing to spare. This calculation does not by itself imply that γ = α must fail under non-unanimous profiles: averaging can also expand the support, and a profile whose splits reject disjoint sets doubles k⋆ while halving ¯e, exactly preserving k⋆ ¯e(k⋆). Whether the cut fires at γ = α therefore depends on the joint rejection profile across splits; in our configurations it collapsed to zero rejections, and we do not attempt to model the required slack. γ = 0 .75 α is an empirical default :

7 BONuS-GA: decoupling by masking instead of splitting 

CFGA and Inflated CFGA both buy the independence of selection from inference by splitting 

the data: the inference half pays for the guarantee by contributing only m/ 2 p-values to the rejection count. We now describe a variant that achieves the same decoupling by masking ,following the bag-of-null-statistics (BONuS) construction of Yang et al. (2021), and uses all 

m p-values for both selection and inference. The idea, due to Barber and Candès (2015) and adapted to multiple testing by Yang et al. (2021), is to generate a bag of synthetic null statistics — “counting knockoffs” — known to be null by construction, hide their real-vs-synthetic identities from the procedure during region selection, and then use the synthetic counts as a calibrator of the unknown null count inside the selected region: eN (Γ) proxies the unobserved V (Γ) . The exactly-uniform null (Assumption 1) makes this the most favourable possible setting for BONuS: the null distribution is known in closed form, so the “bag” is 

generated rather than estimated. 

Three tiers, two with proofs. We present BONuS-GA in three tiers. The oracle tier uses per-node bag-size weights equal to the true null rates π(i) = q(i)r(i)0 ; for it we give a finite-sample FDR proof (Theorem 14). The implementable global-ratio tier replaces the unknown 

r(i)0 by Storey’s plug-in br(i)0 ; it does not inherit a finite-sample proof from Theorem 14 (see Remark 15) and is the highest-power practical variant in the simulations of Section 9. The 

per-node budgeted tier (Section 7.4) replaces the global tail ratio by per-node ratios under per-node budget constraints; it requires no oracle input at all and carries a finite-sample proof (Theorem 18), at a power cost that is negligible at moderate-to-large hubs and visible at small ones. A proof for the global-ratio tier itself remains open.

At moderate-to-large hubs the offsets are negligible and the budgeted rule essentially matches global BONuS-GA at lower FDR, while at small hubs the per-node +1 ’s bite; its empirical behaviour is reported alongside all other methods in every experiment of Section 9, and in the ablation of Table 1. Theorem 18 closes the implementable-tier gap left open by Remark 15: a split-free, fully implementable procedure with exact finite-sample FDR control. 

Remark 19 (Fixed labels suffice) Theorem 18 does not use the random-label model. Its proof conditions on H0 and the non-null points, runs an urn argument within each node, and uses only independence across nodes; no step marginalizes over node labels. Conse-quently the budgeted tier controls FDR ≤ α under the weaker fixed-design assumption that the node labels and null pattern are arbitrary (even adversarial) and the null p-values are mutually independent Uniform[0 , 1] , independent of the non-null p-values. This contrasts with Theorems 6 and 9, whose lifted-space argument genuinely requires the random labels of Assumption 1: under fixed labels the held-out thinning ratios become node-specific and the single supermartingale of Lemma 23 is no longer available (a per-node budgeted CFGA, in the spirit of Algorithm 17, would restore a fixed-label guarantee at the corresponding power cost). 

Remark 20 (Split-free adaptive ε) The bandwidth ε can be tuned without reintroducing a split. Because the masked greedy is driven only by the pooled p-values, any choice of 

> 26 Non-Asymptotic Multiple Testing Over Networks

ε that is a function of the pooled values—for instance the one maximizing a standardized 

χ2 excess of the pooled counts over uniform on [0 , λ ]—is measurable with respect to the masking σ-field Fmask and leaves Theorem 14 intact. Note that the rejection-maximizing sub-split cross-validation of Section 8 is not Fmask -measurable, and hence not available here: a rejection count separates real from synthetic points inside the candidate region—exactly the masked information the knockoff protects—so the split is what licensed that recipe for CFGA. Empirically the pooled-score rule helps precisely where the fixed default ε = α m −1/2

is too conservative: in a side run of the Experiment 4 configuration at mhub = 250 (not shown in the figures) it lifts the implementable variant’s power from 0.60 to 0.77 , but its empirical FDR rises to ≈ 0.22 , marginally above the target α = 0 .2—a reminder that the implementable tier carries no finite-sample guarantee and that data-driven tuning consumes whatever conservatism slack the fixed default leaves. It is therefore a targeted tool for the small-sample regime, to be used with a validity margin in mind, rather than the universal default that adaptive ε is for cross-fit CFGA.

Caveat under within-node dependence. Proposition 22 assumes within-node inde-pendence (Assumption 1). The σ(D1)-measurability argument protects the validity of any data-driven ε, but it does not protect against violations of Assumption 1 itself. Under strong within-node positive dependence the Storey null-rate estimate becomes anti-conservative, and because Algorithm 21 selects the ε that maximizes the validation rejection count, it preferentially picks the value that most fully exploits this optimism. Experiment 3 (Sec-tion 9.4) makes this concrete: under within-node AR(1) correlation the Storey +adaptive-ε

variant’s FDR climbs to 0.32 at ρ = 0 .8, whereas the fixed-ε Storey variant—which does not tune ε to the data—and the Inflated adaptive variant (FDR ≈ 0.04 across the whole range) both stay controlled. Confining the correlation to the hubs removes the runaway entirely (FDR 0.087 –0.094 across ρ ≤ 0.8; Section 9.4), consistent with this mechanism: it is the dependent quiet-node mass, whose Storey and validation statistics the adaptive rule trusts, that fuels the over-rejection. We therefore recommend a fixed ε, or the Inflated adaptive variant, whenever strong within-node dependence is suspected. 

8.2 Computational cost 

Algorithm 21 runs greedy aggregation T times per fold. Each invocation costs O(√m log m)

communication and O(m) local computation; the inner cross-validation step adds no com-munication beyond the inner greedy runs. For CFGA this gives a 2T multiplicative factor on the total cost relative to non-adaptive CFGA. With T = 7 in our experiments, this is a 14 × overhead, which remains O(√m log m) asymptotically. The trade-off (Section 9, Experiments 2 and 4) is a substantial power gain, especially at moderate m.

9.8 Take-aways 

Table 2 condenses the experiments into a practitioner’s guide; the bullets below give the evidence. • Local BH (α) is not globally valid. The headline failure (Experiment 1): unioning per-node BH rejections inflates global FDR past α as quiet nodes accumulate. Local BH (α/N ) restores validity but is nearly powerless. The CFGA/Inflated family controls global FDR and keeps power. • Original Greedy exceeds α at finite m. Across Experiments 1, 2, 4 and 5 its FDR sits above target; the over-shoot is the slowly-vanishing selection optimism of §3, not a bug, and does not disappear as the hubs grow. • Adaptive ε is the recommended power knob, with one caveat. It rescues the small-hub, weak-signal and small-α regimes (Experiments 2, 4, 6), but under strong within-node dependence the un-inflated Storey +adaptive variant can over-reject (Experiment 3); the Inflated adaptive variant is the robust default there. • Inflated CFGA +Storey is valid throughout, and its margin has a real power price. The adaptive-ε Inflated variant stays at ≈ 0.04 –0.06 FDR in every experiment— including under AR(1) dependence at ρ = 0 .8, where it removes the un-inflated adaptive 

> 36 Non-Asymptotic Multiple Testing Over Networks

variant’s runaway—at a power cost of roughly 0.08 –0.10 below CFGA +Storey at the base configuration, growing in the small-hub and heavy-tailed regimes (Experiments 4–5). The fixed-ε Inflated variant is so conservative that it often makes no rejections (it requires 

mhub ≳ 2000 or few quiet nodes to fire); Inflated CFGA should therefore always be run with the adaptive bandwidth. This is consistent with Theorem 9: the guarantee is bought by a margin δ that scales as plog(2 N m )/m b at η = 1 /m , which is not small in networks with hundreds of nodes and moderate mb.• BONuS-GA dominates at moderate-to-large hubs but not at small hubs. Across all six experiments the two provable imple-mentable tiers run within striking distance of their heuristic counterparts: Budgeted BONuS-GA gives up 0.01 –0.06 power against global-ratio BONuS-GA (at uniformly lower FDR), and inflated e-CFGA dominates Bonferroni-2 Inflated CFGA +adaptive at almost every operating point—most visibly at small hubs ( 0.64 vs 0.19 at mhub = 250 )—while staying at FDR ≤ 0.075 everywhere, including under AR(1) dependence at ρ = 0 .8 with a data-driven bandwidth. The remaining price of an exact finite-sample certificate is concentrated in two regimes: very small hubs and heavy tails. 

10 Discussion 

Cross-fitting (CFGA) and masking (BONuS-GA) both deliver finite-sample FDR control for greedy aggregation while preserving the original algorithm’s O(√m log m) total communi-cation budget. CFGA is a thin wrapper around the existing procedure — the proof reduces in the lifted (node-label, p-value) space to a well-established adaptive-BH supermartingale lemma — and the inflated Storey variant lifts the guarantee to the plug-in setting at a vanishing FDR slack η = 1 /m ; its inflation margin δ = O p log(2 N m )/m b

 is a real con-servatism cost in networks with many nodes, so we pair it with the adaptive bandwidth 

> 37 Pournaderi

in practice. BONuS-GA, under oracle weights, uses every real p-value for both selection and inference by replacing the held-out half with a masking-measurable bag of synthetic uniform nulls; in our simulations this recovers the constant-factor power that sample split-ting forfeits. Empirically the two methods are complementary: BONuS-GA dominates at moderate-to-large per-node sample sizes, while CFGA with adaptive ε is the higher-power choice when individual hubs are small (Section 9, Experiment 4). Four directions remain open. Within-node dependence. Both our finite-sample proofs and the asymptotic analysis of Pournaderi and Xiang (2024) require within-node independence. Classical BH controls FDR under positive regression dependence (Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001); extending CFGA’s and BONuS-GA’s guarantees to PRDS or weakly-dependent se-quences would close that gap. Empirically the fixed-ε methods are remarkably robust to AR(1) correlation up to ρ = 0 .8 (Experiment 3), but a proof under dependence is open, and the inflated margin’s resilience there should be read as an empirical observation rather than a dependence correction. Removing the CFGA offset. The thinning identity behind the lifted-space argument is exact whenever the relevant masses are positive, which suggests the additive offset in the CFGA acceptance rule may be removable under careful zero-mass conventions; since additive offsets carry a measurable small-sample power price elsewhere in the paper, this is worth settling. Sharper e-CFGA. The e-value aggregation of Section 6 removes the Bonferroni-2 factor at a fixed fold level γ; characterizing the power-optimal γ

(our boundary calculation of §6 shows only that γ = α leaves no slack in the unanimous regime; γ = 0 .75 α is an empirical default) and extending the e-value route to the small-hub regime, where it currently trails CFGA +adaptive ε, are natural refinements. Closing the re-maining BONuS-GA gap. Theorem 18 now gives a fully implementable, split-free procedure with exact finite-sample FDR control and no oracle input, and at moderate-to-large hubs it essentially matches the global-ratio heuristic. What remains open is the small-hub regime: the scalar-sum per-node rule of Remark 16 (which lets active nodes pool their slack and a single SeqStep + offset, reaching power 0.787 at mhub = 250 against the budgeted rule’s 

0.528 ) still lacks a proof; a finite-sample bound for it — or a budget-sharing scheme that provably recovers the pooled slack — would absorb both regimes under one analysis, as the ablation of Table 1 suggests.
