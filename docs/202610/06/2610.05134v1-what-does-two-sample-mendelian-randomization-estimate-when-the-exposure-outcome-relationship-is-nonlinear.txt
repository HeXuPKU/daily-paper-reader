# What does two-sample Mendelian randomization estimate when the exposure–outcome relationship is nonlinear? 

Eric J. Tchetgen Tchetgen 

Department of Statistics and Data Science, The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania

Abstract 

Two-sample Mendelian randomization with summary statistics has become a popular MR design in Epi-demiology. Its ratio estimate has an average causal effect interpretation only under strong untestable assumptions including linearity. We derive what the per-variant ratio, and the pooled and robust estima-tors built from it, estimate when a continuous exposure has a nonlinear effect, under two conditions on how genetic variation and unmeasured factors shape exposure: no unmeasured common effect modifier, and an exposure control function. Under either condition, each variant’s ratio is a weighted average of the slope of the dose-response curve, with weights that depend on how that variant moves the distribu-tion of exposure and that are nonnegative when the exposure distributions at different genotypes do not cross. Variants that shift everyone’s exposure equally target nearly the same quantity; variants acting at particular doses or on the spread of exposure target different ones, some outside the range of any indi-vidual effect. Pooled estimates therefore have no fixed interpretation, heterogeneity tests and MR-Egger misread nonlinearity as pleiotropy, and summary statistics cannot reveal the problem, even when the dose-response curve is identifiable from individual-level data. For continuous exposures, two-sample estimates rest on a linearity assumption that should be stated and defended; individual-level exposure data resolve the problem and should be prioritized. 

Key words: control function; dose-response; instrumental variables; Mendelian randomization; stochastic monotonicity; summary statistics; two-sample design. 

# 1 Introduction 

Mendelian randomization uses genetic variants as instruments for a modifiable exposure [1, 2]. The two-sample design [3–6] takes the variant–exposure association from one genome-wide association study and the variant–outcome association from another, each as a published coefficient, and reports their ratio. With public repositories and automated software [7], anyone with internet access can run such an analysis [8], and the literature has grown accordingly: from 61 PubMed-indexed papers in 2010, to over 18,000 PubMed records related to Mendelian randomization published since January 2024 to the time of writing [9–11]. Field reviews find recent studies to be two-sample summary-data analyses almost without exception [12, 1arXiv:2610.05134v1 [stat.ME] 4 Oct 2026 13], and the design’s quality problems have prompted calls for editors to reject stand-alone two-sample papers [8, 10]. This paper is about a different, substantive problem: what the ratio means. The ratio of a variant– outcome to a variant–exposure coefficient, the Wald or two-stage least squares estimand, has a causal inter-pretation when the outcome responds linearly to the exposure. The textbook justification assumes a constant linear effect [14, 15]. Identification results that let the effect vary between individuals, for a binary exposure and instrument by Wang and Tchetgen Tchetgen [16] and Cui and Tchetgen Tchetgen [17], and for contin-uous ones by Hartwig et al. [18, 19], retain linearity within the individual. For a binary exposure it costs nothing: a function of a two-valued exposure is automatically linear in it, so the Wald ratio and its binary-exposure identification results are immune to the problems discussed here. For a continuous exposure it is a genuine restriction, doubtful for alcohol, adiposity, blood pressure, sleep, vitamin D and many biomarkers, which is why a literature on nonlinear Mendelian randomization exists [20–24], one that requires individual-level data and has itself proved fragile [10, 25–27]. We ask a prior question: when the dose-response is nonlinear and the standard two-sample pipeline is run anyway, what is estimated, and what do its diagnostics mean? We show that a single valid variant’s ratio remains a causal quantity, a weighted average of the slope of the dose-response curve, through two separate lenses resting on distinct structural assumptions: no unmeasured common effect modifier (NUCEM), a restriction on how unmeasured heterogeneity in the outcome and in the first stage covary, and an exposure control function (ECoF), the existence of a function of exposure and instrument that controls confounding, of which the triangular model of Imbens and Newey [28] and the familiar location-shift first stage are examples. In both, the weight is set by how that variant moves the exposure distribution, and in both it is nonnegative exactly when the distribution functions of exposure at different genotypes do not cross, a comonotonicity condition that dispersive variants violate. Variants that move the exposure distribution differently therefore estimate different things, even in models where the dose-response curve itself is point identified from individual-level data: what two-sample Mendelian randomization loses is not identification but the meaning of the aggregate. We identify what individual-level exposure data make possible and argue that, for continuous exposures, such data should again be the standard. Proofs are in the Appendix; R code reproducing the illustration and all numerical checks is available from the author.

Both lenses therefore ask that higher values of the variant shift the exposure distribution upward in the sense of first-order stochastic dominance: the exposure distribution functions at the three genotypes, or along a score, do not cross . # 5 Summary statistics cannot attribute the heterogeneity 

Every pipeline diagnostic tests whether the ˆ θ j are homogeneous. Nonlinearity makes them heterogeneous with valid instruments. Can summary statistics tell which?

These statistics are cheap, estimable at scale [34], and almost never reported.

# 7 Discussion 

For a continuous exposure with a nonlinear effect, a single valid variant’s Wald ratio is a causal quantity through either of two lenses, no unmeasured common effect modifier, a restriction on the covariation of latent heterogeneities in the outcome and the first stage, or an exposure control function, under which the confounder is observable and the weights are each person’s responsiveness to the instrument. In both the quantity is a weighted average of the slope of the dose-response, proper exactly when the exposure distri-bution functions at different genotypes do not cross. But the quantity belongs to the variant as much as to the exposure–outcome relation, and that is what the multi-variant machinery of two-sample Mendelian randomization cannot appropriately account for, or absorb. Each variant estimates its own functional of the dose-response curve; the pooled estimand changes with the instrument set; heterogeneity statistics and the MR-Egger intercept respond to nonlinearity exactly as to pleiotropy; the median and mode return one variant’s estimand without saying which; and none of this is detectable from summary statistics. The loss is not one of identification: in the additive-density and ECoF models the dose-response curve is point iden-tified from individual-level data, and under a hybrid design its slope is identified from the pipeline’s own outcome statistics together with individual-level exposure data. What the summary-data aggregate lacks is a meaning, not a target. A warning follows for NOSH [18], the most careful existing justification of the ratio for a continuous exposure: it presumes linearity, which is unlikely for many exposures, and it is the linearity, not the independence of heterogeneities, that makes every variant estimate the same thing. NUCEM and an ECoF address the resulting difficulty of interpreting two-stage least squares to some extent, by saying what a single ratio is without linearity, but they do not rescue the aggregate. There is a benign case, which we have stated: when every variant shifts every person’s exposure equally, the pipeline estimates, approximately, the average of individual slopes at individual doses. But that is a statement about the first stage, and the first stage is precisely where genetic epidemiology has documented 11 pervasive heterogeneity; the documentation of variance loci is evidence of crossing distribution functions. For such instruments the non-benign case prevails. Stender et al. [10] advise editors to reject stand-alone two-sample papers because they add nothing to what can be looked up online. Our results add a substantive reason for continuous exposures: what is looked up need not have a causal meaning. We recommend, first, that for a continuous exposure a pooled two-sample estimate be reported with an explicit, defended linearity assumption, as STROBE-MR already requires for the core assumptions [47]. Second, that heterogeneity statistics and the MR-Egger intercept not be read as pleiotropy without an argument that the dose-response is linear or the first stages additive; “robust” sensitivity analyses offer no protection against nonlinearity. Third, that exposure consortia release variant associations with a few transforms or thresholds of the exposure, at negligible cost, so that analysts can recover the weights and check for crossing. Fourth, and chiefly, that individual-level data be reprioritized: a hybrid design with individual-level exposure data recovers the weights and, under NUCEM, the dose-response slope, and a one-sample design permits direct examination of the genotype–exposure relation and control-function estimation of the curve. The advantages that made two-sample designs attractive, larger samples, less winner’s curse, weak-instrument bias toward the null [3, 48, 49], were established under a linear model in which every variant estimates the same thing. Once that is in doubt, the economy of summary statistics is bought at the price of not knowing what is being estimated. A final observation. The recent controversy over nonlinear Mendelian randomization [10, 25–27] con-cerns bias in stratification methods when the genetic effect on exposure is not constant across strata. That is the same first-stage heterogeneity that, here, makes variant-specific weights differ and summary-data aggre-gates uninterpretable. The two problems have one source, and the remedy for both begins with examining the genotype–exposure relation in individual-level data rather than reducing it to a single coefficient. The two stratification methods of nonlinear Mendelian randomization are, in fact, stratifications on the two expo-sure control functions used here. The residual method [20] stratifies on A − μ(Z), an ECoF only under the location shift, which is the constant-genetic-effect assumption; the rank-preserving assumption of the doubly ranked method [24, 25], that the ordering of counterfactual exposures does not change with the instrument, is exactly the z-invariant ordering under which FA|Z (A | Z) is an ECoF, and its strata are coarsened levels of that function. Result 2 applied within strata shows that each stratum-specific ratio is a responsiveness-weighted average of the causal slope over the exposures its members take across instrument values, proper only when the conditional quantile curves do not cross; the heterogeneity in genetic associations across doubly ranked strata documented in ref. [25] is heterogeneity in that responsiveness.
