Abstract 
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have greatly advanced the discovery 
of genetic variants underlying complex traits and diseases. Yet in heterogeneous 
populations, existing GWAS strategies typically either pool all individuals under an 
assumption of population homogeneity or perform meta-analysis across predefined 
subgroups, both of which are limited when latent genetic heterogeneity attenuates 
subgroup-specific effects and masks true associations or subgroup labels are imprecise. 
Here we present UCALM, a unified unsupervised framework that infers genetically 
homogeneous subgroups directly from the data and integrates subgroup-specific 
GWAS with a novel layered meta-analysis method to capture both shared and 
subgroup-specific association signals. Through extensive simulations and analyses of 
large-scale human and livestock cohorts, including the UK Biobank (n ≈ 487,000) and 
a heterogeneous pig cohort (n ≈ 85,000), we demonstrate that UCALM substantially 


alleviated the mean genomic inflation across 24 UK Biobank traits to 1.17 compared 
with 1.43 for GLM and 1.37 for LDAK-KVIK, and further revealed 74 loci in the pig 
cohort that were previously obscured by conventional approaches. Our results establish 
a robust and broadly applicable strategy for association mapping in structured 
populations and improve the resolution of genetic signals across diverse species. 
Keywords: GWAS; Population stratification; Genetic heterogeneity; Adaptive 
clustering; Meta-analysis. 
Introduction 
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have played a significant role in 
revealing the genetic architecture of complex traits in humans, livestock and plants, 
identifying thousands of loci that contribute to phenotypic variation1-5. The expansion 
of biobanks and breeding programs has enabled the integration of datasets comprising 
hundreds of thousands of individuals from diverse genetic backgrounds6-12. Although 
multi-population cohorts can increase power to discover novel associations and refine 
causal variants, their utilization is complicated by population stratification and cryptic 
relatedness13,14,

Conventional GWAS strategies typically follow one of two paradigms: pooled analysis 
of all individuals under an assumption of population homogeneity, in which Principal 
Component Analysis (PCA) and Genomic Relationship Matrix (GRM) are used to 
correct for broad population structure and cryptic relatedness15; or meta-analysis across 
predefined subgroups, which relies heavily on prior subgroup labels that are sometimes 
unavailable, often subjectively defined and may not accurately reflect the underlying 
genetic structure16. However, the former may fail to capture fine-scale, non-linear 
genomic substructures, potentially leading to diluted subgroup-specific effects and 
masked association signals17, whereas the latter may leave residual stratification that 
induces spurious associations. 
Accurate statistical inference of population components directly from the data can 
provide an effective strategy for dissecting the hidden genetic architecture of complex 


populations, but the direct application of clustering algorithms to raw genotype data is 
fundamentally hindered by the curse of dimensionality inherent to sequencing data18,19. 
Existing approaches for population structure inference and genetic clustering include 
distance-based clustering methods, such as k-means, and model-based ancestry 
inference tools, such as STRUCTURE, ADMIXTURE and fastSTRUCTURE20-22. 
Although widely used, these methods typically require the number of clusters or 
ancestry components to be specified in advance and evaluated by comparing solutions 
across candidate values after repeated analyses. These limitations highlight the need for 
an unsupervised and adaptive clustering strategy that can infer fine-scale genetic 
subgroups with minimal manual intervention. 
Determining the optimal association model for heterogeneous cohorts presents a 
significant challenge beyond population inference. Pooled analyses using Linear Mixed 
Models (LMMs) typically assume homogeneous genetic effects and estimate an 
average genetic effect across individuals, which can attenuate or mask heterogeneous 
signals23,24. By contrast, the meta-analysis strategies designed to integrate sub-
population summary statistics can, in principle, accommodate population heterogeneity; 
however, existing methods each have distinct strengths and limitations, and no single 
approach performs well across all kinds of heterogeneous populations and genetic 
architectures. For example, Fixed-Effects (FE) models can suffer substantial power loss 
in the presence of heterogeneity25-27, while Random-Effects (RE) models do not 
explicitly model ancestry-correlated effect patterns28, and the Meta-Regression of 
Multi-Ancestry Genetic Association (MR-MEGA), although able to model linear 
ancestry gradients, may lack sensitivity to population-specific effects when association 
signals are restricted to a single subgroup, as regression-based modeling tends to dilute 
sharply localized effects across populations29. Together, these limitations underscore 
the need for a unified framework that can simultaneously capture shared, ancestry-
correlated, and population-specific genetic effects. 


To address these challenges, we developed UCALM (Unsupervised adaptive 
Clustering-based Association and Layered Meta-integration), a unified framework for 
association mapping in heterogeneous populations. UCALM first infers genetically 
homogeneous subgroups directly from genotype data in a fully data-driven and 
unsupervised manner, thereby eliminating the need for manual intervention or 
predefined subgroup labels that may be unavailable or fail to reflect the true underlying 
genetic structure. It then performs subgroup-specific GWAS to prevent heterogeneous 
effects from being diluted in pooled analyses and to improve the detection of subgroup-
dependent association signals. Finally, UCALM integrates subgroup-level association 
evidence through a novel layered meta-analysis strategy, enabling the joint capture of 
shared, ancestry-correlated and subgroup-specific genetic effects that are difficult to 
resolve with any existing single meta-analysis model. By integrating data-driven 
subgroup inference, subgroup-aware association mapping and layered meta-integration, 
UCALM delivers greater statistical power and more robust Type I error control than 
existing mainstream methods across extensive simulations and real datasets, including 
the UK Biobank (n ≈ 487,000) and a heterogeneous pig cohort (n ≈ 85,000), providing 
a unified and powerful solution for resolving genetic heterogeneity and improving locus 
discovery ability in structured populations. The software and detailed user manual are 
freely available at https://mvp-cpp.github.io/tutorial/ucalm. 
Results 
Overview of the UCALM framework 
The UCALM framework is composed of three major components: unsupervised 
subgroup inference from genotype data, subgroup-specific GWAS, and layered meta-
integration of subgroup-level association signals (Fig. 1). 
First, UCALM infers genetically homogeneous subgroups directly from genotype 
data through a fully automated unsupervised procedure that integrates principal 
component analysis (PCA), Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection 
(UMAP)17, and Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with 


Noise (HDBSCAN)30. In this framework (Fig. 1a), PCs that are derived from GRM 
provide an initial low-dimensional representation of the high-dimensional genotype 
matrix, UMAP further resolves fine-scale non-linear relationships among individuals, 
and an optimized Auto-HDBSCAN delineates genetically homogeneous subgroups 
from the resulting manifold without requiring manual intervention or prior subgroup 
labels. This design enables UCALM to recover latent population structure while 
avoiding the subjectivity and potential misspecification associated with predefined 
subgroup assignment.

Lastly, UCALM integrates subgroup-level summary statistics through a layered 
meta-analysis framework (Fig. 1c). In the first layer, UCALM applies fixed-effects 
meta-analysis (FE)25, Meta-Regression of Multi-Ancestry Genetic Association (MR-
MEGA)29, and a population-specific test based on the Aggregated Cauchy Association 
Test (ACAT)34,35. These three complementary association components are designed to 
capture shared effects, ancestry-correlated heterogeneous effects, and subgroup-
specific effects, respectively. Their resulting statistics are then combined in a second 
layer using an ACAT-based omnibus combination to generate the final association 
signal. By hierarchically aggregating complementary evidence across models, UCALM 
derives a unified association measure that is robust to statistical dependence and 
effectively captures a broad spectrum of allelic effect architectures. 
Fine-scale characterization of genetic structure across species 
In our proposed UCALM framework, principal component analysis can 
effectively reduce the dimensionality of high-density SNP data and mitigate the curse 
of dimensionality; however, determining the optimal number of principal components 
(PCs) to retain is a critical issue, as it directly affects downstream UMAP embedding 
and the resolution of population subgrouping. We therefore systematically evaluated 
the effects of varying numbers of PCs on clustering accuracy and stability in two 
independent cohorts comprising human and pig populations, using the concordance 
between unsupervised adaptive clustering results and available sample prior labels. 
These analyses showed that retaining the first 10 PCs enables robust characterization 
of both global population genetic architecture and fine-scale subpopulation 
differentiation, while yielding clustering assignments with acceptable concordance to 
prior sample labels (Supplementary Figs. 1-2). Notably, prior sample labels often fail 


to capture the full complexity of underlying population structure and should therefore 
be regarded only as a secondary reference for selecting the optimal number of PCs. 
Furthermore, retaining too few PCs obscures subtle subpopulation structure, whereas 
including too many leads to degradation of distance metrics in high-dimensional space 
due to the curse of dimensionality. Accordingly, all downstream analyses in this study 
were based on clustering results generated from the first 10 PCs. 
We assessed the scalability and versatility of the adaptive clustering framework 
across two large-scale real datasets characterized by divergent demographic histories 
comprising the continuous admixture of human populations and the discrete selection-
driven stratification of livestock, both of which included predefined sample labels that 
enabled assessment of concordance between unsupervised clustering assignments and 
prior population classifications. Within the UK Biobank (UKB; n = 487,160), our 
proposed framework resolved global ancestral gradients alongside fine-scale 
substructures in a fully unsupervised manner (Fig. 2a, b). The identification of 20 
distinct clusters, with full cluster assignment details against self-reported ancestry 
provided in Supplementary Data 1, exhibited substantial concordance between inferred 
subgroup assignments and manually curated population labels, particularly at the level 
of major regional ancestries across different geographic groups. Notably, however, 
UCALM further resolved multiple fine-scale substructures within several broadly 
defined populations, most prominently within the British cohort, indicating that 
substantial cryptic genetic heterogeneity remains masked under conventional label 
definitions. In addition, individuals annotated as “Unknown” in the original metadata 
were robustly assigned into genetically coherent subgroups by UCALM, demonstrating 
its ability to recover latent population structure even in the absence of reliable prior 
information. 
We further evaluated the performance of UCALM in a heterogeneous pig cohort 
comprising multiple breeds and commercial lines derived from different breeding 
companies (n = 85,594; Figs. 2c, d). Overall, the inferred subgroup assignments showed 


good concordance with manually defined breed and line annotations, demonstrating the 
ability of UCALM to accurately resolve major genetic structure (i.e., Duroc, Landrace, 
Yorkshire, and Pietrain) within complex livestock populations. The full clustering 
results for breed and within-breed line assignments are detailed in Supplementary Data 
2. Importantly, UCALM additionally identified previously unrecorded sub-line 
structures within several broadly defined populations, revealing cryptic genetic 
stratification that was not captured by existing metadata. Moreover, individuals 
originating from different companies but sharing similar genetic backgrounds were 
consistently grouped into the same genetically coherent clusters, indicating that 
UCALM captures underlying genomic relationships rather than artificial organizational 
boundaries.

As illustrated in either Fig. 2b for human or Fig. 2d for pig populations, horizontal 
comparisons show that UCALM-derived subgroups often encompass multiple 
manually predefined labels, indicating that conventional label-based stratification may 
artificially fragment populations with highly similar genetic backgrounds into smaller 
groups. Such fragmentation reduces effective sample size within subpopulations and 
consequently leads to a loss of statistical power in downstream association analyses. In 
contrast, vertical comparisons reveal that manually defined labels are frequently too 
coarse to capture fine-scale genetic structure, with multiple genetically distinct 
subgroups often coexisting within a single predefined population. This hidden 
substructure may introduce residual confounding and increase the risk of false-positive 
associations in conventional GWAS frameworks. Collectively, these findings 
demonstrate that UCALM, by inferring population structure directly from genomic data 
in a fully data-driven manner, provides substantially higher resolution and biological 
fidelity than manually curated labels. These results further highlight the accuracy, 
robustness and flexibility of UCALM for fine-scale population stratification across 
diverse populations and species. 
To evaluate the computational efficiency and scalability of the UCALM in 
subgroup inference, we performed a benchmark analysis using six randomly sampled 
subsets from the human cohort of approximately 480,000 individuals, with sample sizes 
of 15,000, 30,000, 60,000, 120,000, 240,000 and 480,000. The workflow included 
three core steps: computation of the first 10 principal components, UMAP 
dimensionality reduction and Auto-HDBSCAN clustering. We also recorded the total 
running time of the full program, including data loading, preprocessing and output 
generation. As shown in Table 1, for the full-scale dataset of 480,000 individuals, the 
total running time of the full program was 1,714 seconds (28.6 minutes), with a peak 


memory usage of 929.71 GB. PCA, UMAP and Auto-HDBSCAN required 81, 353 and 
315 seconds, respectively. Among the three core modules, UMAP was one of the main 
time-consuming steps, increasing from 24 seconds at 15,000 samples to 353 seconds at 
480,000 samples. Auto-HDBSCAN also showed a marked increase in running time at 
large sample sizes, from 2 seconds at 15,000 samples to 315 seconds at 480,000 samples. 
The PCA step only computed the first 10 principal components and used truncated 
eigenvalue decomposition to avoid the high computational cost of full matrix 
decomposition, requiring only 81 seconds for 480,000 samples. As the sample size 
increased from 15,000 to 480,000, the total running time of the full program increased 
from 30 to 1,714 seconds, demonstrating that the workflow remains computationally 
tractable at biobank scale on a high-performance server. Overall, these results highlight 
the super computational efficiency and support the engineering feasibility of the 
UCALM in subgroup inference for large-scale biobank and breeding population 
datasets.

Statistical power and type I error control

constructed a genetically heterogeneous simulation panel with representation across 
major ancestries by sampling 50,000 individuals from the UK Biobank, including

Quantitative evaluation of the pooled false-positive rates further supported these trends 
(Supplementary Tables 2-4). The conservative calibration of UCALM likely reflects its

Under the homogeneous-effect scenario (HOMO), UCALM performed 
comparably to the most powerful fixed-effects (FE) model, while substantially 
outperforming LMM and RE (Fig. 4d). Under the ancestry-correlated heterogeneous 
scenario (HETE), UCALM and MR-MEGA achieved the highest power (Fig. 3b), 
whereas pooled LMM frequently produced inflated false-positive signals, reflecting the 
inability of homogeneous-effect models to adequately account for cross-population 
effect heterogeneity (Fig. 4b). The advantage of UCALM was most pronounced under 
the population-specific architecture (HETE_onlyEAS), where causal effects were 
confined to the East Asian subgroup. In this specific architecture, UCALM 
substantially outperformed alternative approaches, and notably, LMM, FE and RE 
failed to detect any significant loci (Fig. 4a), whereas MR-MEGA showed only limited 
sensitivity to effects restricted to minority sub-populations. Within the mixed 
architecture (HOMO_HETE), UCALM provided the most balanced performance by 
capturing both shared and stratified loci (Fig. 4c). All the above trends remained 
consistent across lower (ℎ" = 0.2) and higher (ℎ" = 0.8) heritabilities (Supplementary 
Figs. 3-4), which demonstrates the stability of UCALM across varying genetic effect 
magnitudes.

Application of UCALM to 24 quantitative traits in the UKB 
Rigorous control of genomic inflation with comparable statistical power 
To evaluate the performance of UCALM across diverse genetic architecture of 
real dataset, we first applied UCALM to 24 quantitative traits in the UK Biobank (UKB) 
cohort (n ≈ 480,000). Although this cohort is predominantly of European ancestry 
(approximately 90%), it still contains fine-scale substructure and cryptic relatedness, 


thereby providing a rigorous test bed for evaluating statistical models in near-
homogeneous populations. We compared the performance of UCALM with both a 
conventional general linear model (GLM) incorporating PCA covariates and the 
LDAK-KVIK, a computationally efficient approach that is statistically equivalent to a 
linear mixed model (LMM). 
Across the 24 traits analyzed in the UK Biobank, association signals detected by 
UCALM were highly concordant with those identified by conventional GLM and 
LDAK-KVIK, despite the markedly finer subgroup structure inferred by UCALM, as 
shown in the clustering results of Fig. 2a, b. Representative Manhattan plots for 
standing height (HT; n = 485,495) and body mass index (BMI; n = 484,961) further 
illustrated this overall consistency across methods (Fig. 5a, b). These findings suggest 
that the major association signals in UKB are robust and largely preserved across 
analytical frameworks. A plausible explanation is that, although UCALM resolves 
subtle subgroup structure, the UKB cohort remains relatively homogeneous at a broad 
scale owing to its predominantly European ancestry, such that major association signals 
are consistently captured by all methods. In this setting, the principal advantage of 
UCALM lies not in altering the overall landscape of association results, but in 
improving control of residual structure and calibration without compromising signal 
recovery. 
We rigorously assessed control of population stratification by quantifying the 
genomic inflation factor (𝜆GC) across distinct minor allele frequency categories for each 
trait (Supplementary Table 7). All evaluated methods showed global 𝜆)* values 
greater than 1.0. Such inflation is expected in large-scale analyses of highly polygenic 
traits and is primarily attributable to polygenicity and substantial sample sizes rather 
than inadequate correction for population stratification23,36,37. Nevertheless, UCALM 
consistently demonstrated superior frequency calibration and yielded the lowest global 
inflation across the 24 traits (Fig. 5c). The mean global 𝜆)* for UCALM was 1.1745, 
which is substantially lower than that observed for GLM at 1.4325 and LDAK-KVIK 


at 1.3716. For traits with high heritability such as standing height, UCALM markedly 
attenuated the global inflation 𝜆)* from 1.8096 in the GLM and 1.8569 in the LDAK-
KVIK to 1.5837. Stratified analyses further showed that UCALM provided the most 
conservative rare-variant calibration, with a mean rare-variant 𝜆)* of 0.9159, 
compared with 1.1780 for GLM and 1.0750 for LDAK-KVIK, while preserving the 
expected polygenic inflation among common variants (Supplementary Table 7). 
Together with the simulation evidence of effective type I error control in heterogeneous 
settings (Fig. 3c), the reduced 𝜆)* values observed in the UKB application suggest 
that UCALM improves control of residual population structure and cryptic relatedness 
without compromising major association signals.

Application of UCALM to four economic traits in pigs 
UCALM controls population stratification with significantly improved statistical 
power 
We next evaluated UCALM across multiple traits in a pig cohort comprising 
multiple breeds from different commercial sources, providing a stringent test bed for 
association mapping in a genetically heterogeneous population shaped by complex 
breeding histories, artificial selection and pronounced population structure. Analysis of 
the genomic inflation factor stratified by minor allele frequency revealed clear 
differences among GLM, LMM and UCALM (Fig. 6c; Supplementary Table 8). GLM 
showed severe inflation across all four traits, with a mean overall 𝜆)* of 13.7018, 
whereas both LMM and UCALM effectively controlled genomic inflation. For traits 
such as backfat thickness adjusted to 100 kg (BF) and loin muscle area adjusted to 100 
kg (LMA), LMM showed deflation among common variants, consistent with potential 


overcorrection when principal components and the genomic relationship matrix are 
jointly included. By contrast, UCALM mitigated this loss of power by conducting 
subgroup-specific GWAS within relatively homogeneous genetic backgrounds. 
UCALM also provided substantially improved control of genomic inflation for rare 
variants than LMM. In genetically complex populations, rare variants are more likely 
to be population-specific, and LMM appeared unable to fully account for this structure, 
resulting in inflation for traits such as FCR and LMA. 
Having established that UCALM provides improved control of false-positive 
signals, we next evaluated whether this improved calibration was accompanied by gains 
in discovery power. We systematically quantified the concordance among methods and 
the additional discoveries enabled by UCALM using a genome-wide window-based 
intersection analysis across all four economically important traits. The genome was 
partitioned into independent 100-kb windows as described in Methods, and then 
detection sensitivity was compared across five statistical approaches: Linear Mixed 
Model (LMM), Fixed-Effects (FE), Random Effects (RE), MR-MEGA, and UCALM. 
As shown in Fig. 6d and Supplementary Figs. 8-11, UCALM exhibited strong 
discovery power, capturing 126 loci shared across all five evaluated methods, and 
thereby confirming its reliability in recovering consensus association signals. Beyond 
these common signals, UCALM remained highly sensitive to loci identified by rigorous 
meta-analytic strategies, identifying 111 loci shared with FE and MR-MEGA but 
missed by both LMM and RE. Moreover, 61 loci were detected by both UCALM and 
MR-MEGA but not by either FE or LMM. These loci likely harbor ancestry-correlated 
effects, in which allelic effect sizes vary with breed differentiation, and would therefore 
be attenuated or overlooked in pooled analyses. Most importantly, UCALM uniquely 
identified 74 loci that were not detected by any other method. This additional discovery 
power is consistent with the contribution of the ACAT component, which is particularly 
sensitive to subgroup-specific effects, together with the integration of complementary 
association components. These results highlight that the strength of UCALM lies in its 


explicit modelling and integration of multiple classes of genetic association signals, 
thereby enabling the recovery of loci missed by conventional single-model approaches.  
Similarly, we classified each significant locus according to its predominant effect 
architecture, distinguishing homogeneous, ancestry-correlated heterogeneous and 
subgroup-specific heterogeneous signals, consistent with the classification framework 
used in the preceding human analysis. As shown in Fig. 6e, homogeneous effects 
accounted for the majority of associations for traits such as AGE, whereas the FCR was 
dominated by signals exhibiting population-specific heterogeneity. Such marked 
architectural variation highlights the severe limitations of relying on a single statistical 
model in genetically complex pig populations shaped by intensive selection and 
structured breeding. Collectively, these results indicate that UCALM enhances 
candidate locus discovery by achieving an effective balance between statistical power 
and control of genetic heterogeneity.

Discussion 
The rapid expansion of large human biobanks and livestock reference populations 
marks a pivotal shift in the dissection of complex traits7,46. In both domains, the 
integration of data from diverse genetic backgrounds is increasingly prioritized to 
reduce ancestry-related gaps in human genetic studies and to improve the accuracy of 
joint genetic evaluations in livestock47-50. However, this expansion entails a 
fundamental trade-off, larger sample sizes can increase discovery power, but population 
stratification and effect-size heterogeneity can confound association tests or attenuate 
subgroup-dependent signals29,51,52. UCALM addresses this challenge by coupling 
adaptive unsupervised subgroup inference with a layered association framework that 
integrates heterogeneous association signal, thereby shifting the analytical focus from 
population labels to underlying genetic effect architectures. By validating UCALM in 
the UK Biobank and a heterogeneous commercial pig cohort, we demonstrate that the 
explicit modeling of fine-scale population structure and effect heterogeneity 
substantially improves discovery power while maintaining rigorous stratification 
control across species. 
A key innovation of UCALM lies in its departure from rigid predefined labels, 
such as self-reported ethnicity in humans or registered breed in livestock53. Instead, 
UCALM adopts an adaptive density-based clustering on a non-linear genetic manifold, 
enabling population structure to be inferred directly from genomic data. In modern 
animal production, extensive crossbreeding and composite-line development generate 
complex genetic backgrounds that often cannot be adequately represented by 
conventional breed categorization, paralleling the continuous ancestry gradients 
observed in admixed human populations. By integrating PCA-UMAP with Auto-
HDBSCAN, UCALM effectively resolves fine-scale substructures17,54, including subtle 
stratification within European populations and cryptic breeding lines within pig breeds. 
Moreover, the reassignment procedure based on stable core clusters enables reliable 


recovery of individuals with missing, incomplete or uncertain annotations, such as 
crossbred pigs lacking detailed records or admixed humans without reliable self-
reported ancestry information. Such individuals are often excluded during conventional 
quality control, whereas UCALM can retain them for downstream analysis. This 
maximizes the utilization of available data, increasing effective sample size and 
improving the representation of genetically diverse individuals, thereby enhancing both 
the power and generalizability of downstream analyses in human genetics and animal 
breeding.46,55,56. 
Beyond improving the performance of genetic association, the automated 
clustering strategy also provides a practical computational advantage. By partitioning a 
large heterogeneous cohort into smaller and more homogeneous subgroups, UCALM 
reduces the sample size of each individual-level GWAS, thereby lowering memory 
requirements and computational burden for downstream association testing. This 
divide-and-conquer design is particularly useful for very large biobank-scale datasets, 
where fitting models involving large, dense genomic relationship matrices can impose 
substantial computational and memory demands57. Thus, subgroup-based analysis not 
only improves the modelling of genetic heterogeneity but also offers a scalable route 
for association mapping in increasingly large genomic datasets. 
Statistically, UCALM addresses the challenge of model selection in meta-analysis 
by explicitly integrating multiple plausible architectures of allelic effects. Conventional 
approaches typically favor a single effect model: Fixed-Effects (FE) model is most 
powerful when effects are homogeneous25, whereas MR-MEGA is designed to capture 
ancestry-correlated heterogeneity29. However, the causal variants may also exhibit 
population-specific sparsity58,59. The layered meta-analysis strategy in UCALM utilizes 
the Cauchy combination method to synthesize evidence across homogeneous, ancestry-
correlated and population-specific components, thereby retaining sensitivity to 
whichever architecture best explains the signal. This flexibility is exemplified by the 
identification of the MYOT and NEK1 loci for backfat thickness in pigs, both of which 


were obscured in pooled LMM analyses due to its heterogeneous effect size across 
subgroups. Such findings suggest that missing heritability may fundamentally stem 
from hidden heterogeneity, particularly for traits and populations in which allele 
frequencies and linkage disequilibrium differ substantially across genetic backgrounds. 
The UCALM framework holds substantial potential for broader applications. In 
human genetics, the separation of homogeneous and heterogeneous components could 
enhance the portability of Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS) by prioritizing globally 
consistent variants across genetic backgrounds60. In livestock breeding, UCALM could 
support cross-population genomic selection by enabling historical datasets from 
multiple breeds, lines or breeding programs to be integrated more effectively while 
explicitly accounting for heterogeneity in genetic effects. The resulting association 
signals could serve as input for downstream genomic prediction models, such as 
KAML61 and IFAM62, that build priors using information from GWAS. By providing 
a mathematically robust solution to multi-population association mapping, UCALM 
offers a unified framework to genetic discovery across species. Its ability to distinguish 
shared, ancestry-correlated and population-specific signals helps ensure that 
phenotypic associations can be detected and interpreted more rigorously, even in 
populations with complex genetic structure. 
Methods and Materials 
The UK Biobank dataset 
The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large-scale prospective cohort study that includes 
approximately 500,000 participants from diverse ethnic backgrounds, primarily of 
European ancestry, and offers comprehensive phenotypic records. The genotype data 
utilized in this investigation were sourced from UKB Data-field 22828, which were 
imputed by The Wellcome Trust Centre for Human Genetics (WTCHG), Oxford. 
Quality control (QC) procedures were performed using PLINK software (v2.00)63; 
SNPs were filtered based on: minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.001, genotyping rate ≥ 
0.95, and imputation information score ≥ 0.8. After QC, a total of 487,160 individuals 


and 14,265,293 SNPs were retained for subsequent analyses. The phenotypic dataset 
consisted of 24 quantitative traits that were previously analyzed in the fastGWA study32, 
including anthropometric, physiological, and biochemical parameters (Supplementary 
Table 9). For all association models (GLM, UCALM sub-populations, and LDAK-
KVIK Step 2), and consistent with the covariate adjustment strategy used in the 
fastGWA study, we fitted age, age", sex, age × sex, age" × sex, and the top 20 
principal components as covariates. The sex-specific traits were adjusted for age, age" 
and the top 20 PCs. For LDAK-KVIK, we implemented the recommended two-step 
protocol to optimize computational efficiency. In Step 1, model parameters were 
estimated using a prioritized subset of 602,585 independent variants, generated by 
intersecting the imputed genotype data with HapMap3 variants and performing linkage 
disequilibrium pruning using PLINK2 (window size 1,000, step size 100, 𝑟" > 0.9)64. 
In Step 2, association statistics were computed for all 14,265,293 retained variants using 
the full set of covariates. Given the computational burden of fitting standard linear 
mixed models (LMMs) in large-scale human cohorts and the relatively simple genetic 
structure of the UKB population, UCALM integrated summary statistics from GLM 
analyses conducted independently within each inferred subpopulation. 
The Pig dataset 
This study collected genotype data from 85,594 pigs comprising four breeds and 
three genetic lines. Each pig had 24,439,525 SNPs after genotype imputation. Details 
of the genotype imputation procedure and the results are provided in the Supplementary 
Notes (Supplementary Fig. 14). Quality control (QC) was performed on the imputed 
genotypes using PLINK software (v2.00)63, applying filters for a minor allele frequency 
(MAF) ≥ 0.01 and a genotype call rate ≥ 0.95. After QC, the final dataset included 
85,594 individuals and 19,713,426 high-confidence SNPs for downstream analyses. 
Phenotypic records were obtained for four economic traits comprising backfat thickness 
adjusted to 100 kg (BF, 𝑛= 75,538), age adjusted to 100 kg (AGE, 𝑛= 75,981), 
feed conversion ratio (FCR, 𝑛= 19,822), and loin muscle area adjusted to 100 kg 


(LMA, 𝑛= 51,785). Each GWAS was conducted using individuals with phenotypic 
records for the corresponding trait. Detailed information on the samples and phenotypic 
measurements is provided in Supplementary Table 10. For the pig cohort, the sample 
size was computationally tractable for mixed-model analysis, whereas the complex 
genetic background required explicit control of relatedness and population structure; 
therefore, pooled general linear model (GLM) and linear mixed model (LMM) analyses 
were conducted in the full cohort, while UCALM used LMM-based association 
analyses within each inferred subpopulation. All GWAS models included sex, year-
season, and the top 5 principal components as covariates.  
Independent association signals were defined by partitioning the genome into 100-
kb windows. Applying a genome-wide significance threshold of 𝑃< 2.54 × 10#., we 
identified the lead SNP exhibiting the lowest P-value within each significant window. 
To quantify the additional power provided by our method, UCALM-detected signals 
were classified as novel if the corresponding 100-kb window lacked significant 
association signals in the standard LMM analysis; conversely, signals located in 
windows that also contained significant LMM hits were classified as common. 
Functional annotation was performed using BEDTools (v2.31.1) and the Sus scrofa 
11.1.104 reference genome65. Lead SNPs were mapped to genes within a 100-kb 
window and prioritized hierarchically based on their genomic context: exonic, intronic, 
proximal and distal. 
Adaptive automated clustering and stratification 
We implemented an unsupervised dimensionality-reduction and density-based 
clustering procedure to infer fine-scale population structure directly from high-density 
genotype data. First, a genomic relationship matrix (GRM) was constructed from 
genome-wide markers and subjected to eigen decomposition to obtain the leading 𝑘 
principal components, with 𝑘 set to 3 by default and also adjustable by the user. In this

Simulating phenotypes 
To extensively evaluate the statistical power and Type I error control of our 
proposed framework, we performed simulations based on real genotype data from the 
UK biobank (UKB) dataset. We randomly selected 50,000 unrelated individuals 
comprising four distinct ancestral populations: European (EUR, 𝑛= 35,000), African 
(AFR, 𝑛 = 7,000), South Asian (SAS, 𝑛 = 6,600), and East Asian (EAS, 𝑛 =
 1,400). To enable a rigorous assessment of both statistic power and false positive rates 
within the unified simulation framework, we partitioned the genome according to 
physical position. For each chromosome, the centromeric midpoint was identified, and 
the first half of the chromosome was used to select causal variants for power evaluation, 
whereas the second half was used to select null variants for Type I error assessment. A 
4Mb buffer region was introduced between these two segments to minimize linkage 
disequilibrium (LD) spillover and ensure statistical independence between causal and 
null signals. 
Phenotypes were generated under a standard additive genetic model defined as 
𝑦 = 𝑋𝛽+ 𝑒, where 𝑦 denotes the vector of simulated phenotypes, 𝑋 represents the 
genotype matrix of causal variants, 𝛽 is the vector of effect sizes, and 𝑒 is the 
residual error. The variance of the residuals was determined by the target heritability 
(ℎ") according to the formula 𝜎C" = 𝜎B"(1 −ℎ")/ℎ" , where 𝜎B" is the genetic 
variance. We simulated quantitative traits across varying heritability levels (ℎ" ∈
{0.2, 0.5, 0.8}), performing 20 independent replications for each experimental setting 
to ensure statistical robustness. 
We designed four distinct genetic architectures to cover the major classes of 
association signals expected in multi-population cohorts. First, homogeneous effects 
were simulated using 1,000 causal variants with relatively stable allele frequencies 
across populations (maximum allele-frequency difference < 0.10), with constant effect 


sizes across populations drawn independently from a normal distribution with mean 0 
and variance 1. Second, ancestry-correlated heterogeneity was simulated using 1,000 
variants whose effect sizes varied linearly with population differentiation. Specifically, 
a base effect was scaled by population-specific gradient factors of 1, -0.25, 0.25 and 0 
for EUR, AFR, SAS and EAS, respectively. Third, population-specific effects were 
simulated using 100 variants showing marked allele frequency divergence, defined as 
loci for which the allele frequency in EAS exceeded that in every other population by 
at least 0.20. For these variants, genetic effects were set to be non-zero only in the EAS 
group and zero in all other groups. Finally, a mixed architecture was simulated by 
combining homogeneous and ancestry-correlated variants, yielding a total of 1,000 
causal SNPs, to evaluate simultaneous detection of shared and stratified effects. All 
simulated phenotypes were standardized prior to analysis to ensure consistent scale 
across methods. 
Assessing the statistical power and false positive rate 
To systematically benchmark the performance of our proposed UCALM, we 
compared it with the LMM and three representative meta-analysis approaches, 
including FE, RE, and MR-MEGA, across 20 independent simulation replicates. 
Statistical power was evaluated using two complementary metrics derived from 
simulated causal SNPs: the mean 𝜒" test statistic to quantify overall signal strength, 
and the empirical power, defined as the count of causal variants reaching the rigorous 
Bonferroni threshold (𝑝< 5.56 × 10#.). Simultaneously, to ensure that improved 
sensitivity did not compromise Type I error control, we evaluated the genomic inflation 
of null SNPs from preserved genomic regions by computing the mean 𝜒" statistic, and 
assessed robustness across significance levels by calculating the empirical False 
Positive Rate (FPR) at four nominal thresholds (0.05, 5 × 10#$, 5 × 10#%, 𝑎𝑛𝑑 5 ×
10#&).

Supplementary Note 
The UK Biobank dataset and processing 
The UK Biobank (UKB) imputation release contains approximately 96 million 
markers before study-specific filtering1. Genomic coordinates were based on GRCh372. 
All UKB analyses in this study were restricted to autosomal SNPs; sex-chromosome 
markers were excluded before clustering and association testing. No additional sample-
level filtering was applied beyond the analysis set described in the Methods section. 
Covariate information was taken from UKB Data-field 21022 for age, Data-field 31 for 
sex and Data-field 22009 for genetic principal components. These fields were used to 


construct the covariates described in the Methods section. Supplementary Table 9 
provides the trait information for the 24 UKB quantitative phenotypes, including trait 
abbreviation, UKB data-field ID, number of records, phenotype category and full trait 
name. Phenotypes were analyzed on their original scales. Self-reported ethnicity was 
taken from UKB Data-field 21000. These labels were not used for UCALM clustering. 
They were used only to describe the inferred UKB clusters and to compare data-driven 
clusters with available sample labels. For the LDAK-KVIK comparison, Step 1 variants 
were selected from the UKB genotype set after intersection with HapMap3 variants3 
using PLINK v2.00 with --indep-pairwise 1000 100 0.94. Starting from 1,169,312 
HapMap3 variants, LD pruning removed 566,727 variants and retained 602,585 
variants for Step 1 model fitting. This follows the LDAK-KVIK recommendation for 
dense datasets, where Step 1 can be run on a subset of predictors to reduce runtime, 
whereas all retained genome-wide variants are still used in Step 2 association testing5. 
The Pig Dataset and processing 
Tail tissue samples were collected from each pig during routine tail docking in 
piglets. This sampling was integrated into standard farm management to reduce 
additional handling. Immediately after collection, tail tissues were preserved in 75% 
ethanol and stored at -20℃ to maintain DNA integrity. All preserved samples were 
transported to Yingzi Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for genomic analysis. 
Genomic DNA was extracted from tail tissues using standard protocols. High 
throughput genotyping was performed using the pig 80K functional variants genotyping 
array. The genotype data with 187,000 SNPs were imputed using the reference panel 


and imputation tool downloaded from the AGIDB website6. Variants with imputation 
accuracy 𝑅! > 0.7 were retained. 
Imputation accuracy was assessed by genotype masking in 20-fold cross-
validation. Biallelic SNPs shared between the chip genotype dataset and the phased 
reference panel were divided into 20 mutually exclusive folds of approximately equal 
size. In each round, one-fold was masked from the chip genotype dataset and imputed 
with Beagle v5.57, while the remaining chip variants were kept as observed genotypes. 
The imputed genotypes at masked loci were then compared with the corresponding 
observed genotypes. Imputation accuracy was measured by consistency (CR) and 
correlation (𝑅!). CR was defined as the proportion of correctly imputed hard call 
genotypes among all non-missing observed genotypes. 𝑅! was calculated as the 
squared Pearson correlation between imputed and observed genotype dosages, with 
observed genotypes coded as 0, 1 and 2 and converted to minor allele dosage when 
needed. Both metrics were summarized at the individual and SNP levels. The results 
across the 20 cross-validation folds are shown in Supplementary Fig. 14. Both 
individual level and SNP level summaries showed high CR and 𝑅!, supporting high 
imputation accuracy for individuals and markers in the pig dataset. Supplementary 
Table 10 provides the phenotype information for the four pig traits, including trait 
abbreviation, number of records, trait category and full trait description. Supplementary 
Data 2 provides the final pig cluster composition. In the pig labels, the three-letter code 
denotes company, breed and genetic line. 


The Simulated Dataset 
The simulation analyses used the 50,000-individual UKB panel described in the 
Methods section. Quality control procedures were performed using PLINK v2.00, SNPs 
were filtered based on: minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01, genotyping rate ≥ 0.95. 
After QC, a total of 50,000 individuals and 8,996,037 SNPs were retained for 
subsequent analyses. For LDAK-KVIK, we used the same recommended two-step 
protocol as in the UKB real trait analysis to improve computational efficiency5. In Step 
1, model parameters were estimated using a prioritized subset of 643,087 independent 
variants, generated by intersecting the imputed genotype data with HapMap3 variants 
and performing linkage disequilibrium pruning using PLINK v2.00 with --indep-
pairwise 1000 100 0.94. In Step 2, association statistics were computed for all 8,996,037 
retained variants using the full set of covariates.

Cauchy combination test in UCALM 
UCALM used the Cauchy combination test to integrate dependent component P-
values. In the first layer, subgroup P-values were combined to detect population specific 
signals, with weights proportional to the number of samples contributing to each 


subgroup test. In the second layer, the three-component P-values for homogeneous, 
ancestry correlated and population specific signals were combined with equal weights.
