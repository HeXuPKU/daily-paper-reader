# Score-based confidence intervals for variance-covariance parameters in linear mixed models 

# Matias Shedden Karl Oskar Ekvall 

Department of Statistics, University of Florida

# October 6, 2026 

Abstract 

We propose confidence intervals with near-nominal coverage of variance-covariance parameters in linear mixed models, even when interest or nuisance parameters are near the boundary, that is, when some random effects have small variances or are strongly correlated. In such settings, our simulations show standard Wald, likelihood ratio, and score intervals with nominal level 95% can have coverage as low as 45% and as high as 99%, while the proposed intervals are near nominal. The proposed intervals invert a modified profile score statistic based on the restricted likelihood, with nuisance parameters estimated on an extended parameter set. In a longitudinal study of socialization in children with autism, where the estimated correlation between the random intercept and slope is near minus one, our intervals show that children who start higher improve more slowly. In a genetic study of white blood cell count in mice, they distinguish the contributions of variant classes even though several intervals include zero. Compared to universal inference intervals, which are valid in finite samples, ours are 1.7 to 3.1 times narrower, and finite where one universal interval is not, at a forty-seventh of the computing time. We establish asymptotically correct coverage uniformly in the parameter under a condition known to hold with independent clusters and crossed random effects. 

Key words: Linear mixed models; Boundary parameter; Confidence interval; Nuisance parameter; Restricted maximum likelihood; Score test; Variance components. 

1arXiv:2610.04181v1 [stat.ME] 3 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Many applications of linear mixed models require inference on (variance-)covariance parameters near the boundary of the parameter set, that is, a variance near zero or a correlation near ±1. In longitudinal studies, for instance, the variance of a random slope in time quantifies how much the rate of change varies between individuals, and its covariance with the random intercept indicates whether starting higher is associated with faster or slower change. Section 5.1 illustrates this with socialization scores of children with autism at five ages, where the estimated correlation between the random intercept and slope is near −1. In genetics, a variance component can model the variability in a trait attributable to a class of genetic variants, and the questions are how much each class contributes and how the classes rank (Yang et al., 2010, 2015). In Section 5.2, the trait is white blood cell count in mice and the classes are single-nucleotide polymorphisms grouped by minor allele frequency, and several classes have small or zero estimated variance components. Near the boundary, standard confidence intervals for the covariance parameters are unreliable, and existing valid ones are either restricted to special cases or can be very wide. The asymptotic approximations underlying Wald and likelihood ratio intervals are typically inaccurate for parameters on or near the boundary (Stern and Welsh, 2000), sometimes severely so. Our simulations include settings in which nominal 95% likelihood ratio intervals have coverage as low as 0 .65 and Wald intervals as low as 0 .75, even though the true parameter is interior. In other settings, the Wald interval has coverage up to 0 .995 and the likelihood ratio interval up to 0 .992. Which parameter values are close enough to the boundary to affect coverage depends on the model and the interval, but since the true parameter is unknown, any method whose coverage depends substantially on its value is unreliable. We provide computationally efficient, score-based confidence intervals and show that they have asymptotically correct coverage uniformly in the parameter. In the simulations, they have near-nominal coverage in every setting. Testing whether the parameter is on the boundary is well understood in general (Self and Liang, 1987; Geyer, 1994; Silvapulle and Silvapulle, 1995; Andrews, 2001) and for mixed models in particular (Stram and Lee, 1994; Greven et al., 2008; Baey et al., 2019). However, such a test requires an accurate distribution approximation only at the boundary. A confidence interval requires accurate approximations also at interior values arbitrarily close to the boundary. Existing resampling methods do not resolve the difficulty: the bootstrap can be inconsistent when the true parameter is on the boundary (Andrews, 2000), and modifications of it for testing boundary points in mixed models do not extend straightforwardly to confidence intervals (Sinha, 2009; Guédon 

2et al., 2024), nor do permutation tests for random effects (Fitzmaurice et al., 2007). Recent theory shows that the score statistic standardized by expected information often remains approximately chi-squared near the boundary (Ekvall and Bottai, 2022, 2026). Battey and McCullagh (2024) similarly find that statistic to be unaffected by an atypical geometry of the likelihood that makes the Wald statistic ineffectual for a variance component. However, existing reliable methods apply either in special cases, notably a single variance component or a ratio of two (Harville and Fenech, 1985; Crainiceanu and Ruppert, 2004; Schweiger et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2025), or to the vector of all covariance parameters jointly, using confidence regions (Ekvall and Bottai, 2026). Such regions are difficult to interpret, and scientific questions are often about a particular component. Projecting the region onto one axis is often too conservative, and requires computing the full region. Universal inference gives valid intervals under minimal conditions (Zhang et al., 2026), but, as the examples of Section 5 show, they are much wider and slower to compute than ours, which invert a profile score statistic. Intervals for one component at a time have the others as nuisance parameters, whose estimation, for example by profiling the likelihood, can be computationally intensive and affect coverage. A hard case is a nuisance parameter near the boundary, as in the genetics example of Section 5.2, where one variance component is estimated to be zero and several are nearly so. We address both computing and coverage by extending the parameter set to include nuisance parameter values that are incompatible with the random-effects formulation but still yield a valid marginal likelihood. The intervals remain interpretable because the interest parameters are constrained to their original parameter set. Extended parameter sets have precedents in and outside mixed models (Feng and McCulloch, 1992; Verbeke and Molenberghs, 2003; Ketz, 2018; Stringer and Negrea, 2026), and nuisance variances on the boundary have been treated in testing (Qu et al., 2013; Wood, 2013), but none of these gives confidence intervals with a coverage guarantee in this setting. Proving that the intervals attain their nominal coverage uniformly requires asymp-totics along sequences of true parameters approaching the boundary, whereas classical theory for mixed models assumes a fixed and interior parameter (Miller, 1977; Jiang, 1996). Building on recent theory for general parametric models (Shedden and Ekvall, 2026), we establish the asymptotic distribution of the profile score statistic under such sequences, with the nuisance parameters estimated on the extended set. Although the general conditions are considerably more involved than what suffices without nuisance parameters, we show that for the linear mixed model they reduce, with the right choice of nuisance estimator, to one already known to hold in models with independent clusters and crossed random effects (Ekvall and Bottai, 2026).

# 2.4 Computation 

Making the method practical requires evaluating the test statistic T (ψ1) efficiently at the many values needed to invert the test. Each evaluation requires solving an optimization problem to find the nuisance parameter estimator ˜ψ(2) (ψ1). This is a nonconvex problem whose objective can be irregular. Because ˜Υ(2) (ψ1) is open, the problem is unconstrained; over Υ (2) (ψ1) it would have positive semi-definiteness constraints. We use a trust-region algorithm with the expected information, which the statistic already requires, in place of the Hessian (cf. the average-information algorithm of Gilmour et al., 1995). When the random-effects design is sparse, each evaluation uses a Woodbury identity for Σ( ψ)−1 and that sparsity, so the value, score, and expected information together cost 

O(nq + rq 3 + r2q2) in the worst case and less when the factorizations stay sparse. When 

q ≥ n and Z is dense, as in kernel or kinship models, that cost is dominated by dense 

q × q algebra. Our implementation then forms the matrices ZH j ZT once and works with n × n matrices thereafter, so each later evaluation costs O(rn 3 + r2n2), for any q.To invert the test, we search outward from the estimate until the value of the test-statistic crosses the critical value. The nuisance parameter estimate at each accepted value warm-starts the next optimization, which speeds up the search. Step lengths follow from a secant prediction of the critical-value crossing, refined by regula falsi, and a limit typically needs 15–20 evaluations of the statistic. The R package reconf 

implements these methods. Appendix B gives additional computational details.

# 4 Simulations 

We study finite-sample properties of the proposed intervals and compare them to Wald, likelihood ratio, and constrained score intervals. The latter result from using the smaller parameter set for the nuisance parameters; the interest parameter has the same set for all methods. All intervals are based on the restricted likelihood. In every simulation setting, the true parameter is interior and, therefore, each interval is calibrated against the χ21-distribution. The simulations show how the behavior of the intervals depends on the proximity of the parameter to the boundary and other model quantities. # 5.2 Genetic variance components in mice 

A single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is a position in the genome at which the alleles observed in a population differ in one nucleotide; the SNPs studied here have two alleles. In mice, the genotype at a SNP is the number of copies, 0, 1, or 2, of the less common allele. The frequency of that allele among the genotyped individuals is the SNP’s minor allele frequency. For n individuals genotyped at s SNPs, let D ∈ Rn×s be the matrix of genotypes, with the column for a SNP of minor allele frequency ˆ p centered at its sample mean 2ˆ p and scaled by {2ˆ p(1 − ˆp)}1/2; missing genotypes, 3% of the total, are set to the sample mean before centering. # 6 Discussion 

Coverage of common confidence intervals depends on the proximity of the (unknown) true parameter to the boundary, and more generally on the geometry of the parameter set near the true parameter. The structure of the information matrix at the truth also has an effect. The proposed intervals, by contrast, have asymptotically correct coverage uniformly in the parameter and near-nominal coverage in every simulated setting, and we therefore recommend them as the default. Likelihood ratio intervals are typically shorter, but how to modify them to ensure uniform asymptotic validity in near-boundary settings is an open question. Universal inference intervals are valid in finite samples, but they can be both wide and computationally expensive. The key condition of our theory holds in several linear mixed model settings, including independent clusters and crossed designs. In general, no particular type of dependence or lack thereof is required. We expect the results to extend to other normal models. The setting here covers any covariance matrix linear in the parameters, but nonlinear ones, such as autoregressive, Matérn, or factor-analytic covariances, require additional work. Extending the results to generalized linear mixed models is more difficult: the likelihood integrates against the density of the random effects, so it needs 

ZΨ( ψ)ZT to be a covariance matrix and the extended set of Section 2 has no clear analogue. Other open questions include intervals for ratios, simultaneous coverage for several components, and settings with high-dimensional nuisance parameters. With dense relationship matrices each evaluation of the statistic costs O(rn 3 + r2n2), which lim-its the sample sizes reachable in genetics and may require additional computational developments.

The statistic of Section 2 is built from the restricted likelihood, but its parts are computed from the likelihood of (2): the restricted score is the score for that likelihood evaluated at ˜β(ψ), corrected by a determinant term, and the restricted information has the same traces with a projection inserted. We therefore give the likelihood, score, and information first, assemble the restricted versions in Section B.2, and invert the statistic last.

45 B.4 Inverting the test statistic 

Constructing a confidence region in practice requires evaluating T (ψ1) at many candidate values of ψ1 and retaining those for which T (ψ1) ≤ q1−α. For clarity, we focus on a confidence interval for a single random effect variance, say ψ1, with ψ(2) the nuisance parameter. Thus, we seek the C(α) of Section 2, 

{ψ1 ∈ [0 , ∞) : T (ψ1) ≤ q1−α}.

Similar arguments apply to other parameters of interest, and the accompanying software implements the method for any parameter of interest. In practice, confidence intervals are usually constructed after fitting the model, so (restricted) maximum likelihood estimates of β and ψ are available and provide natural starting values. If they are not available, any ( β0, ψ 0) ∈ Rp × ˜Υ such that T (ψ01 ) ≤ q1−α

can be used to initialize our algorithm. The one-step option takes the Newton step (7) from the value accepted at the previous point of the search, and at the first point from the nuisance components of the fitted model. In the simulations of Section 4, intervals computed this way differ in width from those obtained by profiling to convergence by at most 1 .6%.
