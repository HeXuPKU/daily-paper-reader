Abstract 

Pleiotropy, the phenomenon in which a single genetic variant influences multiple traits, is a fun-damental feature of genome biology and an important consideration in statistical genetics. Many statistical methods exist that leverage pleiotropy to increase the power of association tests, but they are often designed to identify SNPs which are associated with at least one of the underlying traits. Only a limited number of methods exist for identification of “strict pleiotropy”, i.e. those associated with more than one trait, but these methods are either restricted to a small number of traits or become computationally challenging as the number of traits increases. In this paper, we develop two testing procedures to identify SNPs under strict pleiotropy and determine the corresponding minimum num-ber of associated traits. Both methods rely only on z-scores available from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and are applicable to independent as well as correlated traits. The first procedure is frequentist in nature and is designed to control the family-wise-error rate. The second procedure is based on the local false discovery rate and provides improved power while maintaining FDR control. We conduct extensive simulation studies to compare the performance of the proposed methods with alternative methods for testing strict pleiotropy. Finally, we demonstrate an application of the pro-posed methods to Alzheimer’s disease-related biomarkers for the goal of distinguishing SNPs that may represent biomarker-specific variations from those that are related to disease-relevant biology. 

Keywords: Genome-wide association study (GWAS), pleiotropy, local false discovery rate, com-posite hypothesis, family-wise error rate, variance component model, single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) 

# 1 Introduction 

Pleiotropy, the phenomenon where a single genetic variant influences multiple traits [32, 33], is a fundamental property of genome biology and a key concept in statistical genetics. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have provided evidence of widespread pleiotropy across a broad range of human traits [2, 25, 26, 36, 38], demonstrating that genetic architecture is highly interconnected. Researchers have leveraged pleiotropy to improve the power of SNP discovery in GWAS. Assuming that the traits are associated through pleiotropy, multivariate and multi-trait GWAS methods have been developed to jointly test associations between genetic variants and multiple related phenotypes, thereby improving power to detect SNPs associated with the traits [3, 4, 13, 27, 41, 42]. However, 

1arXiv:2610.00962v1 [stat.ME] 1 Oct 2026 these approaches primarily test whether a SNP is associated with at least one trait within a phenotype set, rather than explicitly testing for multi-trait per se. Several methods [24, 28, 29, 44] have been developed to test for “strict” multi-trait association under suitable composite null hypotheses. However, these methods are difficult to generalize beyond two traits and fail to account for genetic correlations among traits and for correlations induced by GWAS sample overlap. [31] introduced a sequential testing framework for pleiotropy across more than two traits, though their method requires individual-level data, which limits its applicability, as in practice, we may not have access to individual-level data. In addition to traditional multivariate testing frameworks, empirical Bayes approaches based on the local false discovery rate (lfdr) [7, 8, 35] have become useful tools for testing genetic effects across multiple traits. Recently, Sun et al. [34] proposed the conditionally symmetric multidimensional Gaussian mixture model (csmGmm) for testing strict pleiotropic association while controlling the false discovery rate (FDR), but this method faces substantial computational challenges when the number of traits is large. Testing for strict pleiotropy becomes highly relevant when investigating the genetic association of latent health conditions that manifest through multiple biomarkers. For example, the early stage of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), when cognitive symptoms are not yet developed, is characterized by al-terations in multiple biomarkers, including amyloid beta, phosphorylated tau (p-tau), neurogranin, synaptosomal-associated protein 25 (SNAP-25), and neurofilament light (NFL) [11, 12]. Likewise, in-flammation underlies various complex diseases, including cardiovascular, autoimmune disorders, and metabolic disorders, and is commonly assessed using biomarkers such as C-Reactive Protein (CRP), high-sensitivity CRP, Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR), immunoglobulin E (IgE), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) [23, 30]. In such cases, genetic variants associated with individual biomark-ers may simply reflect biomarker-specific biological processes. But variants that co-regulate multiple biomarkers simultaneously are more likely to provide insight into the biological mechanisms of under-lying latent conditions. Further, identifying such pleiotropic variants can also lead to a proper genetic “instrument” for testing for causal effects of latent exposures like inflammation on health conditions [17]. When the number of biomarkers is relatively large, it may be, however, too restrictive to identify SNPs which are associated with all traits, and a more suitable formulation for the alternative hypoth-esis would be to identify SNPs which show a certain “degree” of pleiotropy, i.e. they are associated with a minimum number, say, r, of the K traits In this study, we revisit the methodology for testing for strict pleiotropy and make several novel contributions. We propose two testing methodologies to identify pleiotropic SNPs by using the z-scores readily available from GWAS studies. The first method uses a minimum of z-scores to test the alternative that a SNP affects all traits, and we demonstrate that this simplified test statistic is asymptotically equivalent to the likelihood-ratio test requiring individual-level data. For the general alternative that a SNP affects at least r of K traits, we develop a sequential test based on z-score quantiles while controlling the family-wise error rate (FWER). The advantages of the test are that it is simple, it does not involve any complex estimation method, and hence it is computationally efficient to implement, even when the number of traits is large. Moreover, its performance is robust to the dependence structure among the traits. Within the same framework, we further propose a FDR control method for testing the pleiotropy architecture across SNPs. The test accounts for the correlation among observed traits arising from overlapping samples, as well as the correlation of SNP effects across traits due to genetic correlation. While csmGmm models the mean structure of z-scores, our method models the variability of z-scores across SNPs by introducing fewer parameters to reduce computational burden for a large number of biomarkers. We also provide a step-by-step estimation procedure that facilitates the implementation 

2of the proposed test and improves computational efficiency. We conduct extensive simulation studies to compare the performance of these novel methods with that of existing methods. We illustrate the application of these two methods through pleiotropic analysis of established Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers using GWAS summary statistics.

Remark 2. In csmGmm, for each trait k, the score is modeled using a Gaussian mixture, similar to our approach. However, to capture nonzero effect sizes, the method uses pairs of shifted Gaussian components, N (−μkm , 1) and N (μkm , 1) , where μkm > 0 for m = 1 , . . . , M , with M ≥ 1. Moreover, the mean parameters used to represent nonzero SNP effects may depend on the particular configuration of the vector Z. While this construction provides a flexible way to model non-null effects, it also has some practical limitations. In genome-wide applications, significant z-scores can be quite scattered. As a result, even a large z-score may have very small local density under a component such as N (μ, 1) ,unless the observed value lies sufficiently close to the chosen mean μ. Using multiple mean parameters can help address this issue by covering a wider range of possible effect sizes. However, this introduces another practical question: how should the number of mean parameters be chosen? Increasing the number of components also increases the complexity of the model, which can make the computation challenging when the number of traits is large. A simple way to reduce this difficulty is to truncate the z-scores and then use only a small number of mean parameters. This is the approach we adopt in both the empirical study and the real data analysis. However, changing the threshold can impact the power and FDR of the method. In this section, we present simulation studies to evaluate the performance of our two proposed sequential tests and compare them with other existing methodologies: PLACO Plus and csmGmm. PLACO [29] is a statistical method developed to detect “strict” pleiotropic SNPs in the setting of two traits. For a given SNP, the PLACO test statistic is defined as the product of the corresponding z-scores from the two studies, and its null distribution is derived under the assumption that the two z-scores are independent. Later, PLACO Plus [24] was introduced to accommodate dependence between the z-scores across the two traits. However, extending this framework to more than two traits is challenging because of the increasing complexity involved in deriving the asymptotic null distribution of the product statistic. We first consider two traits so that the performance of the proposed method can be compared against both PLCO and csmGMM. We observe that, for some SNPs identified as pleiotropic by lfdr-VGM, one of the z-scores is large and the other one is not sufficiently large, such that the smaller pair is not close enough to the estimated mean induced by the alternative and therefore yields almost zero probability under the alternative and consequently is not identified as pleiotropic by csmGmm. Also, csmGmm imposes a constraint on the mean parameters: the mean parameters of the z-scores under the configuration where a SNP affects exactly r traits must be larger than those under configurations where the SNP affects fewer traits. Hence, in our setting, if one wants to allow a smaller second mean parameter under the two-trait alternative, this parameter must still be larger than the corresponding mean parameters under the single-trait alternatives. Although this constraint is maintained in csmGmm, it can create difficulties when one component of the z-score is large while the other is close to zero. In such cases, the joint density under all configurations 

Z ∈ { 0, 1} × 0, 1 may happen to be almost 0, leading to numerical issues.

# 6 Discussion 

In this article, we proposed two methods, SQT and lfdr-VGM, for identifying pleiotropic SNPs using Wald test statistics. A key novelty of both methods is that, beyond detecting pleiotropy, they can determine the minimum number of traits a SNP is associated with at a given significance level. SQT has

several practical advantages. It is simple and computationally efficient to implement, accommodates both independent and correlated traits, can be applied to an arbitrary number of traits, unlike methods such as PLACO that are restricted to a smaller number of traits, and requires only summary-level test statistics rather than individual-level data. The lfdr-VGM approach involves estimation of the underlying mixture model and is therefore computationally more involved than SQT. However, it provides a substantial power gain. While SQT controls FWER, lfdr-VGM controls FDR, allowing it to identify more pleiotropic variants while maintaining appropriate error control. In addition, lfdr-VGM explicitly models both the correlation among random errors across traits, which can arise from sample overlap, and the correlation among nonzero genetic effects. Incorporating these dependence structures into the joint distribution of the Wald statistics further improves its ability to detect pleiotropic signals. The performance of csmGmm depends on the number of nonzero mean parameters used and has power comparable to SQT in our current setting. It is also conservative in controlling the FDR at the nominal level (one mean parameter is used for each trait in the latent state where a SNP affects all traits), and is therefore substantially less powerful than the lfdr-VGM method. 

15 Another important distinction between csmGmm and lfdr-VGM concerns model complexity. For csmGmm, we consider one set of mean parameters for each trait under each latent configuration. Consequently, the number of mean parameters grows as O(K2). In contrast, the lfdr-VGM method uses a single variance parameter for each trait. Thus, as the number of traits increases, lfdr-VGM requires substantially fewer parameters and can be computationally less intensive than csmGmm. Even though lfdr-VGM maintains FDR, its performance can be sensitive to the estimated probabil-ities in situations where a configuration involving a larger number of traits is assigned a substantially higher probability than a lower-order configuration corresponding to a subset of those traits, and the lower-order configuration is not truly absent but is represented by only a small number of SNPs. We attempted to mitigate this issue by introducing a penalty in the estimation of the configuration prob-abilities. Another limitation is that the method can still become computationally challenging as the number of traits increases. An important direction for future work is therefore to develop a simplified and more scalable version of lfdr-VGM for applications involving a large number of traits.

A.1 Additional Simulation Results 

In this section, we present additional figures depicting the power of lfdr-VGM and SQT and compare these methods with other existing methods. The simulation procedure is the same as prescribed in Section 4. Recall that to perform lfdr-VGM, one intermediate step is to estimate the probabilities of the latent variable Z ∈ { 0, 1}K , with K being the number of traits. To estimate these probabilities, we introduced penalized likelihood. Figure 2 in Section 4 presents the power of lfdr-VGM with penalty 

λ = 1. That figure also presents the FDR of lfdr-VGM and the FWER of SQT for a sample size of 3000. Figure 6 presents the FDR of lfdr-VGM and the FWER of SQT for sample sizes 1000 to 5000. Next, we compare the power and FDR of lfdr-VGM if we reduce the penalty. We present Figures 4 and 5, which illustrate the power and FDR of lfdr-VGM with penalty λ = 0 .01 and 0.1, respectively, for sample sizes 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000 and 5000 and variance on non-zero effect sizes 0.01, 0.05 and 0.1. We observe that lfdr-VGM has higher power than other methods in these two figures. Also, it maintains FDR at a nominal level even after reducing λ to 0.1 and 0.01. However, when we increase 

λ to 1, the FDR for some null hypotheses is smaller than under a smaller penalty. We also investigate the performance of lfdr-VGM with λ chosen by a data-driven method, as depicted in Section 3. We generate z-scores using the estimated parameters obtained by modeling the five AD-related traits with λ = 0. Figure 7 presents the performance of automatic lfdr-VGM and SQT.
