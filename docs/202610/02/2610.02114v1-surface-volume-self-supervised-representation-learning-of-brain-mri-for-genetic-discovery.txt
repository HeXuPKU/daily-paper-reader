Abstract 

Existing genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of brain imaging provide pre-defined or deep-learning-derived imaging phenotypes, yet these phenotypes come from either volumetric scans or cortical surface meshes, so each captures only part of the heritable variation in brain anatomy. Here we introduce MEVA (Mesh-Enhanced Volumetric Autoencoder), a self-supervised framework that encodes voxel-level image intensity together with cortical mesh geometry, including curva-ture and cortical thickness at each surface vertex, into one shared set of imaging features. Combining the mesh and volumetric inputs in MEVA yields modest performance gains in age and sex prediction over models that use either input alone. When these features serve as phenotypes for GWAS in the UK Biobank, they reveal more genome-wide significant loci than features learned from volumes alone or from meshes alone. These results suggest that adding cortical surface geometry to volumetric self-supervised learning captures additional heritable variation and so increases the number of loci detected. 

1arXiv:2610.02114v1 [cs.CV] 1 Oct 2026 Introduction 

Self-supervised learning has become a central tool for extracting structured represen-tations from unlabeled data in domains ranging from natural language to biomedical imaging. In neuroimaging, autoencoders and contrastive learning frameworks trained on brain MRI learn compact representations that capture structural variation across a population without predefined anatomical atlases or expert-curated features 1,2 . These learned representations, sometimes termed imaging endophenotypes , are appealing targets for genetic association studies, as they are high-dimensional representations learned directly from the images and may therefore be sensitive to biological variation beyond what hand-crafted imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) encode 1,3 .Recent advances in machine learning offer flexible methods that learn salient fea-tures directly from raw imaging data such as T1-weighted brain MRI, capturing fine-grained structural detail and producing data-driven representations of the under-lying anatomy 4. A growing body of work has applied deep learning to derive image features for genetic discovery, spanning supervised approaches that rely on disease labels or genetic signals 5–11 and unsupervised or self-supervised methods that learn representations without annotation. In the supervised setting, Yu et al. 12 used single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotypes as class labels to train convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on 2D brain MRI slices, which amounts to a non-linear genome-wide association study (GWAS) but sacrifices interpretability and requires a separate classifier for each of more than 300,000 SNPs. In the unsupervised setting, Patel et al. 1 introduced Unsupervised Deep learning derived Imaging Phenotypes (UDIPs), compact 128-dimensional representations learned by 3D convolutional autoencoders directly from raw brain MRI, and GWAS of UDIPs showed higher heritability than most conventional IDPs and uncovered loci absent from earlier IDP-based GWAS. Subsequent work replaced the convolutional encoder with a vision transformer, which captures both local and long-range structure in the image and revealed further genetic associations 13 . Rakowski et al. 3 took a transfer-learning approach, applying mod-els pretrained on brain MRI from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) or on natural images (ImageNet) to UK Biobank T1-weighted scans to derive low-dimensional representations for GWAS. All of these volumetric approaches, how-ever, operate on 3D intensity arrays alone and leave the folded geometry of the cortical surface unmodeled. Compared with the growing volumetric literature, the use of mesh- and shape-based deep learning for genetic discovery remains sparse. Bonazzola et al. 14 applied unsupervised ensemble-based phenotyping to 3D surface meshes of the left ventricle and showed that learned mesh phenotypes yield genetic loci beyond those discoverable from handcrafted cardiac traits such as volume and mass. In the brain, Dufournet et al. 2 trained a self-supervised contrastive model on sulcal representations of cortical folding and identified genetic influences on folding patterns of the anterior cingulate cortex. In a complementary direction, a line of work has treated geometric shape descriptors themselves as multivariate genetic phenotypes. Ge et al. 15 extended her-itability estimation to multidimensional traits and showed that shape descriptors of subcortical structures are significantly heritable above and beyond volume. Building 2on this idea, Primus et al. 16 used the Laplace–Beltrami spectrum to encode the geom-etry of 22 subcortical brain structures and the cerebellum and performed multivariate GWAS, identifying 80 unique variants influencing brain shape after controlling for vol-ume and surface area. At the cortical surface, Naqvi et al. 17 performed a multivariate GWAS of hierarchically segmented cortical surface morphology in 19,644 individuals, identifying 472 loci influencing brain shape, 76 of which were also associated with face shape. Shadrin et al. 18 applied the multivariate omnibus statistical test (MOSTest) to vertex-wise cortical surface area and thickness in 35,657 UK Biobank participants and identified 780 unique loci, more than an eightfold increase over univariate region-of-interest GWAS. Beyond the brain itself, Goovaerts et al. 19 conducted a multivariate GWAS of 3D cranial vault shape and identified 30 loci, several of them shared with face and brain shape. Despite these efforts, to our knowledge no prior work has used a self-supervised framework to derive mesh-based brain representations for genetic discovery. A recurring finding across organs and imaging modalities is that genetic asso-ciations from volumetric and surface-derived phenotypes overlap only in part and otherwise diverge substantially. In the heart, Bonazzola et al. 14 found that of 49 study-wide significant loci identified by their deep mesh framework, only 9 had been reported in prior GWAS of left-ventricular phenotypes, and 20 were attributable exclusively to the mesh-based representation. Burns et al. 20 similarly reported 43 loci from princi-pal components of bi-ventricular surface meshes, 14 of which had not previously been associated with cardiac traits. In the brain, Primus et al. 16 showed that, after con-trolling for volume and surface area, 49 of 80 shape-associated variants had previously been linked to brain shape-related traits, whereas the remaining 31 were novel, con-firming shape-specific signal beyond gross volumetric measures. Van der Meer et al. 21 

found substantial genetic overlap between sulcal depth and other cortical traits such as thickness and surface area, yet sulcal depth still showed a higher locus yield and higher heritability. Naqvi et al. 17 reported that a multivariate GWAS of cortical surface shape recaptured part of the signal from prior surface-area and subcortical-volume GWAS (121 of 472 loci), whereas most loci (351) were newly described. The consistent pattern across these studies is that volumetric measures capture coarse size variation, whereas mesh and shape representations encode geometry that is at least partly genetically independent. Most comparisons to date, however, rest on catalog-based overlap counts after covariate adjustment and stop short of formal colocalization or fine-mapping, so the mechanistic relationship between the two signal sources remains incompletely resolved. This partial complementarity motivates a framework that captures both sources of heritable variation at once. We address this gap with the Mesh-Enhanced Volumetric Autoencoder (MEVA), a dual-stream architecture that uses volumetric MRI and cortical surface representations together. The volumetric stream encodes the T1-weighted MRI volume, whereas the mesh stream separately encodes vertex-wise cortical features derived from FreeSurfer-reconstructed surfaces 22 . MEVA encodes each modality on its own and then combines the two representations through a residual fusion step. We first trained and evaluated the two streams separately. GWAS of each stream showed that the mesh representation identified about a quarter as many significant 3loci as the volumetric representation (40 versus 153), yet the loci found by the two streams overlapped only in part. These findings suggest that the mesh representation carries weaker genetic signal overall but captures complementary genetic information that the volumetric stream only partly represents. This observation motivated residual fusion over direct feature fusion. With the volumetric representation carrying much stronger genetic signal, unconstrained fusion risks either letting the volumetric stream dominate the joint representation and sup-press mesh-specific information, or perturbing the strong volumetric representation and degrading genetic discovery. We therefore treat the volumetric representation as the primary one and learn the contribution of the mesh stream as a residual correction on top of it. This design preserves the genetic information already captured by the vol-umetric stream while allowing complementary mesh-derived information to improve the representation where it is informative. In the limiting case where the mesh stream adds little, the fused representation falls back toward the volumetric representation and performance is largely retained. We evaluate MEVA in three stages. First, we assess how much phenotypic infor-mation the learned representations carry through prediction of age and sex as well as mesh-derived IDPs, comparing MEVA with volumetric-only and mesh-only baselines to quantify the benefit of integration. Second, we perform GWAS on each dimension of the learned representation in the UK Biobank 23 discovery cohort, using the same individuals and pipeline for all models so that differences in locus counts and identity reflect the representation alone. Third, we will carry out downstream genetic analyses (enrichment of gene sets and tissue expression, together with genetic correlation with clinical phenotypes) to establish the biological relevance of MEVA-specific discoveries, and we will examine the encoder to learn where and why the mesh stream contributes new signal. This paper makes three contributions: 

• We demonstrate that cortical mesh features learned with a self-supervised autoen-coder carry predictive and genetic signal complementary to that of volumetric representations. 

• We show that MEVA, by combining both streams, increases the number of genome-wide significant loci beyond either single-modality baseline. 

• We will provide an explainability analysis to test whether the mesh and volumetric streams contribute to distinct genetic axes, which would explain why the combined representation discovers more loci.

## Genetic discovery from multimodal representations 

The third analysis compares genetic associations across representations in a common UK Biobank discovery cohort, using the same participants, covariates and association pipeline for every representation (Methods, Genome-wide association analysis). Com-bining the per-phenotype summary statistics with the minimum-P method, followed by FUMA locus clumping, MEVA yielded 188 loci, compared with 153 for the volume-only and 40 for the mesh-only representation, and it had the highest summed SNP heritability (36.7, versus 33.7 and 10.5; Table 1). Because locus count alone do not show what fusion adds, we compared the loci across representations, treating two loci as the same when their intervals, padded by 125 kb on each side, overlapped on the 6same chromosome (Fig. 3a; Methods, Locus overlap analysis). # Discussion 

In this work, we introduced MEVA, a dual-stream autoencoder that augments a vol-umetric MRI representation with a residual contribution from cortical mesh features, and showed that this integration yields a representation that is both more phenotypi-cally informative and more genetically discoverable than either modality alone. MEVA achieved the best performance on both phenotype prediction tasks, reaching a Pearson correlation of 0.776 for brain age (compared with 0.768 for the volume-only and 0.671 for the mesh-only representation) and an AUC of 0.984 for sex classification (compared with 0.976 and 0.886, respectively). Beyond improved prediction, MEVA identified 188 genome-wide significant loci, exceeding both the volume-only (153 loci) and mesh-only (40 loci) baselines, and 42 of these loci were detected by neither unimodal represen-tation. MEVA also attained the highest summed SNP heritability ( P h2SNP = 36 .7, compared with 33.7 and 10.5) 24 , indicating that the additional loci reflect a genuine gain in captured heritable variation rather than a redistribution of existing signal across latent dimensions. Notably, although the mesh-only representation carried the weakest genetic signal on its own, adding it as a residual to the volumetric stream substantially increased the cortical thickness variance explained (∆ R2 = 0 .375 versus 0.091 for the volume-only model), showing that the fused phenotypes retain corti-cal information supplied by the mesh stream. Together, these results support residual 7fusion as an effective strategy for combining a strong primary modality with a weaker but complementary one, preserving the genetic signal of the volumetric representation while extending it with mesh-derived information. Residual fusion is only one of several strategies for integrating volumetric and sur-face representations, and alternative designs may capture complementary signal in different ways. A first alternative is contrastive alignment, in which modality-specific encoders are trained to map paired views of the same individual to nearby points in a shared embedding space 25 . We previously showed that contrastive learning across brain imaging modalities yields heritable multimodal representations 26 , and extending this objective to volume–mesh pairs could encourage the model to emphasize anatom-ical variation that is consistently expressed in both modalities. However, because contrastive objectives reward shared information, they may attenuate precisely the mesh-specific signal that residual fusion is designed to preserve. A second alternative is allocation-based fusion, in which the latent budget is explicitly partitioned into shared and modality-specific dimensions, or the contribution of each modality is adaptively weighted per dimension. Such a design would make the division of labor between the volumetric and mesh streams explicit, and would allow loci to be attributed to shared or modality-specific axes directly rather than through post hoc interpretation. A third alternative is fine-grained cross-attention between local tokens of the two modali-ties 27,28 , for example between 3D patches of the MRI volume and vertices or vertex patches of the cortical mesh. Because each cortical vertex has a well-defined spatial location in the MRI volume, cross-attention at the patch and vertex level could learn anatomically grounded correspondences and allow surface geometry to modulate the volumetric features of the underlying tissue, at the cost of substantially higher compu-tational and memory demands. Systematically comparing these fusion strategies under the same GWAS pipeline and evaluation framework 24 will be an important direction for determining which inductive biases best expose heritable brain variation. The present analyses indicate that MEVA improves both phenotypic prediction and locus discovery, but they do not yet characterize the biology underlying the MEVA-specific loci. We are therefore extending this work in two directions. First, we are performing downstream genetic analyses of the MEVA GWAS, including gene-based and gene-set enrichment with MAGMA 29 , tissue-expression enrichment using GTEx 30 , and genetic correlation with neurological, psychiatric, and cardiometabolic phenotypes 31 , to determine whether the loci gained through fusion map to neuro-biologically coherent pathways. Second, we are developing an explainability analysis that tests whether the fusion model’s attributions recover genetically informed cor-tical topography at the 42 fusion-specific lead variants. This analysis compares decoder-perturbation and input-gradient maps derived from the MEVA encoder with gene-expression-weighted cortical profiles constructed from the Allen Human Brain Atlas 32 , weighted by MAGMA gene associations, GTEx brain eQTL effects, or brain transcriptome-wide association statistics 33 . Concordance between the two would indi-cate that the mesh stream contributes signal that is anatomically localized to the cortical regions where the implicated genes are expressed, providing a mechanistic account of why the integrated representation discovers loci that neither unimodal baseline detects. 8Methods 

## UK Biobank participants 

The UK Biobank 34 is a nationwide population resource encompassing approxi-mately 500,000 participants recruited across the United Kingdom, with baseline phenotypic and genetic data collected between 2006 and 2010 and brain imag-ing from 2014 onwards. The study operates under established ethical approval, with governance and oversight details available at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/ learn-more-about-uk-biobank/governance/ethics-advisory-committee. Models were developed in the quality-controlled subset of participants described below. Phenotypes were then extracted for 35,298 participants with T1-weighted MRI, cortical surfaces and imputed genotypes 35 who were not used for model training; these participants form the genetic analysis cohort for every representation. The genetic analysis cohort had a mean age at imaging of 67.8 years (s.d. 7.7; range 46–87). 

## MRI acquisition and volumetric preprocessing 

UK Biobank MRIs were downloaded on October 15, 2021. UK Biobank participants had birth years between 1934 and 1971. UK Biobank has provided a bias-field-corrected version of the brain-extracted T1-weighted (T1) acquired on Siemens Skyra 3T scanners (software VD13A SP4 as of October 2015) with a standard Siemens 32-channel RF receive head coil, at 1-mm isotropic resolution (https://biobank.ctsu.ox. ac.uk/crystal/crystal/docs/brain mri.pdf). To promote generalizability and minimize manual feature engineering, we adopted the standard preprocessing pipeline developed by the UK Biobank MRI team. Brain MRI preprocessing was performed primarily using FSL (https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/ ukbiobank/), and included defacing, brain extraction with BET, linear and non-linear registration to standard MNI152 space using FLIRT and FNIRT, and bias-field cor-rection using FAST. Bias-field-corrected, brain-extracted T1-weighted images were retained for analysis. All images were subsequently linearly registered to MNI152 space using affine transformation with 12 degrees of freedom via UK Biobank-provided precomputed matrices in FLIRT. Linear registration was chosen to normalize head size and achieve cross-subject alignment while preserving subject-specific structural deformations, in contrast to non-linear registration. The resulting linearly registered, defaced, bias-field-corrected images were used in all downstream analyses. Image inten-sities were normalized on a per-scan basis using Z-score normalization. Following affine registration, T1 weighted volumes had dimensions of 182 × 218 × 182 voxels and were zero-padded to 182 × 224 × 182. To construct a high-quality deep learning dataset, we leveraged UK Biobank’s precomputed MRI quality metrics: the inverted contrast-to-noise ratio (Data Field 25735) and the discrepancy between T2 FLAIR and T1 brain images (Data Field 25736). Images with values below the 95th percentile for both metrics (correspond-ing to higher-quality scans) were retained. For participants with multiple visits, only the first scan was kept to ensure consistency across the dataset. This filtering resulted 9in a cohort of 6,130 scans from individuals of diverse ethnic backgrounds. The pre-trained volumetric autoencoder was trained on a random split of these scans into 4,597 training (75%) and 1,533 validation (25%) images, with the validation set reserved for hyperparameter tuning and checkpointing. The mesh autoencoder and MEVA were trained on the 6,067 of these participants with usable cortical surfaces. 

## Cortical surface preparation 

Cortical surfaces were reconstructed with FreeSurfer and resampled to the fsaverage4 icosahedral mesh, giving 2,562 vertices per hemisphere. Each vertex carries five chan-nels: the x, y and z coordinates of the mid-thickness surface, cortical thickness and curvature. The coordinates of each hemisphere were centred and scaled with a sin-gle centre and scale estimated on the training set, and thickness and curvature were standardized with training-set means and standard deviations. 

## Mesh autoencoder 

The mesh-only encoder is a masked graph autoencoder with 33,733 parameters, applied with shared weights to the fsaverage4 mesh graph of each hemisphere. The three coordinate channels and the two morphometric channels are embedded sep-arately by two-layer perceptrons into 32 channels and summed. A graph U-Net backbone with graph convolutions, two levels of top-k pooling (ratio 0.8) and unpool-ing with skip connections returns 32-channel vertex features, from which a vertex-wise perceptron reconstructs the five input channels. During training, 60% of the vertices are masked by setting their inputs to zero, 70% of them as contiguous two-hop patches around 16 seed vertices and the rest at random, and the loss is the mean squared reconstruction error over all vertices. The embedding zmesh is obtained by averaging the vertex features within each hemisphere, concatenating the two hemisphere means and mapping them through a two-layer perceptron to 128 dimensions. The model was trained on 4,853 participants, with 607 for validation and 607 held out, with a learn-ing rate of 10 −3, weight decay of 10 −4 and a batch size of one for 100 epochs; the checkpoint with the lowest validation reconstruction error (epoch 98) was retained. No genetic information was used. 

## MEVA fusion and training 

MEVA combines the pretrained volumetric autoencoder with the frozen mesh encoder (Fig. 2). A fusion module f computes a residual δ = f (zvol , z mesh ), and the fused phenotypes are 

zfused = zvol + δ. (1) The last layer of f is initialized to zero, so training begins from the volumetric rep-resentation, and zfused is passed to the convolutional decoder to reconstruct the T1 image. Training used 5,461 participants, with 606 for validation and a batch size of two, in two stages. In stage 1 (4 epochs), f is a residual multilayer perceptron applied to [ zvol , z mesh ] (layer normalization, linear 256 → 128, GELU, linear 128 → 128), and the loss combines masked T1 reconstruction with an instance-discrimination InfoNCE 10 loss (weight 0.1, temperature 0.1, queue of 512) whose positive pairs are fused embed-dings of two augmented views of the same T1 image. The convolutional encoder was fine-tuned with a learning rate of 5 ×10 −5 and the fusion module with 10 −3. In stage 2 (6 epochs), the encoder and a learned mesh adapter are retained and f is replaced by a two-token transformer: zvol and the adapted zmesh are processed as two tokens by two layers of four-head self-attention, concatenated and projected to δ. The InfoNCE term is removed, and a distillation head D (linear 128 → 128, GELU, linear 128 → 128) predicts zmesh from zfused with the target under stop-gradient, 

L = LT1 + λ Lmesh , λ = 10 . (2) The final epoch of each stage was used. Surface information therefore enters MEVA only through zmesh and the distillation loss; vertex features are not reconstructed. The modules added to the volumetric autoencoder (adapter, fusion block and distillation head) have about 0.18 million parameters (Supplementary Table S2). 

## Volumetric autoencoder baselines and training 

The volumetric stream uses the CNN autoencoder of our previous work 1. The volume-only representation in this study is the pretrained CNN autoencoder (138.1 million parameters, trained for 40 epochs), used without further training; it also initializes the MEVA volume encoder. Briefly, the CNN architecture is an autoencoder with CNN blocks of 3 × 3 × 3kernels and 2 × 2 × 2 max pooling. The model consists of 5 encoding CNN blocks, a fully connected layer with dimension 128 and 5 decoding CNN blocks. 

## Embedding extraction 

For each representation, the encoder was frozen and the decoder and any auxil-iary heads were removed. The MEVA encoder takes both the T1 image and zmesh 

computed from the participant’s cortical surface. The volume-only, mesh-only and MEVA encoders were applied to the same 35,298 genetic-analysis participants, giving a 35 ,298 × 128 phenotype matrix per representation. 

## Phenotypic prediction and statistical analysis 

Phenotypes were evaluated in the 35,298 held-out participants. Age was predicted by ridge regression ( α = 1) with five-fold cross-validation and summarized by the Pearson correlation and mean absolute error between predicted and observed age. Sex was predicted by class-balanced logistic regression with stratified five-fold cross-validation and summarized by the AUC. Cortical information was measured as the incremental variance explained, ∆ R2 = R2(measure ∼ phenotypes + covariates) −

R2(measure ∼ covariates), from ordinary least squares fitted in sample and averaged over the components of each measure set: FreeSurfer regional thickness, surface area and grey-matter volume of the 68 Desikan–Killiany regions 36 . Confidence intervals were obtained from 500 bootstrap resamples of participants; for ∆ R2, both models 11 were refitted on every resample and the interval is the point estimate ±1.96 bootstrap standard errors. 

## Genotype quality control 

We applied the quality-control pipeline of our previous work 1 to the imputed autoso-mal genotypes. Duplicated variants were removed, and individuals whose genetically inferred sex did not match their self-reported sex were excluded. 

## Genome-wide association analysis 

For each representation, the 128 phenotypes were tested on their original scale, with-out rank-based inverse normal transformation, for association with 8,931,083 variants in the 35,298 participants, using fastGWA 37 with covariates, including age, age 2,sex, their interactions, genetic principal components. The per-phenotype summary statistics were combined together with minimal p value, and genome-wide significance was set at P < 5 × 10 −8/128. Total SNP heritability across multiple phenotypes was estimated with a sum-h2 pipeline 24 . Basically, we define the total heritability as tr( P −1G), and it could be estimated by first using PCA to decorrelate phenotypes, fol-lowed by sum up heritability from single phenotype estimated by the Haseman–Elston regression. 

## Genomic locus annotation 

Genomic loci were annotated using FUMA 38 . Lead SNPs were first identified based on linkage disequilibrium ( r2 ≤ 0.1) and physical proximity ( <250 kb), and assigned to non-overlapping genomic loci. Within each locus, the SNP with the lowest p-value (the top lead SNP) was used to represent the locus. 

## Locus overlap analysis 

To assess locus overlap, each identified locus was extended by 125 kb on either side, and interval trees were constructed for each chromosome. Loci from our analysis were then queried against these interval trees to determine overlapping regions.
