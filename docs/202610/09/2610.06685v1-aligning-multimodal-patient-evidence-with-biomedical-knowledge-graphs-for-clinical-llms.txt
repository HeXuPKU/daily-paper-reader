Foci Labs (fo.ci) 4University of Pennsylvania (upenn.edu) Clinical questions often depend on linking a patient’s multimodal evidence to external biomedical knowledge, yet existing predictive systems rarely represent such links explicitly, so they can neither be traced to their evidence sources nor removed to measure their contributions. We present MM-KG ( Multi modal Knowledge Graph), which represents heterogeneous, multimodal patient observations and biomedical concepts as separate layers in one typed graph, joined by explicit alignment edges. First, modality-specific harmonizers convert EHR text, imaging, genomic, and biospecimen data into typed observations mapped to UMLS concepts, which a route-prioritized aligner links to a biomedical knowledge graph. Query-conditioned retrieval then selects a compact subgraph for downstream use by a large language model or a graph neural network. We build MM-KGs for MIMIC-IV and ADNI, and evaluate them with a 2 × 2 design that separates patient evidence, biomedical knowledge, and their interaction. On questions that require both sources, neither source alone performs far above chance, whereas their combination yields a drug-controlled AUROC interaction of +0 .194 on MIMIC and +0 .299 on ADNI. On held-out five-candidate ranking, MM-KG outperforms MindMap by +0 .131 Hits@1 and leads an adapted GraphCare on the items that require consulting the patient, and deleting the single answer-bearing relation from the retrieved packet returns Hits@1 to the no-knowledge baseline. Finally, query-conditioned retrieval reaches 0.731 AUROC with 6.8× less context than the strongest generic policy, whereas static knowledge graph context gives no consistent gain on ordinary outcome prediction. Knowledge graphs thus benefit clinical LLMs not as background context but as explicit links between multimodal patient evidence and the relation a question requires, and MM-KG makes these links retrievable, traceable, and testable.

1 Introduction 

Reasoning about an individual clinical case requires connecting heterogeneous patient observations to general biomedical knowledge. Patient records describe observations , e.g., laboratory measurements, images, reports, medications, longitudinal events (Johnson et al., 2023; 2019b); whereas biomedical knowledge bases describe general relations among diseases, drugs, genes, and phenotypes (Chandak et al., 2023; Bodenreider, 2004; Stear et al., 2024). Neither is sufficient alone when a question depends on a link between the two, and such questions are common: across 28,582 MIMIC-IV ICU stays, 29.9% exhibit a laboratory-evident organ state that never appears in the coded diagnoses while the patient receives a medication that PrimeKG lists as contraindicated for that state (App. B). Answering such questions correctly requires reasoning about the current measurement and the drug-specific relation; either source in isolation leaves the question undecidable. Existing works model each of these sides, but rarely their connection. Multimodal clinical models fuse EHR data, text, and images into predictive representations, but discard the links from individual observations to biomedical concepts (Hayat et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2024b;a). Knowledge-graph methods supply external facts or build patient-specific graphs for prediction and language-model reasoning (Jiang et al., 2024; 2025; Wen et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Their patient side, however, is typically derived from structured codes or clinical text alone; imaging, genomic, and biospecimen evidence is

Where multiple modalities are represented graphically, they are generally not linked to standardized biomedical concepts (Wu et al., 2024). Consequently, no explicit path runs from a source observation, through its normalized concept, to the biomedical relation used to interpret it, and the connection cannot be retrieved or ablated. We introduce MM-KG ( Multi modal Knowledge Graph), which represents multimodal patient evidence and a biomedical knowledge backbone in a single typed graph. Each modality passes through its own harmonizer, converting raw input into typed observation nodes rather than a fused vector. A vision-language model turns chest radiographs into calibrated textual findings; brain MRIs are segmented into regional abnormality findings under a normative model; and codes, measurements, and report text are resolved by vocabulary lookup, value-aware rules, and entity linking. Provenance edges retain each observation’s source and timestamp, and a route-prioritized aligner links the resulting concepts to PrimeKG (Chandak et al., 2023), preferring exact identifier routes over synonym and parent hops (§3.3). Because instance and ontology nodes keep separate identities, each layer can be traced or removed independently. Query-conditioned retrieval then assembles a compact subgraph per question, joining the relevant patient neighborhood to candidate-conditioned domain relations under deterministic rules with no access to the label. The result can then be serialized for a downstream LLM or used as input to a GNN, exposing a path from source observations, to aligned concepts, to the relation supporting an answer (Fig. 1). This explicit structure makes the patient evidence, biomedical knowledge, and their alignment separately traceable and testable. We build two independently constructed graphs: 51,844 MIMIC-IV patients with chest radiographs and reports, and 1,000 ADNI participants spanning longitudinal imaging, history, cognition, genetics, and biospecimens (Veitch et al., 2022). Because instance and ontology layers remain separately addressable, a 2×2 design can isolate patient evidence, KG relations, and their interaction (§3.5), a decomposition which fused representations do not support. Our primary evaluation targets questions in which neither source is sufficient alone. On 3,200 controlled MIMIC questions that link a condition documented in laboratory values or radiology reports to a drug relation, each source alone leaves performance near chance, whereas together they yield a drug-controlled AUROC interaction of +0 .194 (p< 0.001 ), reproduced on the 70-item ADNI test set at +0 .299 across a different disease domain and evidence channel. On held-out five-candidate ranking, MM-KG exceeds MindMap by +0 .131 Hits@1 and leads an adapted GraphCare on the items a candidate-only prior cannot resolve (0.582 against 0.375), while deleting the answer-bearing relation, replacing or shuffling packets reduces Hits@1 from 0.575 to 0.185– 0.299 against 0.200 chance. The effect therefore requires the specific correspondence that alignment makes explicit, which query-conditioned retrieval supplies at AUROC 0.731 with 1,206 input tokens,

Our contributions are summarized as follows. (1) A typed instance–knowledge graph construction framework in which multimodal observations, their provenance, and their alignment to a biomedical backbone remain separately addressable, so that each layer can be traced or ablated rather than fused away. (2) Query-conditioned retrieval that joins patient evidence to candidate-conditioned relations, recovering the answer path for 99.7% of positive items and reaching AUROC 0.731 with 6.8× less context than the strongest vector, full-context, or typed one-hop policy. (3) A factorial evaluation showing that patient evidence and KG relations combine superadditively on questions that require both, yielding interactions of +0 .194 (MIMIC) and +0 .299 (ADNI) AUROC, and that removing or corrupting the answer-bearing alignment returns held-out ranking to the no-knowledge level or below, whereas static KG context alone does not improve outcome prediction. MM-KG construction, retrieval, benchmark, and evaluation code will be released upon acceptance. 1

2 Related work 

Multimodal clinical representation. Clinical representation learning has progressed from longitudinal EHR models such as RETAIN (Choi et al., 2016) and GRAM (Choi et al., 2017) to multimodal architec-tures that combine structured records, text, and medical images. MedFuse (Hayat et al., 2022), DrFuse (Yao et al., 2024b), and DDL-CXR study fusion (Yao et al., 2024a) under missing or asynchronous modalities. MedGTX (Park et al., 2022) jointly encodes structured EHR graphs and clinical text, while MUSE (Wu et al., 2024) represents patient–modality relationships with a bipartite graph and evaluates on both MIMIC and ADNI. MedMod (Elsharief et al., 2025) provides a benchmark for multimodal prediction using MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-CXR. These methods are important prediction baselines, but they do not explicitly preserve the paths from source observations through normalized biomedical concepts to external biomedical relations. 

Patient representations and biomedical KGs. Multimodal KGs associate symbolic entities with het-erogeneous attributes or evidence (Chen et al., 2024). Biomedical resources such as PrimeKG and PetaGraph (Chandak et al., 2023; Stear et al., 2024) provide large concept-level backbones but do not by themselves represent a patient’s multimodal clinical history. Several methods enrich patient or code representations with biomedical knowledge. MMUGL (Burger et al., 2023) maps structured codes and concepts extracted from clinical text into a shared UMLS-derived representation; ClinVec and MedTok combine standardized codes with textual or relational knowledge (Johnson et al., 2026; Su et al., 2025);and GraphCare constructs patient-specific graphs using external biomedical resources (Jiang et al., 2024). KG4Diagnosis proposes a hierarchical multi-agent framework that constructs a graph from unstructured medical text for diagnostic reasoning (Zuo et al., 2025). These studies show the value of combining patient records with biomedical knowledge; MM-KG builds on this further by retaining source-level multimodal observations, recording how each was derived, and linking the resulting concepts explicitly to a shared biomedical backbone. 

Knowledge retrieval for clinical prediction. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020) retrieves external evidence before producing an answer. In clinical prediction, RAM-EHR re-trieves relevant information from external knowledge sources (Xu et al., 2024), while KARE organizes knowledge into communities and retrieves community-level evidence for healthcare prediction (Jiang et al., 2025). RAD retrieves disease-centered guideline knowledge and uses it to align multimodal features for clinical diagnosis (Li et al., 2025). KARE and RAD are especially relevant comparators, but they use retrieved knowledge primarily to construct summaries or guide latent representations. In contrast, MM-KG retrieves an explicit typed subgraph spanning both patient evidence and biomedical relations, which can be consumed by an LLM or a heterogeneous GNN.

DOSSIER checks claims against structured patient records and biomedical knowledge graphs while limiting the exposure of private records to the LLM (Zhang et al., 2024). 3 Method: multimodal instance–knowledge graphs 

Overview. MM-KG represents patient evidence and biomedical knowledge within a single graph while keeping their node sets disjoint, connected only by explicit alignment edges. This separation is the central design decision. Because each alignment edge is an individually addressable object rather than a component of a fused representation, it can be retrieved, traced, and removed, and the contribution of a given patient–relation correspondence to a downstream prediction can be measured directly rather than inferred. Modalities are accordingly not fused into a shared embedding, and the benefit comes from the correspondence rather than from added graph text: length-matched graph text with the correspondence disrupted reduces held-out ranking to near chance (§5.3).

3.3 Ontology alignment 

Alignment assigns at most one aligns-to edge per observation, so deleting that edge detaches the observation from its concept, and from every relation of that concept, while leaving the rest of the graph unchanged. For observation z, the aligner collects candidate concepts Cm(z) ⊆ VO through modality-specific mapping routes, such as exact identifiers, synonyms, and parent concepts, selects 

c∗ = arg max c∈Cm(z) sm(z, c ), and adds (z, aligns-to , c ∗) to E only if Γm(z, c ∗) = 1 . Here sm ranks candidates by mapping route (exact identifier, then synonym, then guarded parent, then blocklisted name match) , and Γm a type-and-route validity check; in our instantiation acceptance is route-based rather than thresholded. Exact identifiers are preferred over synonym or parent routes, observations without a valid mapping remain on the instance side, and all mappings are frozen before evaluation. App. C gives complete routes and coverage statistics.

4 Datasets and Implementation 

We instantiate MM-KG as independently constructed MIMIC and ADNI graphs linked to PrimeKG (Chandak et al., 2023), using the same provenance-preserving construction with dataset-specific ex-tractors (Appendices B, H, and K). The MIMIC graph covers 51,844 patients, combining MIMIC-IV EHR events (Johnson et al., 2023) with MIMIC-CXR radiographs and reports (Johnson et al., 2019a;b): diagnoses, prescriptions, procedures, and laboratory measurements become provenance-linked obser-vations, and BiomedCLIP (Zhang et al., 2023) with CheXpert-calibrated thresholds (Irvin et al., 2019) converts radiographs into textual findings. The ADNI graph covers 1,000 participants and 4,977 visits spanning imaging, medical history, cognition, genetics, and biospecimens (Veitch et al., 2022); regional brain-MRI features from FastSurfer (Henschel et al., 2020) are compared with normative models, their deviations become imaging findings, and direction-aware anchors to HPO phenotypes connect these findings to the other clinical domains. We evaluate outcome prediction using 28,582 MIMIC ICU stays and 619 ADNI participants, biomedical fact-checking using 1,920 balanced questions, and patient-specific joins using 3,200 MIMIC items and the ADNI paired contraindication benchmark. The ADNI benchmark contains 510 development items, a 114-item validation set and a 70-item test set; the latter provides the primary cross-cohort ADNI result. The primary MIMIC experiments use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and a two-layer heterogeneous GraphSAGE consumer, while the primary ADNI test evaluation uses Qwen3.5-9B with thinking disabled. We report AUROC or balanced accuracy as appropriate, with paired-item bootstrap inference for MIMIC and participant-clustered inference for ADNI. Further details on entity linking, evaluation settings, model implementation, and ADNI-specific evaluation details are provided in Appendices C, D, J, and L, respectively.

5 Experiments and Results 

We will evaluate MM-KG on whether patient evidence and biomedical knowledge interact (§5.1), how MM-KG compares with external methods (§5.2), whether the interaction depends on the alignment edges (§5.3), retrieval context efficiency (§5.4), and how each source performs alone (§5.5). 

5.1 Aligned patient and knowledge evidence is superadditive 

Because instance and ontology nodes remain separately addressable, either evidence source can be withheld or supplied independently, which permits the 2×2 design of §3.5: condition A receives neither source, B the patient modalities, C the KG relations, and D both. Fused representations do not admit this design. We call the two sources superadditive when the improvement from supplying both exceeds the sum of the improvements from supplying each alone, that is, when the interaction IM of §3.5 is positive. The MIMIC join benchmark targets questions for which such an interaction is expected: across all 28,582 MIMIC-IV ICU stays, 29.9% exhibit a laboratory-evident organ state, (e.g., creatinine >2.0, bilirubin 

>2.0, platelets <100 , or hemoglobin <8) that is absent from the coded diagnoses while PrimeKG lists a current medication as contraindicated for that state. Items come from four question families with same-patient matched negatives and length-matched KG lists (App. E).

5.3 Patient–KG interaction requires correct alignment, not graph text 

Because retrieval returns each relation as a separately addressable object, the patient–relation corre-spondence can be disrupted while the patient evidence and nearly all of the graph text stay in place. We apply three such disruptions to condition D on the five-candidate MIMIC ranking benchmark of §5.2: deleting the single answer-bearing relation from the answer candidate’s packet, replacing the answer candidate’s packet (the relations retrieved for it) with a length-matched packet from another candidate, and shuffling packets across the five candidates. All three fall below condition D ( p < 0.001 ;Table 4): deleting the edge returns Hits@1 to the level of condition A, and shuffling drives it below the 0.200 chance level. Because the decoys are matched on prescription frequency, graph degree, packet length, and drug class, the drop reflects the loss of the correspondence rather than of graph text. The ADNI 70-item test set shows the same ordering, with all four prespecified disruptions below condition D (after Holm correction): removing the decisive edge lowers AUROC from 0.860 to 0.714, and keeping node names while removing their relations returns it to the level of condition A. MM-KG also cites the evidence it uses: on the ADNI validation set, all 85 attempted KG citations referred to relations in the supplied packet, and after the decisive edge was removed the model never cited it (App. L). The patient-side contribution localizes in the same way. On MIMIC, removing laboratory values or report findings reduces Hits@1 by 0.148 and 0.123 ( p< 0.001 ), whereas removing imaging findings or coded history has no measurable effect (App. N.3); on the ADNI validation set, medical history alone with the KG reaches AUROC 0.831 against 0.853 for the full record, and it is the only patient block whose enablement estimate ( +0 .294 ) exceeds the prespecified minimum detectable interaction of 0.137 (App. L). The interaction therefore depends on the alignment edges: mismatched packets return both cohorts to the no-knowledge level, and in each cohort the interaction arises from the evidence block that contains the condition the question concerns.

5.5 MM-KG’s multimodal patient representations for outcome prediction 

Finally, we examine each source on tasks that do not require their combination. For ICU mortality, the zero-shot LLM reaches AUROC 0.733 with the full MM-KG prompt, against 0.570 with demographics alone, and the trained GNN reaches 0.804, against 0.778 for a laboratory-only model; laboratory values contribute most for both (Table 5). The GNN also outperforms logistic regression on 30-day readmission (0.644 against 0.548; App. J), the harmonizer’s structured imaging findings exceed the radiologist’s verbatim prose by +0 .025 AUROC (App. H), and in ADNI, adding biomarkers and imaging to clinical history improves prognosis balanced accuracy from 0.595 to 0.652 (App. L). Removing PrimeKG leaves mortality AUROC unchanged for both consumers, within run-to-run noise (App. I), consistent with §5.1: the knowledge graph contributes when a question requires the patient–relation correspondence. Conversely, when a question concerns a relation directly, complete PrimeKG edges improve balanced accuracy on 1,920 pharmacological questions in all eight relation families, by +0 .063 to +0 .420 , whereas entity names, length-matched incorrect neighborhoods, and corrupted names do not (App. Table 7); because the model follows behaviorally corroborated and contradicted edges alike (App. G), traceability to an edge does not establish clinical correctness. Each source is therefore informative on the task suited to it, and interaction (§5.1) arises when a question requires both. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced MM-KG, which joins multimodal patient evidence to a biomedical knowledge graph through explicit, provenance-preserving alignment edges. In two independently constructed cohorts, the two sources are individually uninformative on questions that require both but superadditive together, and replacing the aligned relations with mismatched ones returns performance to the no-knowledge level. Query-conditioned retrieval recovers these links with a fraction of the context of generic retrieval, and MM-KG outperforms external graph methods on the items that require consulting the patient. Knowledge graphs thus help clinical LLMs not as background context but as explicit links between a patient’s state and the relation a question requires.

B.1 MIMIC modality construction 

Each admission connects to diagnoses, prescriptions, procedures, time-bounded laboratory features, and chest-radiograph studies. Diagnoses and prescriptions are preserved as typed patient-side observations before their standardized concepts are aligned with PrimeKG. Laboratory measurements remain attached to their admission and observation window, including their values, units, timestamps, and source-table identifiers. Each chest radiograph is processed with BiomedCLIP (Zhang et al., 2023) to produce scores for named radiographic findings. Finding-specific thresholds are calibrated against CheXpert reference labels (Irvin et al., 2019). Findings that pass their thresholds become textual imaging-finding nodes linked to the source radiograph and study. The ontology aligner then maps these finding nodes to compatible phenotype concepts in PrimeKG. This image-to-finding-to-concept path allows image content to participate in graph retrieval without discarding the original radiograph or its provenance. Radiology reports are processed independently. Report-derived findings become separate observation nodes connected through reported-finding edges. Consequently, an image-derived finding and a report-derived finding remain distinguishable even when they align to the same biomedical concept. This separation supports channel ablations and allows retrieved evidence to be traced to either the image model or the clinical report. Entity-linking routes and coverage are described in App. C.

Coverage reaches 80.5% of disease diagnosis rows (64.2% of all rows; administrative Z/V/Y codes are excluded from the knowledge join). The ICD-to-CUI table must be keyed by (version, code) , not by code alone. Twenty-one code strings have different meanings in ICD-9 and ICD-10; for example, E8341 denotes a transport accident in one version and hypermagnesemia in the other. D Evaluation settings, models, and statistical procedures 

Outcome prediction. The primary MIMIC cohort contains 28,582 ICU stays from 18,396 patients, including 2,306 in-hospital deaths. We retain the first ICU stay per admission and restrict current-stay inputs to the first 48 hours. Longitudinal history includes only admissions preceding the current admission. The zero-shot LLM is evaluated on the full eligible cohort. The trained GraphSAGE consumer uses a fixed 20% patient-level test split containing 5,676 stays, with no patient shared between the training and test sets. The primary metric is AUROC. A separate longitudinal ADNI prognosis

experiment contains 619 participants and is evaluated across five seeds using balanced accuracy. The complete GraphSAGE architecture and training procedure are given in App. J. 

Biomedical fact-checking. We construct 1,920 balanced yes/no questions across eight biomedical relation families from a source cohort of 47,100 patients and 183,950 admissions. The experimental conditions compare questions without KG evidence against complete PrimeKG relation lists, entity-only controls, shuffled neighborhoods, and corrupted-name controls. The evaluation unit is one question, and the primary metric is balanced accuracy. App. F describes the retrieval-completeness experiments and reports results for all eight families. 

Patient-specific join questions. The MIMIC benchmark contains 3,200 balanced items, comprising 400 matched positive–negative pairs in each of four families. The factorial design compares a conventional patient record (A), added patient modalities (B), added PrimeKG relations (C), and both channels (D). The primary drug-controlled analysis uses 1,895 items whose candidate drug appears in both label classes. The ADNI paired contraindication benchmark contains 510 development items, 114 validation items from 61 participants, and a 70-item test set from 39 participants. The one-time test evaluation provides the primary ADNI cross-cohort result, while the validation set is retained for detailed controls and model-sensitivity analyses. Detailed benchmark construction and leak controls are provided in App. E, and the ADNI evaluation is described in App. L. 

Language models and scoring. The primary MIMIC experiments use Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with 4-bit GPTQ quantization, served locally with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023). The primary ADNI test set evaluation uses Qwen3.5-9B, and the same frozen ADNI validation set with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct was performed as a model-sensitivity analysis. Separate ADNI retrieval studies use local Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B-FP8 models and are reported separately from the ADNI validation set. For yes/no questions, we normalize the first-token probabilities over yes and no , pyes = P (yes )/[P (yes ) + P (no )] , and use pyes as the continuous score; balanced accuracy uses the fixed threshold pyes > 0.5.

Uncertainty and statistical comparisons. MIMIC confidence intervals and comparisons use 2,000 paired-item bootstrap samples. The primary ADNI confidence interval treats participants as clusters, while matched-pair and candidate-drug clustering provide sensitivity analyses. Factorial interactions use the difference-in-differences definition in §3.5. Positive interaction indicates that one information source becomes more useful when the other is available.

G Behavioral validation of PrimeKG and layer refinement 

For every drug–condition pair we compute the log odds ratio of prescription given the condition over 183,950 admissions. PrimeKG’s indication layer is strongly corroborated: the mean log odds ratio is +1 .13 , compared with +0 .08 for unlabeled pairs ( p< 0.001 ). By contrast, contraindication pairs are not avoided as a group ( +0 .12 ), and their mean is indistinguishable from that of unlabeled pairs. We split the cohort into disjoint patient halves and require avoidance to replicate across both halves. This procedure identifies 649 behaviorally corroborated contraindications and 1,817 relations that observed prescribing reliably contradicts, with replication rates of 97.6% and 96.3% against 50% chance. Inspection of the latter group identifies clear errors; for example, PrimeKG lists fondaparinux as contraindicated in heparin-induced thrombocytopenia even though it is a standard treatment. When we repeat the fact-checking audit on both subsets, the model follows corroborated and contradicted edges similarly ( +0 .104 versus +0 .130 ; not significantly different). We additionally intersect these behavioral partitions with the answer-bearing contraindication edges of the controlled join benchmark, retaining each selected positive item together with its same-admission matched negative. The behaviorally corroborated subset contains 152 items (76 matched pairs) and retains an AUROC interaction of +0 .145 

(95% CI [+0 .074 , +0 .224] , p < 0.001 ). The behaviorally contradicted subset contains 580 items (290 matched pairs) and gives a nearly identical interaction of +0 .150 (95% CI [+0 .111 , +0 .190] , p < 0.001 ). Together, these analyses show that the model can join patient evidence to a supplied relation regardless of whether observed prescribing behavior corroborates that relation; they do not establish the clinical correctness of the underlying KG edge.

For the zero-shot LLM, image-derived findings contribute approximately zero to mortality prediction in the full context (Table 5). A separate single-channel LLM comparison explains this result. I Run-to-run noise floor 

For patients lacking a chest film, the full-prompt and no-imaging conditions are identical, yet scores differ across serving runs because batch composition changes floating-point reduction order. Across 19,678–21,383 identical-prompt pairs, the AUROC gap between runs is +0 .0003 –+0 .0042 , and the median per-item score drift is 2.5 × 10 −3. We therefore treat ±0.004 AUROC as the noise floor; all interaction estimates exceed it by more than an order of magnitude. Within-run comparisons (all join-task conditions; all ladder conditions) are scored by a single engine instance and do not incur this term. 

J GNN experiments 

The trained consumer in Table 5 is a two-layer heterogeneous GraphSAGE network. It uses HeteroConv 

with a per-edge-type SAGEConv , sum aggregation, hidden size 32, and dropout 0.5. Frozen pretrained features are projected to the hidden size: SapBERT name embeddings for patient-side concept nodes, PubMedBERT description embeddings for PrimeKG nodes, Bio_ClinicalBERT embeddings for matched radiology reports, and BiomedCLIP features for radiograph studies. The admission’s demographics and first-48-hour laboratory features are concatenated with the pooled graph representation before a two-layer prediction head. Training uses Adam with learning rate 3 × 10 −3, weight decay 10 −3, and batch size 512 for at most 100 epochs. We use validation early stopping with patience 12. The test set is a fixed 20% patient-level stratified split, so no patient appears in both training and test sets. We ensemble predictions from five training seeds. Channel ablations remove the corresponding node and edge types and their features. On 30-day readmission, the GraphSAGE model reaches AUROC 0.644 against 0.548 for tabular logistic regression. On ICU mortality, the full model reaches AUROC 0.804 against 0.778 for the laboratory-only comparator; on the imaging-present subset, it reaches 0.854 against 0.819. In the paired ICU ablations, laboratory values have the largest contribution ( +0 .085 ), followed by imaging ( +0 .008 ) and prior-visit history ( +0 .005 ); all three changes are significant. Radiology reports, medications, and PrimeKG do not produce measurable gains. In particular, dropping PrimeKG changes AUROC by only −0.002 (p=0 .16 ), matching the null result from the LLM consumer.

M ADNI retrieval-regime stress test 

Design. The hop ladder contains five balanced families with 100 items each and manipulates only shortest-path length. The three one-hop families are the individual legs of the longer drug–protein– disease–disease chain. The closed-book condition supplies no graph. Static context serializes a relation-guided packet, while the query loop lets the model issue one neighbors , find , or answer action per round. Hop exclusivity is checked during item construction, and positives and negatives are matched on evidence length. The experiment uses full PrimeKG because the cohort-specific phenotype-anchored graph is materialized only one hop from cohort seeds. It therefore evaluates graph access rather than the sufficiency of the ADNI cohort-specific graph.

The regimes fail differently. Closed-book performance falls to chance at two and three hops, showing that the model does not reliably supply the missing relations from its parameters. Static context falls below closed book in every family even though the gold path is usually present. At two hops, it answers yes on 82% of items and has specificity 0.080. Complete retrieval is therefore necessary but not sufficient, and the two graph-enabled regimes fail in opposite directions. 

Qualitative traces. On a one-hop enzyme question, the larger Qwen3 model independently queries the enzyme neighbors of celecoxib and warfarin, intersects the two lists, and correctly identifies CYP2C8, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4. On a whole-regimen question, the smaller model repeats the same truncated disease-neighborhood query for all 15 rounds, never queries from the drug side, and returns the wrong answer. A paired two-hop example shows a complementary static-context failure. The packet contains the positive path from lutein through ALB to gastric neoplasm, but the model also answers yes to a negative naproxen–plasma-cell-myeloma item with no such path. These examples show why a retrieved answer should be checked against an explicit path rather than treated as grounded simply because graph text was present. 

Result boundaries. The hop ladder is a knowledge-access stress test, not a clinical outcome task, and it uses full PrimeKG rather than the materialized ADNI graph. The reported language-model runs also require an archived repeat-run noise estimate. In addition, the 32-token constrained-decoding configuration produced whitespace-only truncations before the answer field in some earlier studies. These issues must be resolved before interpreting small differences between retrieval policies.

P.2 Condition A–D block definitions 

Each item stores seven text blocks. The four conditions differ only in which blocks are included; the question and every included block are identical across conditions. For exact reproduction, the frozen prompts retain the legacy “first 24 hours” laboratory header, although all laboratory values were computed from the prespecified first-48-hour feature window; this header was identical across experimental conditions. The labs , report , and imaging blocks are omitted when empty (no chest film in the window: 380 items lack a report block and 1,458 an imaging block), whereas an empty codes block is rendered as the fixed sentence above. Because a positive and its matched negative share the admission, patient-side block presence is identical within every pair. The kg block is never empty: every negative has a non-empty packet (the presence-leak control of App. E). 

P.3 Knowledge-graph packet serialization 

The main experiments use the prose-list serialization. A packet is a header sentence naming the candidate drug (its PrimeKG name) and the relation family, followed by the complete list of related PrimeKG concept names, one per line, sorted alphabetically: 

> Facts from a curated biomedical knowledge graph. {Drug} {relation phrase} (complete list, {k} entries): {concept 1} {concept 2} ...

with relation phrases “is contraindicated in the following conditions” (contra-labs, contra-report), “has the following documented adverse effects” (drug-effect), and “acts on proteins implicated in the following conditions” (mechanism). For mechanism, the retriever applies the two-hop drug →protein →disease template of §3; the packet lists the disease endpoints of that path and does not name the intermediate protein, so the model joins the endpoint to the patient’s finding. The typed-triple rendering compared in Table 8 carries the same entries as 

> Knowledge-graph retrieval results (complete; {k} triples): (:Drug {name: "{Drug}"})-[:CONTRAINDICATED_IN]->(:Condition {name: "{concept 1}"}) ...

with relation names CONTRAINDICATED_IN , HAS_ADVERSE_EFFECT , and TARGETS_PROTEIN_IMPLICATED_ IN , exactly as written by the v2 preparation step into the frozen item file.

P.6 Five-candidate ranking prompts 

The ranking benchmark of §5.2 reuses the patient-side blocks of App. P.2 unchanged (same renderer and caps, including the 40-name medication cap; test items list 13–40 medications, median 37). For the per-candidate scoring, the record is followed by a candidate line and one question per candidate: 

> Candidate drugs received by this patient: 1. {drug 1}; 2. {drug 2}; ...; 5. {drug 5}. Question: Does this patient have a documented condition in which {drug i} is contraindicated, or a documented finding that is a listed adverse effect of {drug i}? Answer yes or no.

Condition D in the R4q formulation includes only the queried candidate’s contraindication and adverse-effect packets (both relation families, same prose serialization as App. P.3); the earlier five-packet rendering included all five candidates’ packets in every question. The five candidates are ranked by their yes-probabilities.
