Abstract 

Gene set analysis is a cornerstone of functional genomics, yet it remains labor-intensive and heavily dependent on manual curation and ex-pert biological interpretation. While Large Lan-guage Models (LLMs) have emerged as power-ful tools for genomic reasoning and annotation, most existing approaches rely on symbolic gene names and fail to capture domain-specific bio-logical structure, particularly protein sequence information that governs molecular activity, in-teractions, and downstream gene function. In this work, we propose SoftGene , a novel frame-work for LLM-based gene set annotation that leverages the hierarchical structure of gene sets. First, we use a hierarchical attention-based en-coder built on ESM, a protein language model, to represent each gene set using protein-level amino acid sequence information. Second, we construct a hybrid prompting scheme that com-bines soft prompts derived from gene set em-beddings with hard prompts containing auxil-iary context generated by an LLM, and feed the resulting prompt into a local LLM for annota-tion. We evaluate our framework on two bench-mark datasets: Gene Ontology (GO) and the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Our results show that integrating protein-sequence representations with textual context improves gene set annotation overall, while per-domain analyses reveal that the contribution of protein embeddings varies across biological domains. 

1 Introduction 

Gene sets represent cohorts of functionally related genes that collectively drive specific biological pathways and cellular processes (Ashburner et al., 2000). Generating gene set annotations, which are concise descriptions that delineate shared func-tional behaviors and metabolic roles, is a fundamen-tal task in modern genomics (Ji et al., 2026). As sequencing continues to reveal novel gene associa-tions, the ability to rapidly and accurately annotate

these sets is essential for translating raw data into biological insights (Salzberg, 2019). Traditionally, gene set annotation has relied on expert curation or statistical enrichment meth-ods, such as Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) (Beissbarth and Speed, 2004) and Gene Set Enrich-ment Analysis (GSEA) (Subramanian et al., 2005). However, manual annotation is a time-consuming process that cannot scale with the current pace of data generation (Baumgartner Jr et al., 2007). Furthermore, existing automated methods are in-herently limited by their dependence on curated databases, which are often incomplete or biased toward well-studied genes and biological processes (Stoeger et al., 2018; Haynes et al., 2018). The recent emergence of Large Language Mod-els (LLMs) has introduced a promising paradigm for a wide range of biological and biomedical tasks (Luo et al., 2022; Jin et al., 2024; Ferruz et al., 2022) including automated gene annotation (Wang et al., 2024; Joachimiak et al., 2024). However, current LLM-based approaches often overlook the domain-specific biological structure of constituent proteins. Protein-sequence representations can pro-vide evolutionary and structural signals such as subcellular localization (Thumuluri et al., 2022), interaction-relevant interfaces (Sun et al., 2025), and structural properties (Simon and Zou, 2025) that may complement symbolic gene information (Anfinsen, 1973). The utility of these sequence-derived signals, however, can vary across biolog-ical domains. Moreover, hard-prompting strate-gies typically treat genes as semantic tokens, and LLMs remain prone to hallucination (Kalai et al., 2025), which reduces their reliability for this task. Lastly, although many LLM approaches provide narrative explanations through generative reason-ing (Hu et al., 2025), they still lack a concrete de-cision path showing how specific genes or protein domains contribute to the predicted annotation. 2 Methodology 

In this section, we formalize the gene set annotation task and introduce the associated notation. We then provide an overview of the proposed model architecture, followed by a detailed description of each component of the method. 

2.1 Problem Formulation 

A gene set is a collection of genes that are grouped together based on shared biological function, par-ticipation in a common pathway, or co-regulation. Gene sets are widely used in genomics to represent higher-level biological processes and functional modules. Let {G 1, . . . , GN } denote a collection of gene sets. Each gene set G consists of a variable number of member genes G = {g1, . . . , g M } where M is the length of the set. The ordering of genes within a set is assumed to be irrelevant. Each gene gi en-codes one or more proteins {pi, 1, . . . , p i,K i } where 

Ki represents the number of protein isoforms asso-ciated with gi; Ki may vary across genes, reflecting multiple isoforms or splice products. Each protein 

pi,j is represented as a sequence of amino acids 

pi,j = ( a1, a 2, . . . , a Li,j ), (1) where Li,j denotes the length of the protein and each aℓ ∈ A is an amino acid from the standard set of 20 residues, A = {A, C, . . . , Y}.For each gene set G, we are given a free-text annotation y = ( y1, . . . , y T ) describing the bio-logical function, pathway membership, or func-tional role. These annotations are concise, domain-specific phrases or sentences designed to capture the essential functional properties of the gene set without extensive narrative context. 

Task : Given a gene set G = {g1, . . . , g M },with each gene gi associated with proteins 

pi, 1, . . . , p i,K i represented as amino acid se-quences, the goal of the gene set annotation task is to predict the corresponding annotation sequence 

y = ( y1, y 2, . . . , y T ),

where yt ∈ V are tokens drawn from the vocabu-lary V of the pretrained language model. Formally, the task can be framed as conditional sequence generation; we seek a model fθ such that

where ˆy = (ˆ y1, ˆy2, . . . , ˆyT ) is the predicted annota-tion sequence. In other words, the model must learn a mapping from the hierarchical representation of the gene set, incorporating amino acid sequences for all constituent proteins, to a concise, technical description of the gene set’s biological function. 

2.2 Model Overview 

The model consists of two main components: 

• A Hierarchical Attention-Based Gene Set En-coder , which learns context-aware gene set rep-resentations by encoding proteins with a frozen pretrained protein language model (PLM) and hi-erarchically aggregating them into gene- and gene set–level embeddings using attention pooling. 

• The Hybrid Gene Set Soft Prompting module, wherein the resulting gene set embedding is pro-jected by a trainable multilayer perceptron (MLP) into soft prompt tokens that condition a frozen lan-guage model for free-text annotation generation. These soft prompts are combined with task instruc-tions and auxiliary contextual information from a large pretrained language model and fed to a local LLM to produce the final predicted annotation.

2.4 Hybrid Gene Set Soft Prompting 

As shown in Figure 4, this module generates free-text annotations from the gene set embedding pro-duced by the hierarchical encoder. To condition the local LLM (e.g., Llama) on the gene set embedding, we map it to the LLM input space using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP): 

S = MLP soft ( ˆG), (9) where S ∈ Rn×dLLM is a sequence of n tunable soft tokens in the embedding space of the LLM. These tokens serve as a learnable continuous prompt that encodes the structured biological information from the gene set. In addition to the soft prompt, we append two hard prompts to guide the LLM during generation. The first prompt, Hinst , contains the instruction for the model, specifying that it should generate a candidate annotation for the input gene set. The second prompt, Hcont , is generated by a commer-cial large pretrained language model 1 (in our case, GPT 4.1 Nano) and provides context and analy-sis of the genes in the set through semantic-level reasoning as follows: 

Hcont = LLM commercial ([ g1, g 2, . . . , g M ]) . (10) The prompts passed to LLM commercial are shown in Figure 3. This context supplies knowledge that the smaller local LLM may not encode in its pre-trained weights.

3.3 Evaluation Metrics 

Following previous works (Wang et al., 2024, 2025), we employ lexical and semantic similar-ity measures to evaluate our method. We use the following metrics for our evaluation: 

• METEOR (Lavie and Agarwal, 2007) measures the alignment between the candidate annotation and the reference by considering unigram matches while accounting for synonyms, stemming, and word order. We report METEOR to capture lexical alignment while accounting for synonyms, stem-ming, and word order. 

• ROUGE (Lin, 2004) identifies the longest com-mon subsequence between the generated and ref-erence texts. This ensures that essential biological keywords and descriptions from the ground truth are captured in the model’s output. 

• BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2019) addresses the limitations of exact-match metrics by using contex-tual embeddings to calculate similarity. By com-paring tokens based on their vector representations, BERTScore rewards candidate annotations that are synonymous with the ground truth, even when ex-act phrasing differs.

3.4.2 Ablation Study 

We conduct an ablation study in Table 3 to evaluate the contribution of each component in SoftGene. Removing the soft prompt and relying solely on hard prompts (“w/o Soft Prompt ( S)”) leads to a substantial performance drop across all metrics and both datasets, highlighting the importance of di-rectly conditioning the LLM on structured gene set representations. Excluding LLM-generated con-textual information (“w/o Context (H cont )”) results in a consistent degradation in performance while still outperforming baselines, indicating that high-level semantic context improves generation qual-ity. When replacing pretrained PLM embeddings with randomly initialized embeddings (‘w/o Pro-tein Embeddings’), overall performance decreases, indicating that sequence-derived representations provide complementary information beyond the downstream architecture. As shown by the per-domain analysis, however, the magnitude of this contribution varies substantially across biological domains. Notably, without pretrained protein em-beddings, SoftGene still outperforms all baselines, demonstrating that gains are not simply attributable to PLM embeddings. These results confirm that SoftGene’s gains arise from the combined effect of structured gene set embeddings, attention-based pooling, and soft prompt conditioning, with each contributing meaningfully to final performance. 3.4.3 Soft Prompt Length 

We analyze the effect of soft prompt length on model performance by varying the number of learned soft prompt tokens and evaluating outputs. Results for the GO and MSigDB datasets are shown in Figures 5 and 6. Across both datasets, perfor-mance varies only modestly across the evaluated prompt lengths. Since increasing prompt length yields no significant performance gains, we select 10 tokens as an empirical choice to reduce compu-tational cost while preserving information. 

3.4.4 Gene-Disjoint Generalization 

To further evaluate whether SoftGene learns bi-ologically meaningful sequence representations rather than memorizing gene co-occurrence pat-terns, we conduct additional experiments using a gene-disjoint train-test split. In this setting, train and test partitions are constructed such that genes appearing in the test set are excluded from the train-ing set as much as possible while maintaining an ap-proximate 70/30 split ratio. This provides a substan-tially more challenging evaluation setting by requir-ing the model to generalize to previously unseen genes rather than relying on overlap between train-ing and evaluation gene sets. Results are reported in Table 4. We observe that SoftGene trained under the gene-disjoint split performs comparably to the standard random split across all evaluation metrics on both the GO and MSigDB datasets. While mod-est decreases in performance are expected due to the stricter evaluation protocol, the overall degra-dation remains limited. These findings suggest that SoftGene is not solely memorizing recurring gene compositions, but instead learns transferable biological sequence patterns and functional rela-tionships that generalize to unseen genes.

3.4.6 Case Verification: Zinc Ion Transmembrane Transport 

We first examine the gene set GO:0071577 (zinc ion transmembrane transport), for which the model produces a near-perfect prediction. The high-est attention weights are assigned to genes from known zinc transporter families, including SLC39 (ZIP) and SLC30 (ZnT), which mediate zinc influx and efflux across cellular membranes (Jeong and Eide, 2013; Eide, 2006). The top-ranked genes, SLC39A5 and SLC39A4, are well-characterized zinc importers (Wang et al., 2004); SLC39A4 is mutated in acrodermatitis enteropathica (Wang et al., 2002), a classic inherited zinc deficiency disorder. Genes less directly related to zinc trans-port, such as the glutamate transporter SLC1A1 (Kanai and Hediger, 2003), receive lower atten-tion weights. This indicates that the gene-level attention layer ranks genes by functional relevance rather than treating all set members equally. 

3.4.7 Interpretability: Smooth Muscle Signaling 

We next analyze attention scores for GO:0045986 (negative regulation of smooth muscle contrac-tion), shown in Figure 7. SoftGene predicts the related term “negative regulation of cGMP biosynthetic process,” consistent with the nitric ox-ide–cGMP pathway that regulates vascular smooth muscle relaxation (Hofmann et al., 2006). At the gene level, the highest attention weights are as-signed to CALCA, encoding the vasodilator calci-tonin gene-related peptide (Brain and Grant, 2004), and the beta-adrenergic receptors ADRB2 and ADRB3, which promote smooth muscle relax-ation via cAMP signaling (Johnson, 2006). At the isoform level, the highest-weighted PRKG1 pro-tein is ENSP00000363092.5 (PRKG1 α). Unlike PRKG1 β, PRKG1 α localizes to smooth muscle stress fibers and mediates vascular relaxation in response to cGMP (Francis et al., 2010; Hofmann et al., 2006). This preference emerges without isoform-level supervision, suggesting that protein language model embeddings encode sequence sig-nals distinguishing functional splice variants.

LLM Soft Prompting: Advances in parameter-efficient fine-tuning have led to a family of methods that steer frozen models through learned prompts rather than updating model weights. Prefix-Tuning introduces continuous, learnable vectors prepended to Transformer layers, enabling parameter-efficient steering of frozen models (Li and Liang, 2021). Prompt Tuning shows that, for sufficiently large models, optimizing soft tokens at the input embed-ding layer can achieve performance comparable to full fine-tuning (Lester et al., 2021). P-Tuning models dependencies among soft prompts using a continuous prompt encoder, improving stability and allowing prompts to be conditioned on spe-cific inputs (Liu et al., 2024b). P-Tuning v2 ex-tends soft prompting to deeper Transformer layers to maintain effectiveness on smaller-scale models (Liu et al., 2022). Soft prompting has also been explored in biological domains, including multi-modal protein modeling in ProCyon (Queen et al., 2025), gene-focused dialogue systems in GeneChat (Dhanasekar et al., 2025), and genomic reasoning such as BioReason (Fallahpour et al., 2025). 

5 Conclusion 

In this work, we presented SoftGene, a hybrid prompting framework that integrates a hierarchi-cal attention-based encoder over protein sequences with soft prompt conditioning for language model– driven gene set annotation. Experimental re-sults on the Gene Ontology and MSigDB bench-marks show that SoftGene outperforms prompt-based, agent-based, and prompt-tuning baselines across the reported metrics. Additional analyses show that protein-sequence representations pro-vide complementary information, while their con-tribution varies substantially across biological do-mains. These findings support sequence-aware soft prompting as a promising approach for inter-pretable gene set annotation while highlighting the importance of domain-specific evaluation and bio-logical validation. 

6 Ethical Considerations 

SoftGene should not be used as a substitute for expert biological judgment or experimental vali-dation, particularly in high-stakes biomedical or clinical settings. We encourage users to treat gen-erated annotations as hypothesis-generating tools rather than definitive functional claims, and future work should explore mechanisms for uncertainty estimation, expert-in-the-loop evaluation, and bias-aware benchmarking in gene set interpretation. 

7 Limitations 

Dataset Coverage : SoftGene is trained and eval-uated on curated resources biased toward well-studied genes and biological processes, which may limit generalization to poorly annotated or emerging functions. Moreover, the contribution of protein-sequence embeddings varies across GO do-mains. Future work should evaluate on less curated gene sets and further characterize when sequence information provides the greatest benefit. 

Evaluation Scope : Although we supplement lexical metrics with biomedical semantic and ontology-aware measures, these evaluations remain reference-based and do not fully capture biological correctness or experimental validity. Future work should incorporate expert evaluation and down-stream biological validation to more rigorously as-sess generated annotations.

A Additional Experiments 

A.1 LLM Backbone for Context Generation 

We evaluate the impact of different commercial large language models for generating textual con-text describing each gene set in Figures 8 and 9. Specifically, we compare contexts produced by GPT, DeepSeek (Liu et al., 2024a), Claude, and Gemma (Team et al., 2025) while keeping all other components fixed. Contexts generated by GPT and DeepSeek result in the strongest overall performance across all performance metrics, with DeepSeek achieving the highest average scores. Claude yields slightly lower but comparable perfor-mance, while Gemma consistently underperforms the other models. 

A.2 Encoder Protein Language Model 

We evaluate the robustness of our approach to the choice of protein language model by training with embeddings generated from multiple protein en-coders, including three ESM-2 checkpoints of vary-ing capacity and ProtBERT (Elnaggar et al., 2022), a BERT model trained on amino acid sequence data. As shown in Figures 10 and 11, performance is highly consistent across models, with minimal vari-ation in performance metrics across random seeds. These results indicate that our method is not sen-sitive to the specific protein embedding backbone used, suggesting that improvements stem from how gene set representations are integrated into the lan-guage model rather than from any particular protein encoder. 

A.3 Performance by Description Length 

We analyze model performance as a function of an-notation length, stratified by the number of words in the reference description, for both the GO and MSigDB datasets in (A) of Figures 12 and 13. A.9 Additional Case Studies A.9.1 Regulation of Megakaryocyte Differentiation 

We examine the biological process gene set GO:0045652 (regulation of megakaryocyte differ-entiation), for which the model produces a near-perfect prediction. The highest gene-level attention weight is assigned to MEF2C, a transcription fac-tor known to be essential for megakaryocyte dif-ferentiation and platelet production (Gekas et al., 2009). RBM15, which directly regulates alterna-tive splicing of key megakaryopoiesis transcription factors including GATA1 and RUNX1, receives an intermediate attention weight, consistent with its established role in this process (Zhang et al., 2015). In contrast, LOX and EIF6, which are a matrix-remodeling enzyme and a general transla-tion factor, respectively, receive among the lowest attention weights, reflecting their more peripheral relevance to megakaryocyte differentiation specifi-cally. These results suggest that the hierarchical at-tention encoder successfully distinguishes between genes that are central to megakaryocyte differentia-tion and those with more general cellular functions within the same gene set. 

A.9.2 Hypoxia-Inducible Factor in the Cardiovascular System 

We examine the BIOCARTA_HIF_PATHWAY gene set, which represents hypoxia-inducible fac-tor (HIF) signaling. The model assigns its highest attention to HIF1A, VHL, and VEGFA. HIF1A and VHL form the central oxygen-sensing regu-latory mechanism, where VHL controls degrada-tion of HIF1A under normal oxygen conditions (Jaakkola et al., 2001). VEGFA is a key down-stream target that drives vascular growth in re-sponse to hypoxia (Semenza, 2012). In contrast, genes with more general cellular functions, such as HSP90AA1 and P4HB, receive lower attention weights, consistent with roles in protein folding and cellular maintenance rather than hypoxia-specific signaling. Overall, the model concentrates atten-tion on core pathway regulators and outputs, while assigning lower importance to broadly expressed housekeeping genes. 

A.9.3 Glyoxylate and Dicarboxylate Metabolism 

We examine the KEGG_GLYOXYLATE_ AND_DICARBOXYLATE_METABOLISM pathway, for which the model assigns elevated attention to several canonical metabolic enzymes. The highest attention weight is assigned to CS (citrate synthase), a core enzyme of central carbon metabolism that catalyzes entry into the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Zhang et al., 2025). Related enzymes including ACO1/ACO2 and MDH1/MDH2 also receive high attention weights, consistent with their established roles in in-terconnected dicarboxylate and mitochondrial metabolic processes (Omini et al., 2024). The model additionally assigns elevated attention to HAO1, HAO2, and GRHPR, which are more directly associated with glyoxylate metabolism and detoxification pathways (Wanders et al., 2025; Garrelfs et al., 2024). Together, these patterns suggest that the model upweights metabolically related enzymes within the pathway rather than assigning attention uniformly across all genes.

C.3.1 Scalability to Large Gene Sets 

A potential concern is the scalability of SoftGene to large gene sets, as the GO dataset contains gene sets with up to 13,616 member genes (Table 1). Our attention pooling operates in O(n) time with respect to gene set size, as we employ query-only attention rather than pairwise attention, avoiding the quadratic memory bottleneck common to stan-dard self-attention. We additionally leverage sparse scatter operations to perform efficient aggregation across variable-length gene sets in a single batched operation. To empirically validate this, we report inference runtime on the five largest gene sets in our evaluation set in Table 12, observing that even at this scale SoftGene remains computationally tractable in this setting. 

D Dataset Preparation 

In this section, we outline the steps taken to prepare the benchmark datasets. 

D.1 Protein Sequences and Isoforms 

We obtain protein sequences from the Ensembl proteome ( Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.pep.all.fa 3)(Martin et al., 2023). This resource also provides a mapping from gene symbols to their corresponding splice isoforms, giving us the hierarchical gene-to-protein information required for the gene set anno-tation task. The Ensembl proteome thus provides all necessary information to construct input repre-sentations for both the GO and MSigDB datasets, enabling the model to leverage gene- and protein-level features consistently across benchmarks. 

D.2 GO Dataset 

To construct the GO gene sets, we use two primary input files: the GO ontology file ( go.obo 4) and the human gene annotation file ( goa_human.gaf 5) ob-tained from the GO Consortium. The ontology file defines all GO terms and their hierarchical relation-ships, while the GAF file provides gene-to-term annotations. We load the ontology using GODag from the 

goatools library and read the GAF following the GAF v2.2 format. For each GO term, we collect all associated human gene symbols, storing the term’s name and namespace (biological process, molecu-lar function, or cellular component). Finally, gene sets are exported as a JSON object mapping each GO ID to its genes, description, and domain.

To construct the MSigDB gene sets from the C2:CP collection, we use two input sources: the GMT file (c2.all.v2025.1.Hs.symbols.gmt 6)and the accompanying SQLite database (msigdb_v2025.1.Hs.db 7). D.4 Dataset Contamination Analysis 

Data contamination is a common concern in NLP and LLM research, particularly when evaluation datasets are drawn from publicly available re-sources that may overlap with model pretraining corpora. We address two potential sources of con-tamination relevant to our evaluation. 

Memorization of GO and MSigDB annotations. 

We construct the datasets from late-2025 releases of GO and MSigDB. For GPT-4.1 nano, the docu-mented knowledge cutoff predates these releases, reducing the possibility that release-specific up-dates were included in its pretraining data. How-ever, many underlying genes, pathways, and anno-tations predate the selected releases and may have appeared in model pretraining corpora. Therefore, the use of recent database releases reduces but does not eliminate the possibility of contamination. 

Data leakage through auxiliary context. A sep-arate concern is whether Hcont encodes ground-truth label information, as commercial LLMs may have memorized annotations and pathway descrip-tions from biomedical literature. F Additional Related Works 

F.1 Protein Language Models 

Protein language models (PLMs) apply the self-supervised pretraining paradigm from NLP to amino acid sequences, learning representations of protein structure and function directly from evo-lutionary data. Rives et al. (2021) established the foundation for this line of work by training ESM-1b on 250 million protein sequences, demonstrat-ing that structural and functional properties emerge from sequence-level pretraining alone without ex-plicit structural supervision. Lin et al. (2023) scaled this approach to 15 billion parameters with ESM-2 and ESMFold, enabling direct inference of atomic-level protein structure from primary sequence. El-naggar et al. (2022) introduced ProtTrans, a family of transformer models pretrained on protein se-quences, demonstrating that NLP pretraining strate-gies transfer effectively to amino acid data; Prot-BERT serves as one of the encoder backbones in our ablation study. Meier et al. (2021) showed that PLMs support zero-shot prediction of muta-tional effects without any task-specific supervision, demonstrating that PLM representations encode functional information beyond structural topology and motivating our use of these embeddings to dis-tinguish functionally distinct isoforms. Rao et al. (2021) introduced the MSA Transformer, which in-terleaves row and column attention across multiple sequence alignments to incorporate co-evolutionary context, achieving substantially improved contact prediction over single-sequence models. Hayes et al. (2025) presented ESM3, a multimodal genera-tive PLM that jointly reasons over protein sequence, structure, and function, representing the frontier of PLM capability and pointing toward richer biologi-cal representations for gene annotation tasks. 

F.2 Traditional Gene Set Annotation Methods 

The analysis of gene sets has a long history in func-tional genomics, with early methods framing the problem as statistical over-representation testing. Beissbarth and Speed (2004) introduced one of the first automated approaches, identifying statis-tically overrepresented GO terms within a gene list by comparing observed annotation frequen-cies against a background distribution. While con-ceptually straightforward, such over-representation methods treat gene sets as unordered collections and discard magnitude and directionality informa-tion present in expression data. Subramanian et al. (2005) addressed this by operating on ranked gene lists derived from differential expression, using a weighted Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic to detect coordinated shifts in gene set activity. However, GSEA produces enrichment scores at the cohort level, limiting applicability to single-sample set-tings. Barbie et al. (2009) extended this to individ-ual samples by computing per-sample enrichment scores, while Hänzelmann et al. (2013) further gen-eralized the approach as a non-parametric kernel es-timation problem applicable across microarray and RNA-Seq platforms. A separate line of work rec-ognized that gene membership alone is insufficient to capture signaling complexity. Tarca et al. (2009) incorporated pathway topology by weighting genes according to their connectivity within curated sig-naling graphs, though such methods remain de-pendent on the completeness of curated databases (Liberzon et al., 2015). Collectively, these methods share a fundamental limitation: they map gene sets to predefined categorical labels rather than generat-ing descriptive natural language annotations, and the methods discussed above do not directly incor-porate protein-sequence representations.
